Does Cracking Your Knuckles Cause Arthritis? The Doctor Who Tested It on One Hand

Generations of children have been told to stop cracking their knuckles or they will get arthritis. One doctor spent decades cracking only one hand to find out. What his experiment, and the larger studies after it, actually show.

5 min read·1 December 2026

“Stop doing that, your bones will get weak.” “You’ll get arthritis when you’re old.” If you crack your knuckles, you have probably heard some version of this from a parent, grandparent or teacher. In many Indian homes, the habit is also scolded as inauspicious, something that simply shouldn’t be done.

The warning is so common that most of us assume there must be something to it. One doctor decided to find out the hard way.

The one-hand experiment

Donald Unger, a doctor in California, was warned as a child by his mother and aunts that cracking his knuckles would cause arthritis. He decided to test it on himself. He cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice a day, and left the knuckles of his right hand alone, so that it could serve as a comparison.

In 1998, he reported the results in a short letter to the journal Arthritis & Rheumatism. By then he had kept the habit up for about 50 years, cracking his left knuckles an estimated 36,500 times or more. Neither hand had arthritis, and there was no apparent difference between them. He concluded there was no clear link between knuckle cracking and arthritis in the fingers, and added, with a straight face, that the result called into question other parental beliefs, such as the importance of eating spinach.

In 2009, by which point his experiment had run for more than 60 years, Unger was awarded the Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine. The Ig Nobels honour research that “makes people laugh, then think”. In his acceptance speech, he joked that his tombstone should read: “Here lies Donald Unger, who has finally quit cracking his knuckles.”

His experiment was clever, but it was also a study of one person, who was not a neutral observer. It cannot settle the question on its own. Fortunately, others have looked at many more hands.

What larger studies found

In 1975, a small survey of older people by Robert and Stuart Swezey found no link between a history of knuckle cracking and wear-and-tear changes in the knuckle joints.

In 1990, the doctors J. Castellanos and D. Axelrod examined 300 patients aged 45 and older, 74 of whom were habitual knuckle crackers. Crackers were no more likely to have arthritis in their hands. But they were more likely to have hand swelling and had a weaker grip. The study could not show that cracking caused these differences; crackers were also more likely to do manual labour, bite their nails, smoke and drink alcohol, any of which might play a part.

A larger study came in 2011, when Kevin deWeber and colleagues compared 215 people aged 50 to 89 whose hand X-rays had been examined for osteoarthritis. About 20% were regular knuckle crackers. Crackers were no more likely to have osteoarthritis, in any joint, than non-crackers. Even people who had cracked their knuckles for decades showed no higher risk. What did predict hand arthritis was age and a family history of the condition.

In 2017, a team led by the radiologist Robert Boutin examined 400 knuckle joints in 40 healthy adults, 30 of whom were habitual crackers, using ultrasound and physical tests. They found no immediate pain, swelling or loss of function after cracking. If anything, joints that cracked showed a slightly greater range of movement afterwards.

Across several studies, knuckle cracking has not been linked to arthritis. Whether it has any subtler long-term effect on the hands is less clear.

So what is the crack?

The sound is not bones grinding or breaking. Your knuckles are surrounded by a capsule filled with a lubricating liquid called synovial fluid. When you pull or bend a finger in a particular way, the joint surfaces separate a little, pressure inside the capsule drops, and gas comes out of the fluid to form a bubble, much like opening a bottle of soda.

Exactly how this makes a sound has been argued about for decades. In 1971, researchers proposed that the crack comes from a bubble collapsing. In 2015, Gregory Kawchuk and colleagues filmed knuckles cracking inside an MRI scanner and saw a cavity form at the moment of the crack, which remained visible afterwards. They argued that the sound comes from the bubble forming, not collapsing. In 2018, a mathematical model by Vineeth Chandran Suja and Abdul Barakat suggested both sides could be partly right: a partial collapse of the bubble could produce the sound, leaving some gas behind.

This also explains why you cannot crack the same knuckle again straight away. The gas takes time to dissolve back into the fluid, often estimated at around 20 to 30 minutes.

Why the myth lasts

Several things help the warning survive:

  • It sounds harmful. A loud pop from a joint feels as if something must be getting damaged.
  • Arthritis is common in older age. Many people who cracked their knuckles will develop arthritis anyway, simply because they grow older, and it is easy to blame the habit. This is the classic mistake of treating a coincidence as a cause.
  • It is useful for parents. The sound annoys many people. A health warning is a more persuasive way to stop a child than “it irritates me”.

The broader lesson is a familiar one. A belief can be passed down for generations, feel completely obvious and still not hold up when someone finally tests it, as with many other household rules (see Don’t Sweep After Sunset). It is also a reminder that one dramatic experiment, however entertaining, matters most when larger studies point the same way (see Survivorship Bias, on why vivid single cases can mislead).

When to pay attention

Ordinary, painless knuckle cracking appears to be harmless for most people. But joint noises are worth checking if they come with pain, swelling, stiffness or reduced movement, or if they began after an injury. Arthritis organisations advise seeing a doctor if your hands or fingers have unexplained swelling or stiffness that does not go away within a few days, or if your joints become painful. People who already have a joint condition may want to ask their doctor before making a habit of it.

So your elders were not entirely wrong to tell you to stop. They may just have had the wrong reason.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Studies of habitual knuckle crackers have not found a higher rate of arthritis in the hands.
  • The cracking sound is linked to a gas bubble forming in the joint fluid, not to bones rubbing or breaking.
  • A knuckle usually cannot be cracked again for a while after cracking.
  • Age and family history are much stronger predictors of hand osteoarthritis.

Still uncertain

  • Exactly what produces the sound: bubble formation, partial collapse or both.
  • Whether habitual cracking has subtler effects, such as on grip strength or swelling; the evidence comes from one older study.
  • Whether very frequent, forceful cracking over a lifetime affects the joint in ways current studies would miss.
  • How the results apply to people who already have joint disease.
