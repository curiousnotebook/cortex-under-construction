Who Said So?

“Breakfast Is the Most Important Meal of the Day.” Who Said So?

We hear it from parents, teachers and cereal boxes. The line began in a magazine edited by a cereal pioneer and spread through advertising. What happens when scientists actually test it?

10 min read·27 December 2026

Few pieces of health advice are repeated as often, or with as much confidence, as this one. Many of us heard it as children while being made to finish a glass of milk before school. It appears on cereal boxes, in school posters and in countless magazine articles. Skipping breakfast is treated almost as a small moral failing.

But where did the saying come from, and is it true? The answer turns out to involve a health sanitarium in Michigan, a public relations campaign for bacon, decades of studies that measured the wrong thing, and a more careful set of trials that gives a more interesting answer than either “yes” or “no”.

Who first said it?

The earliest well-documented use of the phrase in roughly its modern form comes from 1917. That year Lenna Frances Cooper, a dietitian who also co-founded what became the American Dietetic Association, wrote in a magazine called Good Health that breakfast was in many ways the most important meal, because it was “the meal that gets the day started”.

The detail that makes historians smile is who ran the magazine. Good Health was published by the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan, a famous health resort run by Cooper’s mentor, Dr John Harvey Kellogg. Kellogg co-invented corn flakes, and his brother, Will Keith Kellogg, built the cereal company that still carries the family name.

That does not mean Cooper was simply writing an advertisement. Kellogg was a genuine, if eccentric, health campaigner who believed plain grain foods were better for body and character than the heavy, meat-rich breakfasts many Americans ate at the time. But the people most loudly promoting breakfast were also the people inventing new things to eat at it.

Breakfast was not always a given

For much of history, a hearty morning meal was not considered essential, or even normal. According to historians quoted by Smithsonian magazine, the Romans generally believed one main meal a day was healthiest. In medieval England, most people ate their first proper meal in the late morning. Breakfast was mainly for those who needed it: the sick, the elderly, travellers and labourers starting early in the fields.

Breakfast became a regular daily meal for most people as working life changed. When work began at fixed hours, often early in the morning and far from home, eating before leaving made practical sense. By the nineteenth century, middle-class American breakfasts could be large and heavy, and complaints about indigestion were common. That was the world in which Kellogg and his rival C. W. Post began selling lighter, ready-to-eat cereals as a healthier alternative.

Selling the morning meal

The cereal makers were not the only ones with something to gain. In the early 1920s, the meat company Beech-Nut hired Edward Bernays, often called the father of modern public relations, to help sell more bacon. Bernays arranged for doctors to be surveyed about whether a light or a hearty breakfast was better for health. A 1922 newspaper report described physicians from across the United States, with 73% favouring a hearty breakfast. The findings were publicised widely, and bacon and eggs became fixed as the classic American breakfast. In his memoir, Bernays later wrote openly that the company’s research showed bacon sales went up when people ate heavy breakfasts.

Cereal companies kept the message going. Popular histories of breakfast point to a 1944 campaign by General Foods, makers of Grape-Nuts, under the slogan “Eat a good breakfast, do a better job”, which reportedly included radio adverts telling listeners that nutrition experts considered breakfast the most important meal of the day. According to the same accounts, wartime government nutritionists and cereal makers were recommending much the same thing: a good breakfast of whole-grain cereal and fruit.

The famous line did not come from a scientific discovery. It came from a dietitian working within Kellogg’s health empire and was spread by companies selling breakfast food. That does not make it false, but it means it was a belief long before anyone tested it properly.

What the early studies seemed to show

For decades, research appeared to back the slogan. Large surveys repeatedly found that people who skipped breakfast tended to weigh more. Later studies that followed thousands of people over many years linked skipping breakfast with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes. A 2019 review of six such studies found that the risk rose with each extra day a week people skipped breakfast, levelling off at around four to five days.

The most striking result came the same year. A study of about 6,500 American adults aged 40 to 75, surveyed in the late 1980s and early 1990s and followed until 2011, found that those who said they never ate breakfast were 87% more likely to die of cardiovascular disease, such as heart attacks and strokes, than those who ate it every day, even after adjusting for many other factors.

But look closely at who the breakfast-skippers were. In that same study, people who never ate breakfast were more likely to have smoked, to drink heavily, to be physically inactive, to have lower incomes and to eat a poorer-quality diet overall. Researchers can try to adjust for these differences statistically, but they can never be sure they have captured everything. Skipping breakfast may simply be one sign of a busier, more stressful or less healthy life, rather than a cause of ill health in itself. This is the classic problem of confounding, the same trap that can make data mislead in ways explored in our piece on Simpson’s paradox.

Belief beyond the evidence

In 2013, the researchers Andrew Brown, Michelle Bohan Brown and David Allison looked at 92 studies on breakfast and obesity. They found that the research on this question kept repeating the same kind of observational study, which added little new knowledge, and that many papers described their results in ways that overstated the case for breakfast. They titled their paper “Belief beyond the evidence”.

The way to test cause and effect is a randomised controlled trial, where people are assigned by chance to eat breakfast or skip it. When researchers finally ran such trials, the picture changed.

  • The US weight-loss trial (2014). Emily Dhurandhar and colleagues assigned 309 adults who wanted to lose weight to eat breakfast, skip it, or follow general advice that did not mention breakfast. After 16 weeks, all three groups had lost about the same amount of weight.
  • The Bath Breakfast Project (2014). In the UK, James Betts and colleagues assigned lean adults either to eat at least 700 calories by 11 a.m. or to eat nothing until noon, for six weeks. Skipping breakfast did not slow metabolism, and the skippers did not fully make up the missed calories later. Breakfast eaters, however, were more physically active, mostly through light everyday movement. Blood sugar control later in the day was somewhat less steady among the skippers.
  • The BMJ review (2019). Katherine Sievert and colleagues in Australia combined 13 randomised trials. People assigned to eat breakfast took in about 260 more calories a day on average and were slightly heavier, by about 0.44 kg, than those assigned to skip it. The authors warned that the trials were mostly short and of low quality, but concluded that the evidence did not support telling adults to eat breakfast to lose weight.

For adults, the best current evidence suggests breakfast is neither a magic key to a healthy weight nor a disaster to skip. When people are randomly assigned to eat or skip it, the differences in weight are small.

Children may be a different story

Most of the strongest trials were done in adults. Children’s bodies and brains are still developing, and the evidence for them looks somewhat different.

A 2016 systematic review by Katie Adolphus and colleagues at the University of Leeds examined 45 studies on breakfast and children’s thinking skills. They found that, compared with going without, eating breakfast gave a short-term boost on the same morning to attention, memory and “executive” skills such as planning and self-control, and the effects were clearer in undernourished children. They could not draw firm conclusions about long-term school breakfast programmes, because there were too few good studies.

This matters in India, where many children arrive at school without having eaten. India’s National Education Policy 2020 recommended adding breakfast to school meals, arguing that the morning hours after a nutritious breakfast are useful for more demanding subjects. A national rollout has not followed; the Finance Ministry declined the proposal in 2021–22, and as of late 2025 several states were still pressing for breakfast to be added to the PM POSHAN scheme.

Tamil Nadu went ahead on its own, launching the Chief Minister’s Breakfast Scheme in government schools in September 2022, and later extending it to about 24 lakh students. An interim report by the state’s Planning Commission, presented in December 2024 and covering 5,410 children in 100 schools, reported better punctuality, attention and memory for lessons. These results are encouraging, but they come from the government running the scheme and the published summaries do not describe a comparison with similar children who did not receive breakfast, so on their own they cannot show how much of the change was caused by the meal itself.

So who should eat breakfast?

The honest answer is: it depends on the person, and on the breakfast.

  • What you eat matters more than when. A bowl of sugary cereal and a plate of idli and sambar, a bowl of poha with vegetables, or eggs and fruit are all “breakfast”, but they are not the same meal. Most of the trials say little about the quality of the food.
  • Children, especially those who are underfed, appear to benefit most, at least in the short term, in attention and memory at school.
  • Some people need regular meals for medical reasons. For example, people with diabetes who take insulin or certain tablets that lower blood sugar can be at risk of dangerously low blood sugar if they skip meals. Anyone in this situation should talk to their doctor before changing their eating pattern.
  • For many healthy adults, it is a matter of preference. If breakfast keeps you from grabbing unhealthy snacks later, it may help you. If you are genuinely not hungry in the morning, the evidence does not suggest you are harming yourself by waiting until later.

So who said so? A dietitian working within Kellogg’s health empire, a publicist hired to sell bacon and a cereal company’s wartime campaign helped turn a habit into a rule. Science took much longer to catch up, and when it did, it found a quieter truth: breakfast can be a good meal, but for most adults it is not the most important one.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The phrase was popularised in the early twentieth century, including in a 1917 article in a magazine published by Kellogg’s sanitarium, and was later spread by food marketing.
  • In randomised trials in adults, eating breakfast has not been shown to help weight loss; if anything, it adds a little to daily calories.
  • Skipping breakfast does not appear to slow down resting metabolism.
  • In children, breakfast seems to give a short-term boost to attention and memory, especially for those who are undernourished.

Still uncertain

  • Whether the links between skipping breakfast and diabetes or heart disease are caused by breakfast itself or by other differences between people.
  • The long-term effects of eating or skipping breakfast, since most trials last weeks, not years.
  • How much school breakfast programmes improve learning over months and years.
  • How the type and quality of breakfast, rather than just eating it, change the results.
