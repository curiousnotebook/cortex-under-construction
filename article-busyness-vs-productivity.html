How to Stop Confusing Busyness With Productivity

A day packed with messages, meetings and small tasks can feel like hard work and still leave nothing important done. Why our minds mistake motion for progress, and how to tell the two apart.

11 min read·25 December 2026

You answered forty messages before lunch. You sat through three meetings, replied to a group chat, sorted out a small problem for a colleague and rearranged your to-do list twice. By evening you are tired in the way that feels earned. Then someone asks a simple question: what did you actually get done today? And the honest answer is that the one task that mattered is still sitting untouched.

Busyness and productivity look alike from the inside, but they measure different things. Busyness is about how full your time is. Productivity is about what changes because of how you spent it: a chapter understood, a report finished, a problem solved, a skill improved. You can be extremely busy and barely productive, and, less obviously, you can be very productive on a day that looks quite empty.

Research from psychology, economics and workplace studies helps explain why we confuse the two so easily. The confusion is not a personal failing. Our minds, our workplaces and even our ideas about status all push us towards it.

Our minds prefer motion to stillness

In a 2010 study, the psychologists Christopher Hsee, Adelle Yang and Liangyan Wang gave university students a survey to fill in, followed by a 15-minute wait before the next one. Students could hand in the first survey at a desk nearby and sit idle, or at a desk about a 12 to 15-minute walk away and back, which would fill the wait. A small sweet was given at either location.

When the same sweet was on offer at both desks, only about a third chose the walk. When the far desk offered a different sweet, giving people an excuse to go, around six in ten walked. Yet the students who stayed busy reported feeling happier during those 15 minutes, whichever sweet they got. The authors called this “idleness aversion”: people dislike doing nothing, but they want a reason, however flimsy, to be busy.

A later study took this further. In 2014 Timothy Wilson and colleagues asked people to sit alone in a plain room for between 6 and 15 minutes with nothing to do but think. Most found it hard and not very enjoyable. In one version, participants were allowed to give themselves a mild electric shock, one they had earlier said they would pay to avoid. During 15 minutes alone, 67% of men and 25% of women pressed the button at least once.

Our minds tend to prefer activity, even pointless or unpleasant activity, to sitting still. That makes “busy” a comfortable default, but it is not evidence that anything useful is happening.

Busy has become a badge

Busyness is not only comfortable. It also signals something to other people. In a series of studies published in 2017, Silvia Bellezza, Neeru Paharia and Anat Keinan showed American participants descriptions of people who were either very busy or had plenty of leisure. In one study, a friend who wrote about being overworked was rated more than twice as high on a combined measure of wealth and status as one who described a relaxed life. Even small details mattered: a woman wearing a Bluetooth headset, suggesting she was always on call, was seen as higher in status than one wearing ordinary headphones.

Interestingly, the effect was not universal. In a comparison with Italian participants, working long hours was linked with lower status. The authors linked the difference to how strongly a culture believes that hard work is the route to getting ahead.

A related bias makes visible effort look like quality. In a 2004 study on what its authors called the “effort heuristic”, Justin Kruger and colleagues showed people the same poem, painting or piece of medieval armour, but told some that it had taken many hours to make and others that it had taken far fewer. The work said to have taken longer was rated as better and worth more, especially when its quality was hard to judge.

Workplaces can reward the same illusion. The sociologist Erin Reid studied a global strategy consulting firm whose culture expected near-total availability, often 80-hour weeks. Interviewing 82 consultants and reviewing their evaluations, she found that some men quietly worked much less, roughly 50 to 60 hours, while appearing to meet the ideal. They received the same strong reviews and promotions as colleagues who truly worked the longest hours. People who openly asked for reduced hours, mostly women, were the ones who were penalised. In other words, the firm was rewarding the appearance of availability rather than measuring output.

The urgency trap

In 1954, speaking at Northwestern University near Chicago, the US president Dwight Eisenhower quoted an unnamed former college president who said he had two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important: “The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent.” The line later inspired the well-known “Eisenhower matrix” for sorting tasks.

Modern experiments suggest the quip captures something real. In five experiments published in 2018, Meng Zhu, Yang Yang and Christopher Hsee gave people a choice between a task that seemed urgent because it would “expire” soon and one with no deadline but a bigger reward. Many chose the urgent task even when it paid less, and even when there was plenty of time for both. They called this the “mere urgency effect”. It was stronger in people who described themselves as generally busy, and it shrank when people were reminded of the payoffs at the moment they chose.

Modern work is full of small urgencies. Microsoft’s 2025 analysis of anonymised data from users of its workplace software estimated that the average employee was interrupted by a meeting, email or chat message roughly every two minutes, about 275 times a day, and received over a hundred emails and a similar number of chat messages daily. Nearly half of the employees it surveyed said their work felt chaotic and fragmented. Each message feels like it needs a response now. Together they can fill an entire day.

The hidden cost of switching

Jumping between tasks feels efficient, but it has a cost. In laboratory experiments by Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans, people lost time every time they switched from one kind of task to another, and the loss was larger when tasks were more complex or less familiar. Each switch may cost only a fraction of a second in the lab, but in real work, where switches involve remembering what you were doing and why, the delays add up.

Switching also leaves something behind. The management researcher Sophie Leroy found that when people move from an unfinished task to a new one, part of their attention stays stuck on the first. She called this “attention residue”, and it made people perform worse on the next task.

The psychologist Gloria Mark has spent two decades measuring how office workers actually use their attention, using computer logging and observation. In her early studies, around 2004, people stayed on one screen for about two and a half minutes on average before switching. By 2012 that had fallen to about 75 seconds, and in more recent studies it was around 47 seconds. Her team also found that when people are pulled away from a piece of work, it takes on average about 25 minutes before they return to it, often after visiting other tasks along the way.

There is encouraging evidence that small limits help. In a two-week experiment by Kostadin Kushlev and Elizabeth Dunn, 124 adults spent one week checking email only three times a day and another week checking as often as they liked. They reported less daily stress during the limited week, though many found the rule hard to stick to.

A day broken into hundreds of fragments can feel intensely busy while leaving little room for the kind of sustained attention that produces real results.

More hours do not always mean more output

In 1955, the historian C. Northcote Parkinson wrote a satirical essay in The Economist that opened with an observation now known as Parkinson’s law: work expands to fill the time available for its completion. He pointed, tongue in cheek, to the British Admiralty, whose office staff kept growing while the number of ships in the navy shrank.

Harder data come from an unusual source. The Stanford economist John Pencavel analysed records from British munitions factories during the First World War, collected by government investigators studying worker fatigue. Up to roughly 49 hours a week, output rose in step with hours. Beyond that, each extra hour added less and less. Output at 70 hours a week was barely different from output at 56 hours. Weeks without a rest day were particularly damaging to output.

The question of hours is a live one in India. In October 2023, the Infosys co-founder N. R. Narayana Murthy said young Indians should be willing to work 70 hours a week to help the country compete. In January 2025, a video of the Larsen & Toubro chairman S. N. Subrahmanyan suggesting 90-hour weeks, including Sundays, set off a wide public debate. Supporters argue that fast-growing economies have been built on hard work and personal sacrifice. Critics point to health and to research like Pencavel’s.

The health side of the evidence is fairly clear. A 2021 joint analysis by the World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization estimated that working 55 or more hours a week was linked to about 745,000 deaths from stroke and heart disease worldwide in 2016. Compared with a 35 to 40-hour week, it was associated with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease. India’s own Economic Survey for 2024–25 cited this kind of evidence when it noted that working beyond about 55 to 60 hours a week can harm health.

None of this means effort does not matter. At ordinary levels, more time spent working does produce more, and there are short periods, such as an exam season or a deadline, when extra hours make sense. The point is narrower: beyond a certain level, extra hours mostly buy tiredness and mistakes, and the hours themselves stop being a good measure of how much is being achieved.

How to tell the difference

None of the research gives a perfect formula, but it points towards some practical habits.

  • Count outcomes, not activities. At the end of a day or week, ask what exists now that did not before, or what has moved forward. “Answered 60 emails” is an activity. “Finished the first draft” or “can now solve this type of problem” is an outcome.
  • Choose the important task before the urgent ones arrive. Decide the night before, or first thing in the morning, which one task would make the day worthwhile. Because the mere urgency effect weakened when people were reminded of what each task was worth, keeping that choice visible may help.
  • Protect blocks of single-task time. Set aside periods with notifications off and a single piece of work in front of you. Check messages at set times instead of constantly.
  • Close loops before you switch. When you must stop, take a moment to note where you are and what comes next. This may help reduce attention residue, because the unfinished task has somewhere to rest.
  • Notice “justifiable busyness”. When you feel restless, ask whether the task you reach for actually serves a goal, or simply fills the discomfort of having nothing to do.
  • Treat rest as part of the work. Pencavel’s finding about rest days is a reminder that recovery is not the opposite of productivity. Thinking time, sleep and a genuine day off are often where tiredness clears and ideas form.

For anyone who leads a team, the same logic applies. Microsoft’s 2022 survey of more than 20,000 workers found that 87% of employees said they were productive, while only 12% of leaders were fully confident that their teams were. The report warned that tracking activity rather than impact can encourage “productivity theatre”, where people work at looking busy. Judging work by what it achieves, rather than by hours online or speed of reply, removes much of the reason to perform busyness at all.

If you have ever tried to change how you work and slipped back within a week, that is normal too. The patterns that keep us busy are habits, and our earlier piece on why we abandon new habits after a few days looks at how to make new ones stick.

Busyness asks, “How full was my day?” Productivity asks, “What changed because of it?” The first question is easy to answer and pleasant to boast about. The second is harder, sometimes uncomfortable, and much more useful.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Switching between tasks has measurable time costs, which grow with the complexity of the tasks.
  • Very long working hours, around 55 or more a week, are associated with higher risks of stroke and heart disease.
  • In historical factory data, output per hour fell once weekly hours went well beyond about 50.
  • In experiments, people often prefer being occupied to sitting idle, and judge work as better when they believe more effort went into it.

Still uncertain

  • How well findings from First World War factory work apply to today’s office, creative and study work.
  • Whether busyness signals status in the same way in India; the main studies were done in the United States and Italy.
  • How much the mere urgency effect, studied in short experiments, shapes real working lives.
  • Figures on interruptions come from relatively small observational studies and from company reports based on their own software users.
  • Which practical strategies, such as batching messages or planning one key task, work best and for whom.
