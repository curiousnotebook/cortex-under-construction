“Finish the Whole Course”: Is the Antibiotic Rule Actually True?

For decades we were told that stopping antibiotics early breeds superbugs. A 2017 analysis in the BMJ argued that the evidence points the other way. The real answer depends on the infection, and in India, on a much bigger problem.

11 min read·29 December 2026

Almost everyone has heard it. A doctor writes a prescription, the chemist hands over a strip of tablets, and someone adds the warning: finish the whole course, even if you feel better, or the germs will come back stronger. It is one of the few pieces of medical advice that nearly every family repeats. When the World Health Organization surveyed people in 12 countries in 2015, anyone who said they would stop once they felt better was counted as having given the wrong answer.

Then, in July 2017, a group of British infection doctors, health psychologists and a statistician published an analysis in the BMJ with a blunt title: “The antibiotic course has had its day.” They argued that the idea behind the rule, that stopping early breeds resistance, is not supported by evidence for most common infections, and that taking antibiotics for longer than necessary is what really raises the risk. Was the rule wrong all along? The honest answer is: mostly, but not always, and the exceptions matter a great deal.

Where the rule came from

The fear of “too little” antibiotic is as old as antibiotics themselves. In 1941, when Howard Florey’s team in Oxford treated a policeman named Albert Alexander, the first patient to receive their penicillin, they had so little of the drug that they recovered it from his urine to use again. Over four days they stretched about four grams, less than a single day’s dose by modern standards. When it ran out, his improvement reversed and he died. Nothing showed that his bacteria had become resistant, but the lesson that seemed to follow was simple: stop too soon and the infection wins. (The full story is in Penicillin’s Forgotten Second Discovery.)

Four years later, accepting the Nobel Prize, Alexander Fleming painted a vivid warning. He imagined a man with a sore throat who buys penicillin, takes too little, and breeds bacteria that can survive the drug. The man passes them to his wife, and she dies of an infection that penicillin can no longer cure. Fleming’s moral was that if you use penicillin, you must use enough. He also made a prediction that sounds uncomfortably modern:

The time may come when penicillin can be bought by anyone in the shops. Alexander Fleming, Nobel lecture, 11 December 1945

Notice what Fleming was actually worried about: underdosing, a dose too weak to kill. That is not the same as stopping a full-strength course once the infection is beaten. And there is an irony. The bacterium in his story, Streptococcus pyogenes, the usual bacterial cause of sore throat, has never become resistant to penicillin.

The lengths of the courses themselves were never carefully worked out either. They were set by habit and caution, usually at 7, 10 or 14 days. The American infectious diseases physician Brad Spellberg has pointed out that these round numbers follow the seven-day week, a calendar fixed by the Roman emperor Constantine in AD 321, rather than anything we know about bacteria.

Two ways resistance can grow

To see why the 2017 authors challenged the rule, it helps to know that resistance can arise in two quite different ways.

The first is the one Fleming imagined, which the authors call target selection. Inside an infection, huge numbers of bacteria are multiplying, and a few may carry a chance mutation that lets them survive the drug. If treatment is too weak, or uses a single drug where several are needed, those survivors can take over. This genuinely happens with tuberculosis. In the 1940s, the first TB trials showed resistance emerging in patients treated with the antibiotic streptomycin alone, which is why TB is always treated with several drugs at once.

The second route, collateral selection, is now far more important. Our gut, skin and nose are home to vast numbers of bacteria, including E. coli, Klebsiella and Staphylococcus aureus, that usually do us no harm. Every dose of antibiotic, taken for any reason, kills the sensitive ones among them and leaves resistant ones free to multiply. Those resistant strains can then sit quietly, ready to cause a urinary infection, a wound infection or sepsis later on, and many can pass their resistance genes to other bacteria. These everyday “opportunist” bacteria cause most of the drug-resistant infections that threaten patients today.

For collateral selection, what matters is how much antibiotic the body’s bacteria are exposed to. A 2010 review of 24 studies found that after people were given an antibiotic in primary care for a urinary infection, their odds of carrying bacteria resistant to it were about two and a half times higher over the next two months, and still raised up to a year later. Longer courses and repeated courses were linked with more resistance.

For most infections, resistance does not come from the tablets left in the strip. It comes from the tablets taken, especially the ones that were never needed.

What the 2017 analysis claimed

Putting these pieces together, Martin Llewelyn and his colleagues argued that there was no evidence, for any common bacterial infection, that stopping antibiotics early raises a patient’s risk of a resistant infection. For the opportunist bacteria that pose the biggest threat, no clinical trial had shown more resistance with shorter treatment. Meanwhile, trials of shorter courses for pneumonia, cellulitis, kidney infections and abdominal infections had mostly found them to work as well as longer ones. In pneumonia caught in intensive care units, shorter treatment had even been linked with fewer later resistant infections.

They urged doctors, teachers and health agencies to stop saying “complete the course” and to admit openly that it had not been based on evidence. They suggested that something like “stop when you feel better” might one day replace it, but stressed that any new message needed testing first. Some agencies had already shifted quietly: the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Public Health England were telling people to take antibiotics “exactly as prescribed” rather than to finish every tablet.

The response was swift. The Royal College of General Practitioners in the UK warned that feeling better does not always mean an infection has been cleared, and urged patients not to change their behaviour on the strength of one article. That was a fair point: the piece was an argument built on existing studies, not a new trial. But both camps were arguing over the same gap. For many common infections, nobody had properly tested how short treatment could safely be.

What the trials have found

That gap has been closing, and the results have mostly favoured shorter treatment.

India produced one of the earliest large tests. In a trial published in 2004 across seven Indian referral hospitals, 2,188 children aged 2 to 59 months with non-severe pneumonia were given oral amoxicillin for either three or five days. Cure rates were almost identical: 89.5% and 89.9%. Children on the shorter course were also more likely to take every dose (94%, against 85% on the longer one).

In the UK and Ireland, the CAP-IT trial gave 814 young children leaving hospital with a chest infection either 3 or 7 days of amoxicillin. In both groups, exactly 12.5% needed another antibiotic within four weeks. The only notable difference was that cough lasted a little longer after the short course: a median of 12 days, compared with 10.

In France, adults admitted to hospital with pneumonia who were stable after three days of treatment were given either five more days of antibiotic or dummy tablets. Stopping at three days proved no worse: 77% of the placebo group were cured, compared with 68% of those who took all eight days.

Even for some of the most serious infections, the direction has been the same. The BALANCE trial, published in 2024, assigned 3,608 hospital patients with bacteria in their bloodstream, in 74 hospitals across seven countries, to 7 or 14 days of antibiotics. By 90 days, 14.5% of the 7-day group and 16.1% of the 14-day group had died. One week was as good as two.

These trials share an important feature. A doctor decided when treatment stopped, often after checking that the patient was stable. They tested shorter prescriptions, not patients quietly giving up on their own.

When the full course still matters

None of this means that duration never matters. The same research that undermines the blanket rule also marks out clear exceptions.

  • Tuberculosis. TB needs months of combination treatment, and incomplete treatment is a classic route to drug-resistant disease. This matters enormously in India, which in 2024 accounted for about a quarter of the world’s TB cases and nearly a third of all people who developed multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB.
  • Other “professional” germs. The 2017 authors listed HIV, malaria, gonorrhoea and typhoid among infections where resistance can arise inside the very infection being treated, so how they are treated matters a great deal.
  • Ear infections in toddlers. In a US trial of 520 children aged 6 to 23 months, treatment failed in 34% of those given 5 days of amoxicillin-clavulanate, compared with 16% of those given 10 days. Here, the shorter course was clearly worse.
  • Strep throat. The traditional 10 days of penicillin was chosen partly to prevent rheumatic fever, a reaction that can permanently damage heart valves. A Swedish trial of 433 patients found that 5 days of penicillin, taken four times a day at a higher daily dose, relieved symptoms as well as 10 days and faster, but the longer course was better at clearing the bacteria. Rheumatic fever is now rare in wealthy countries. India, however, was one of five countries that together accounted for nearly three-quarters of the world’s deaths from rheumatic heart disease in 2015, so a result from Sweden cannot simply be copied here.
  • Staphylococcus aureus in the blood. The BALANCE trial deliberately left out these infections, which specialists still treat for longer.

The strep throat trial also shows why “stop when you feel better” is not a simple answer. Feeling better and being cured are related but not identical. That is why the right person to shorten a course is the prescriber, who knows what is being treated.

India’s bigger problem

In India, the half-finished strip is rarely the main problem. The bigger issue is how many antibiotics are taken without a clear diagnosis, often bought straight from the chemist, exactly as Fleming feared.

Between 2014 and 2015, researchers trained people to visit 622 pharmacies in Delhi, Mumbai and Patna, posing as patients with two to three weeks of cough, a classic warning sign of TB. Only 13% of these visits ended with the ideal response: advice to see a doctor, with no antibiotics or steroids. In 37% of visits, the “patient” was handed antibiotics. Reassuringly, no pharmacy sold first-line TB drugs.

A study of private-sector sales, which make up the large majority of antibiotic use in India, counted about 5,071 million daily doses sold in 2019. Azithromycin was the single most consumed antibiotic formulation, and almost half of all doses (47%) came from formulations that the central drug regulator had never approved. Public understanding has not kept pace either: in the WHO’s 2015 survey, three-quarters of Indian respondents wrongly believed that antibiotics can treat colds and flu.

The rules exist on paper. Since March 2014, some third- and fourth-generation antibiotics and the anti-TB drugs have been listed under Schedule H1, which requires a prescription, a red “Rx” warning on the pack and a separate sales register at the chemist. Enforcement, though, has been patchy. In January 2024 Kerala launched Operation AMRITH, with surprise checks on pharmacies and a toll-free number for the public to report antibiotic sales without a prescription.

The stakes are high. A major global analysis estimated that in 2019, 1.27 million deaths were directly caused by drug-resistant bacterial infections, and nearly 5 million were associated with them. A follow-up study forecast more than 39 million such deaths between 2025 and 2050. The WHO’s 2025 surveillance report found that one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide was resistant to standard antibiotics, rising to one in three in its South-East Asia Region, which includes India.

The most useful question is not “Did you finish the strip?” but “Did you need an antibiotic at all, and for how many days?”

So what should you do?

The evidence points to a few practical habits. None of them involves quietly stopping a prescribed medicine on your own.

  • Take antibiotics only when a qualified doctor decides you need one. Colds and flu are caused by viruses, and antibiotics do nothing for them.
  • Ask how long, and why. It is reasonable to ask whether a shorter course would work for your infection. For many conditions, recommended durations have become shorter as evidence has accumulated.
  • If you feel better early, ask before you stop. Your doctor or pharmacist can tell you whether yours is an infection like TB, where every dose counts, or one where stopping sooner is safe.
  • Never save leftovers for “next time” or share them. A half-strip in a drawer easily becomes the next self-prescription for a viral fever. (For getting rid of old medicines, see What Really Happens to a Medicine After Its Expiry Date?)

“Finish the course” was a simple rule built on a reasonable fear. The evidence now suggests a better one: use antibiotics only when they are needed, for no longer than they are needed, and let that length be set by evidence rather than by the calendar. In a country where antibiotics are easier to buy than to use well, that is the message most worth passing on.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Taking antibiotics selects for resistant bacteria in a person’s own body, and longer or repeated courses are linked with more resistance.
  • Most drug-resistant infections today are caused by everyday “opportunist” bacteria, such as E. coli and Klebsiella, through collateral selection.
  • For several common infections, including pneumonia in children and adults and many bloodstream infections, randomised trials found shorter courses as effective as longer ones.
  • Some infections, such as tuberculosis, need a full combination course; incomplete treatment can breed resistance.
  • Ear infections in children under two did worse with 5 days of treatment than with 10.

Still uncertain

  • The shortest safe course for many common infections, especially with the antibiotics most used in India.
  • Whether letting patients decide for themselves when to stop (“stop when you feel better”) is safe and effective; this has not been well tested.
  • Whether shorter courses for strep throat protect against rheumatic fever as well as 10 days, in countries where it is still common.
  • How much resistance in India would fall if sales without prescription were truly controlled.
