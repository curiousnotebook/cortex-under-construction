Why Praise Seems to Fail and Punishment Seems to Work

Praise a brilliant performance and the next one is usually worse. Scold a poor one and the next is usually better. Teachers, parents and managers see this every day. Most of it has nothing to do with the praise or the scolding.

6 min read·23 November 2026

Early in his career, a young psychologist named Daniel Kahneman was teaching flight instructors in the Israeli Air Force about the psychology of training. He explained that rewarding good performance works better than punishing mistakes. One experienced instructor disagreed. In his experience, when he praised a trainee for a beautifully executed manoeuvre, the next attempt was usually worse. When he shouted at a trainee for a bad one, the next attempt was usually better. So, he said, don’t tell me that praise works and punishment doesn’t.

Kahneman, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on judgement and decision-making, described this as one of the most satisfying “eureka” moments of his career. The instructor’s observation was correct. His explanation was wrong.

The pattern hiding in the noise

Any performance is a mix of two things: underlying skill, and luck. A trainee pilot’s skill changes slowly over weeks. But on any single attempt, many small things vary: a gust of wind, a moment’s distraction, a lucky correction. An exceptionally good manoeuvre is probably a combination of decent skill and good luck. An exceptionally bad one is probably decent skill plus bad luck.

Luck does not repeat on demand. So after an unusually good attempt, the next one is likely to be closer to the trainee’s normal level, which means worse. After an unusually bad attempt, the next one is likely to be closer to normal too, which means better. This tendency for extreme results to be followed by less extreme ones is called regression to the mean.

Now add the instructor. He praises after the best attempts and shouts after the worst. Regression guarantees that, on average, praise will be followed by a decline and shouting by an improvement, even if his words have no effect at all. Kahneman later demonstrated this with a simple exercise. He asked officers to throw coins at a target behind their backs, twice. Those who did best the first time mostly did worse the second time, and those who did worst mostly improved, without anyone saying a word.

We reward and punish people at their extremes, so we almost always see them move back toward the middle afterwards, and we credit ourselves for the change.

Where the idea came from

The pattern was first described in the 1880s by the British scientist Francis Galton, who studied the heights of parents and their adult children. Very tall parents tended to have children who were tall, but not quite as tall as themselves. Very short parents tended to have children who were short, but not quite as short. Galton called this “regression towards mediocrity”. Height is not lucky in the way a coin throw is, but the logic is the same: whenever two measurements are related but not perfectly, extremes on the first tend to be less extreme on the second.

People really do learn the wrong lesson

Is this just a story? In 1985, the psychologist Paul Schaffner tested it. Participants were asked to use rewards and punishments to improve the performance of others they were overseeing. Unknown to them, that performance was entirely random; nothing the participants did could change it. Even so, participants came to believe that both reward and punishment worked, and they developed a preference for punishment. Regression to the mean had taught them a false lesson, just as it taught the flight instructor.

Everyday versions

Once you know the pattern, you can see it almost everywhere.

  • Exams. A student who tops one class test and is showered with praise often scores lower next time. A student who fails and is scolded often does better. Some of that is effort; a good part may simply be the luck of which questions came up.
  • Sport. A batter who scores a century is often out cheaply in the next match. In the United States, athletes have joked for decades about a “Sports Illustrated cover jinx”, a slump after appearing on the magazine’s cover. In many Indian homes, the same story is told as nazar lag gayi, the evil eye. Players reach the cover, or the headlines, at a peak, and peaks are usually followed by something more ordinary.
  • Work. The salesperson of the month often has a quieter month next. The worst-performing branch, after a stern review, often improves. Managers may conclude that criticism works and praise makes people complacent.
  • Health. People tend to see a doctor or try a remedy when symptoms are at their worst, and many conditions ease afterwards on their own. This is one reason improvement in a placebo group is not proof that placebos are powerful (see The Placebo Puzzle).
  • Public policy. Speed cameras are often installed at spots that have just had an unusually bad run of crashes. Crashes there would tend to fall anyway. When a 2013 analysis for the UK’s RAC Foundation evaluated 551 fixed cameras, it adjusted for regression to the mean and still found fatal and serious collisions near cameras fell by an average of 27%. Without that adjustment, the cameras would have been given credit they had not earned.

So does praise work after all?

Regression to the mean doesn’t prove that praise works or that punishment fails. It shows that the before-and-after pattern most of us rely on cannot tell us either way. To find out what actually works, you need to compare people who received praise or punishment with similar people who did not, ideally assigned at random.

When researchers have done careful comparisons, punishment looks much less impressive than it feels. A 2016 analysis by Elizabeth Gershoff and Andrew Grogan-Kaylor combined five decades of research covering more than 160,000 children. Spanking was linked to more aggression, more defiance and more mental health problems, and was not linked to better compliance, either immediately or in the long term. Since most studies observed families rather than randomly assigning punishment, these findings show links rather than proof of cause, but they point consistently in one direction.

None of this means that every kind of praise is helpful or that every consequence is harmful. Praise that is specific and sincere tends to be more useful than vague flattery, and clear, calm consequences are very different from harshness. But the instinct that “being tough gets results” is partly built on an illusion that regression creates.

How not to be fooled

  • Expect extremes to fade. After an unusually good or bad result, the next one will probably be closer to normal, whatever you do.
  • Judge on averages, not single results. Look at several performances over time before deciding what is working.
  • Watch out when you act at a peak or a trough. If you only step in after the worst results, you will almost always see improvement, and you will be tempted to credit yourself.
  • Ask “compared with what?” The only fair test of praise, punishment, a medicine or a policy is a comparison group that didn’t receive it (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).
  • Be wary of causal stories after extremes. A jinx, the evil eye, a complacent star, a “wake-up call”: these may all be regression in disguise.

The flight instructor was a careful observer. He noticed a real pattern. What he couldn’t see was the invisible hand of chance pulling every extreme back towards the middle. Most of us make the same mistake, every day, with the people we care about.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Extreme results on one occasion tend to be followed by less extreme results, purely because of chance variation.
  • Before-and-after comparisons after an extreme result can make an action look effective when it isn’t.
  • People can learn false lessons about reward and punishment from random performance.
  • Physical punishment of children is linked with worse outcomes and not with better compliance.

Still uncertain

  • Which forms of praise are most helpful, for whom, and in what settings.
  • How much of the link between harsh punishment and poor outcomes is caused by the punishment itself.
  • How large regression effects are in specific real-world settings, such as exam scores or workplace ratings.
  • How best to teach people to recognise regression in daily life.
