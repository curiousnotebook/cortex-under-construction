Born Unfinished: Why Human Babies Can’t Hold Up Their Heads

A foal can stand within hours of birth. A human baby needs months just to hold up its head. The usual explanation involves our hips, but the full story also runs through our brains and our mothers’ energy budgets.

8 min read·19 October 2026

A newborn foal can wobble to its feet within hours of birth and follow its mother across a field. A calf does much the same. A human newborn, by contrast, cannot even hold up its own head. In the first months, every parent learns the same reflex: one hand always behind the baby’s neck. So why is the species with the biggest brain born so spectacularly unready?

The classic answer is a story about hips. When our ancestors stood up on two legs, the pelvis narrowed, so babies had to be born early, before their heads grew too big to fit. There is real truth in that story. But over the past fifteen years, researchers have shown that it is incomplete, and the fuller explanation is, if anything, more interesting.

Why a newborn’s head wobbles

Start with the neck itself. A newborn’s head is enormous relative to its body, making up roughly a quarter of its total length, compared with about an eighth in an adult. Holding up that weight takes strong neck and upper-back muscles, but strength alone is not enough. Keeping a heavy head steady while the body moves needs constant, finely tuned adjustments from the brain.

That control system is not ready at birth. The nerve pathways that carry movement commands from the brain to the muscles are still being insulated with myelin, a fatty coating that lets signals travel quickly and precisely. Motor control generally matures from the head downwards, which is why babies usually gain steady head control first (typically by around three to four months), then sitting, then standing, and finally walking. A wobbly head is less a sign of a weak neck than of an unfinished brain.

Many animals are born helpless. Humans are helpless in an unusual way.

It is worth correcting one part of the puzzle first. Plenty of animals are born helpless: kittens, puppies and mice arrive with their eyes closed and little ability to move. Biologists call these species altricial. Others, such as horses, cattle, deer and most primates, are precocial: they have long pregnancies, usually one well-developed baby at a time, open eyes and quick mobility.

Humans are a strange mix. In most ways we follow the precocial primate pattern: a long pregnancy, usually a single baby, eyes open and senses working from birth. Yet our newborns are as physically helpless as an altricial species. In the 1940s the Swiss zoologist Adolf Portmann called this secondary altriciality: helplessness that evolved anew in a lineage whose ancestors had produced more capable infants. Compared with other primates, he suggested, human babies are effectively born about a year early.

Standing up changed the shape of birth

Our ancestors began walking on two legs somewhere around 5 to 7 million years ago. Upright walking reshaped the pelvis. In a chimpanzee, the pelvis is long and the birth canal is roomy compared with the size of the baby’s head. In humans, the pelvis became short and bowl-shaped, to support the organs above it and to anchor the muscles that keep us balanced on one leg with every stride.

The result is a birth canal that is not just tight but twisted. Its entrance is widest from side to side, while its exit is widest from front to back. A human baby therefore has to rotate as it descends, and it usually emerges facing away from its mother. That makes it hard for a mother to clear the baby’s airway or guide it out on her own, which may be one reason why, in almost every culture, women rarely give birth alone.

Then, starting about 2 million years ago, brains began to grow dramatically larger. A big-brained baby passing through a twisted, bipedal birth canal is a tight squeeze. Even today, obstructed labour, where the baby simply cannot pass through, affects roughly 3 to 6 per cent of births worldwide.

The “obstetric dilemma”

Put those two facts together, a narrow bipedal pelvis and a growing brain, and you get one of the best-known ideas in human evolution. The obstetric dilemma, named by the anthropologist Sherwood Washburn in 1960, says that evolution faced a trade-off. A wider pelvis would make birth easier but walking less efficient; a narrower one would make walking efficient but birth dangerous. The proposed compromise was to give birth earlier, while the baby’s head is still small enough to fit, and finish growing the brain outside the womb.

The numbers do look striking. A human newborn’s brain is only about a quarter of its eventual adult size (estimates range from roughly 25 to 30 per cent). A newborn chimpanzee’s brain is closer to 36 to 40 per cent of its adult size. The human brain then grows at a remarkable pace after birth, reaching about 90 per cent of its adult size by around the age of five.

Cracks in the classic story

It is a neat explanation, and parts of it are clearly right: birth really is tight, and the human brain really does do most of its growing after birth. But several findings suggest that the pelvis is not what sets the timing of birth.

Human pregnancy isn’t actually short. At around nine months, it is a little longer than a chimpanzee’s, not shorter. And once the mother’s body size is taken into account, human pregnancies are longer, and human newborns larger, than expected for an ape. If evolution had cut pregnancy short to protect the pelvis, we would expect the opposite.

Wider hips don’t seem to cost much. The dilemma assumes that a broader pelvis makes walking and running less efficient. When researchers measured this directly in 2015, pelvic width did not predict the energy cost of walking or running, in women or in men, and women were just as efficient as men.

Humans start walking right on schedule. A 2009 study of 24 mammal species, from rodents to hoofed animals to humans, found that the age at which an animal starts walking is predicted remarkably well by the size of its adult brain, as long as you count from conception rather than from birth. By that measure, humans are not late at all. Our first steps arrive just when you would expect for a mammal with a brain as large as ours.

A new suspect: the mother’s energy budget

If the pelvis isn’t setting the clock, what is? In 2012 the anthropologist Holly Dunsworth and her colleagues proposed the energetics of gestation and fetal growth (EGG) hypothesis. Growing a fetus is metabolically expensive, and the cost climbs steeply in the final months, as the baby, and especially its brain, grows fast. Mammals seem to have a ceiling on how much energy their bodies can sustain over long periods, at roughly two to two-and-a-half times their resting rate. By around nine months, a pregnant woman is approaching that ceiling.

In this view, birth happens not because the baby’s head is about to stop fitting, but because the mother’s body can no longer meet the fetus’s growing needs from the inside. Once the baby is born, the cost of feeding it can be spread out over time and, eventually, shared with other people.

The energy demands do not end at birth. One analysis of brain scans found that a child’s brain is at its hungriest in early childhood, around the ages of four to five, when it burns glucose equivalent to about two-thirds of the body’s resting energy use. Building a human brain is a long and costly project, wherever it takes place.

So which explanation is right?

Probably both, in different ways. Many researchers now see the pelvis and the large brain as having shaped how we are born, with a rotating baby, a difficult labour and a need for help, while energy limits may better explain when we are born.

That still leaves a puzzle. If wider hips cost so little, why hasn’t evolution simply widened the birth canal and made obstructed labour rare? One mathematical answer, the “cliff-edge” model, points out that selection favours bigger, healthier babies right up to the point where a baby is too big to be born, and then the cost becomes catastrophic. When the safe zone ends at a cliff edge like that, a population can settle uncomfortably close to it, so a small proportion of births will always be too tight.

Human babies are not helpless simply because pregnancy was cut short. They are helpless because the human brain is so large that much of its growth has to happen after birth, whatever finally sets the timing of labour.

A baby that needs a village

Whatever the cause, human helplessness has had enormous social consequences. A baby that cannot hold its own head up needs constant carrying, feeding and protection for months, and dependent care for years. Mothers in our evolutionary past could rarely have managed this alone. Many anthropologists argue that this helped make humans cooperative breeders, raising children with help from fathers, grandmothers, older siblings and others.

Portmann described the first year of life as a kind of “social womb”: a time when the brain does much of its growing surrounded not by amniotic fluid but by faces, voices, touch and language. Seen this way, being born unfinished is not only a cost. It means the human brain is shaped, from very early on, by the world and the people around it.

There is a lesson about science here, too. Like the story of ulcers and stress (see The Bacterium in the Stomach), the textbook version of the obstetric dilemma captured something real but not the whole picture. And the brain that keeps growing long after birth is the same brain that, years later, learns best through effort and a little forgetting (see Forgetting Is Not the Enemy).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Upright walking reshaped the human pelvis, producing a narrow, twisted birth canal and a birth in which the baby must rotate.
  • Human newborns have a smaller share of their adult brain size than newborn chimpanzees, and much of human brain growth happens after birth.
  • Babies gain motor control gradually over the first year, starting with the head.
  • Obstructed labour remains a significant cause of complications worldwide.

Still uncertain

  • Whether the timing of birth is set mainly by the mother’s energy limits, by the pelvis, or by a mix of both.
  • Why the birth canal has not evolved to be wider, if wider hips cost little in walking efficiency.
  • When in human evolution today’s pattern of very helpless newborns first appeared.
