The Ship of Theseus: What Makes You the Same Person Over Time?

If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it still the same ship? It sounds like a riddle for idle philosophers, until you realise the ship is you.

11 min read·21 December 2026

Find a photograph of yourself at the age of six. That child had a different body, a different voice, different hopes and fears, and very few of the memories you have now. Yet you say, without hesitation, “that’s me”. What exactly makes it true?

It is one of the oldest questions in philosophy, and it still has no agreed answer. But the attempts to answer it, from ancient Athens and ancient India to modern laboratories, reveal a surprising amount about how we think about ourselves, our futures and the people we love.

A ship in an Athenian harbour

The puzzle gets its name from a story told by the Greek writer Plutarch, around the end of the first century. According to legend, the hero Theseus sailed home to Athens in a thirty-oared ship. The Athenians kept that ship for centuries, until the time of the statesman Demetrius of Phalerum in the late fourth century BC. Whenever a timber rotted, they replaced it with a sound one. Plutarch reports that philosophers used the ship as a standard example in their arguments: some said it remained the same ship, others that it did not.

In 1655, the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes added a twist. Suppose someone collected every old plank as it was removed and later rebuilt the original ship from them. Now there are two ships. One has the original history, sailing continuously in the harbour. The other has the original material. Which is the Ship of Theseus? Both seem to have a claim, but they cannot both be the same ship as the original, because they are not the same ship as each other. Hobbes himself called the result absurd.

Philosophers still disagree about how to resolve it. Some say identity follows the continuous history, some the material, and some argue that the question has no single right answer at all. The ship is a useful warm-up, because the same puzzle applies, with much higher stakes, to people.

Is your body the ship?

You may have heard that your body replaces itself completely every seven years. The truth is more interesting, and messier.

In a 2021 study, Ron Sender and Ron Milo of the Weizmann Institute estimated that the human body replaces about 330 billion cells every day, around four million every second. Most of these are blood cells, especially red blood cells, and cells lining the gut, which are worn away constantly. By weight, the body turns over roughly 80 grams of cells a day.

Other parts barely change. In 2005, the stem-cell researcher Jonas Frisén and colleagues at the Karolinska Institute in Sweden found a clever way to date human cells, using carbon-14 released into the atmosphere by nuclear bomb tests during the Cold War. Cells take up carbon when they are made, so the amount of carbon-14 in their DNA works like a birth certificate. They estimated that the average cell in an adult body may be only seven to ten years old, but the cells in parts of the brain’s cortex were as old as the person.

So you are not a ship that gets entirely rebuilt every seven years. You are more like a ship in which some planks are swapped daily, others every few years, and a few, in the brain, are kept for life. Even so, almost nobody thinks that personal identity rests on keeping the same cells. If it did, a heart transplant would make someone a slightly different person. Most philosophers have looked elsewhere.

Your body is constantly rebuilt, some parts in days and others not at all. Physical continuity matters, but the same cells are not what make you “you”.

Locke: you are what you remember

In 1694, the English philosopher John Locke proposed the most influential answer. A person, he argued, is a thinking being who can recognise itself as the same over time, and personal identity reaches back exactly as far as consciousness, essentially memory, can reach. You are the same person as that six-year-old to the extent that you are connected by remembered experience.

Locke tested his idea with a thought experiment. Imagine a prince’s mind, with all his memories, entering the body of a cobbler. Who is the person who wakes up in the cobbler’s body? Locke said it would be the prince. The person goes where the memories go.

The idea soon met objections. The Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid offered a famous one in 1785. Imagine a boy who is flogged for robbing an orchard, who later, as a young officer, remembers the flogging and performs a brave act in battle, and who later still, as an old general, remembers the battle but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke’s test, the general is the same person as the officer, and the officer is the same as the boy, yet the general is not the same as the boy. Identity cannot work like that. Earlier, the philosopher Joseph Butler had raised another problem: to remember an experience as yours, you seem to need to be the same person already, so memory cannot be what makes you the same.

Modern versions of Locke’s view avoid these objections by talking about psychological continuity rather than direct memory: overlapping chains of memories, beliefs, intentions and personality, each stage linked to the one before. The old general is connected to the boy through the officer, like links in a chain. There is a further twist, too: memory itself is less a recording than a reconstruction (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller), so the chain is made partly of stories we retell ourselves.

Parfit: maybe identity is not what matters

The British philosopher Derek Parfit pushed these ideas to their limit in his 1984 book Reasons and Persons. He asked readers to imagine cases that break our ordinary sense of identity. In one, a machine scans you, destroys your body and builds an exact copy on Mars, with all your memories and personality. Is the person on Mars you? In another, the machine malfunctions and leaves the original alive as well as building the copy. Now two people are psychologically continuous with the person who stepped in, and they cannot both be you.

Parfit’s radical conclusion was that the question “Is it really me?” may not always have an answer, and that it matters less than we assume. What we actually care about, he argued, is psychological connectedness and continuity, whether or not strict identity holds. He found the conclusion freeing. When he believed his identity was a deep further fact, he wrote, his life felt like a glass tunnel leading into darkness. Once he stopped believing it, the walls of the tunnel disappeared, and he found himself caring less about his own future and more about other people.

Or are you simply an animal?

Not everyone accepts that we are bundles of memories. Some philosophers, sometimes called animalists, argue that each of us is a human organism, and we persist as long as that living organism does, whatever happens to our memories. On this view, you were once a foetus with no psychology at all, and you would still exist in a deep coma. Its critics point to imagined brain transplants: if your brain, with all your memories and character, were moved into another body, most people feel that you would go with it, not stay behind in the empty skull. The debate between psychological and biological views continues.

An older answer from India

Long before Locke, a very similar question was discussed in India. A Buddhist text called the Milindapañha, “The Questions of King Milinda”, records a dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and an Indo-Greek king, Milinda, usually identified with Menander, who ruled in the north-west of the subcontinent in the second century BC.

When the king asks what a person really is, Nāgasena asks him how he arrived. By chariot, says the king. Then what is the chariot? Is it the wheels? The axle? The frame? The king agrees that none of the parts, alone, is the chariot. Nāgasena explains that “chariot” is a useful name for a particular arrangement of parts, and that “person” is the same kind of name, for a changing collection of body and mind with no unchanging core behind it.

Asked whether a person is the same from moment to moment, Nāgasena offers an image as good as any in Western philosophy: milk turns into butter and then into ghee. They are not the same thing, yet they are not unrelated either. A person, he says, is a continuity that is neither the same nor entirely different. Other Indian traditions, notably many schools of Hindu philosophy, hold the opposite view, that there is an enduring self, the ātman. The ship, in other words, has been sailing through Indian thought for more than two thousand years.

Many traditions arrive at a similar idea: a person may be less like a fixed object and more like a continuing process, such as a flame or a river, that is neither exactly the same nor completely different over time.

What ordinary people think makes you “you”

Psychologists have asked what non-philosophers actually believe. Their answers suggest that Locke was only partly right.

In a 2015 study, the psychologists Nina Strohminger and Shaun Nichols surveyed 248 people whose family members had a neurodegenerative disease: frontotemporal dementia, Alzheimer’s disease or motor neurone disease. They asked how much the patient seemed to have become a different person. The strongest predictor was not memory loss. It was change in the patient’s moral character, such as honesty, kindness and empathy. Patients with frontotemporal dementia, which can change behaviour and social conduct, were seen as having changed the most. Earlier experiments by the same researchers had found that people consider moral traits more central to identity than other mental or physical traits.

We seem to recognise a loved one less by what they remember than by how they treat others. In everyday life, the “self” that matters most is moral.

The person you will become

The question also points forward. In a 2013 study of more than 19,000 people aged 18 to 68, the psychologists Jordi Quoidbach, Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson found that people of every age reported having changed a great deal in the past ten years, but predicted that they would change relatively little in the next ten. The researchers called this the end of history illusion: at every age, we tend to believe we have finally become who we will be. As a result, people were willing to overpay today for things that suit their current tastes, assuming those tastes would last.

If your future self will be noticeably different, it can feel almost like a stranger, and people tend to under-invest in strangers. In 2011, a team led by Hal Hershfield showed some participants digitally aged images of their own faces before asking how they would divide money between spending now and saving for retirement. Seeing their older selves made people choose to save more: in one study, they were willing to put about a third more into retirement savings than a comparison group. Making the future self feel real changed how people treated it.

So is it the same ship?

Perhaps the most honest answer is that “same” means different things depending on what we care about. If we are asking about responsibility, such as whether to hold someone accountable for something they did decades ago, we care about psychological continuity and moral character. If we are asking about legal identity, the continuous body and its documents usually decide. If we are deciding whether to save for retirement, what matters is how connected we feel to the person who will spend the money.

The ship of Theseus does not have to be solved to be useful. It reminds us that we are not finished objects but ongoing projects: partly rebuilt every day, partly kept for life, and connected across the years by memories, habits, relationships and values that we keep choosing to carry forward.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The body replaces hundreds of billions of cells each day, mostly blood and gut cells, while some brain cells last a lifetime; the “new body every seven years” claim is an oversimplification.
  • Memory-based theories of identity face serious objections, which led to modern theories of psychological continuity.
  • When judging whether a loved one has “changed”, people weigh moral character more heavily than memory.
  • People of all ages tend to underestimate how much they will change in the future.

Still uncertain

  • Whether personal identity is best explained by psychology, by the body, by both, or by neither; philosophers have no consensus.
  • Whether questions about identity always have a definite answer, or whether some cases are genuinely indeterminate.
  • How far findings about people’s beliefs on identity, mostly from Western samples, hold across cultures.
  • How large and lasting the effects of “future self” interventions are in real financial decisions.
