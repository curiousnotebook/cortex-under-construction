Occam’s Razor: Is the Simplest Explanation Always Best?

“The simplest explanation is usually the right one” is one of the most quoted rules of thinking. It is also widely misunderstood. Used well, it is a sharp tool. Used carelessly, it cuts away the truth.

7 min read·7 November 2026

In the 1840s, astronomers had a problem. The planet Uranus was not moving quite as Newton’s laws predicted. One option was to conclude that Newton’s laws were wrong. Another was to add something new: an unseen planet, further out, tugging Uranus off course. The French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated where such a planet would have to be. In 1846, astronomers in Berlin pointed a telescope at that spot and found Neptune.

A few years later, Le Verrier noticed that Mercury had a similar problem. The point where its orbit came closest to the Sun was shifting slightly faster than Newton’s laws allowed. He tried the same trick and proposed another unseen planet, closer to the Sun, which became known as Vulcan. Astronomers searched for it for decades. Some even reported seeing it. It was never there. In 1915, Albert Einstein’s general theory of relativity explained Mercury’s orbit exactly, with no extra planet needed.

Both times, the same move was made: add one new thing to keep a trusted theory. Once it was a triumph, once a dead end. Which explanation was the “simple” one? That question sits at the heart of Occam’s razor.

The man, and the words he didn’t say

The razor is named after William of Ockham, an English Franciscan friar and philosopher who lived from about 1287 to 1347. The famous wording, “entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity”, does not actually appear in his surviving writings. The Latin version most often quoted was written down in 1639 by an Irish theologian, John Punch, who described it as a common saying among scholars. Ockham did use the idea often, insisting that something should only be assumed to exist if reason, experience or scripture gave grounds for it.

The principle is much older than Ockham. Aristotle preferred proofs that needed fewer assumptions. Isaac Newton made it the first of his rules of reasoning: admit no more causes of natural things than are both true and sufficient to explain them. Notice the word sufficient. The razor was never “choose the simplest story”. It was “don’t add more than you need to explain what you see”.

What “simple” actually means

Philosophers distinguish two kinds of simplicity, and they often pull in opposite directions. One is about how few things a theory says exist, sometimes called parsimony. The other is about how few and how neat its rules are, sometimes called elegance.

Neptune shows the tension. Adding a planet made the list of things in the solar system longer. But it kept the laws of motion exactly as they were. The alternative, rewriting Newton’s laws to fit Uranus, would have meant fewer planets and messier laws. Astronomers chose the extra object, and they were right. With Mercury, adding Vulcan kept the laws simple at the cost of an invisible planet that kept refusing to appear. In the end, a new set of laws that explained everything with no special additions won out. Simplicity is not one measurement. It is a judgement about where complexity is doing real work.

A myth about simplicity in the history books

A popular story says that Copernicus replaced an absurdly complicated Earth-centred universe, full of circles on circles, with a far simpler Sun-centred one. The reality is less tidy. The historian Thomas Kuhn concluded that Copernicus’s system, as he actually worked it out, was “neither more accurate nor significantly simpler” than the Ptolemaic system it challenged. Copernicus still used perfect circles and needed many of them. It was Johannes Kepler, decades later, whose elliptical orbits made the Sun-centred picture both more accurate and genuinely simpler. A simple-sounding idea is not always simpler in practice, and the story of its success can itself be simplified after the fact.

Occam’s razor does not say the simplest story is true. It says: don’t add assumptions unless the evidence makes you.

Why simplicity earns its place: the statistician’s answer

Is there any deeper reason to prefer simpler explanations, beyond taste? Statisticians have found one.

Imagine plotting ten points on a graph and trying to draw a line through them. A straight line may miss a few points slightly. A wiggly curve with enough bends can pass through every single point perfectly. The wiggly curve looks better, but it has learned the noise along with the pattern. Give it ten new points and it will usually predict them worse than the straight line. This is called overfitting, and it is a central problem in statistics, economics and machine learning. Flexible models can fit almost anything, which is exactly why fitting the data well proves so little.

In 1974, the Japanese statistician Hirotugu Akaike turned this into a practical tool. His Akaike information criterion rewards a model for fitting the data and penalises it for every extra adjustable part. It is now used routinely to choose between competing models.

Bayesian statistics offers a related insight. In 1992, William Jefferys and James Berger showed that a simple theory makes sharp, risky predictions, while a flexible theory spreads its bets across many possible outcomes. When the data land where the sharp theory said they would, the evidence favours it strongly. Their example was Mercury itself. Einstein’s theory predicted Mercury’s extra drift with no adjustable parts, and the observations matched. A version of Newton’s theory with an extra “fudge factor” could also fit the observations, but only because it could have fitted almost anything. On their calculations, the evidence favoured Einstein by a factor of roughly 28. In this sense, Occam’s razor is not just a preference. It falls out of reasoning carefully about probability (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

There is a link here to the replication crisis, too. Studies analysed with too much flexibility can find patterns in noise, just like an overfitted curve (see The Replication Crisis).

When the simple answer is wrong: lessons from medicine

Medical students are taught the razor as a guide to diagnosis: try to find one condition that explains all of a patient’s symptoms, rather than several. It is good advice, because one disease is usually more likely than two unrelated ones appearing at the same time.

But medicine also has a famous counter-rule, known as Hickam’s dictum and attributed to the American physician John Hickam: “A patient can have as many diseases as he damn well pleases.” In a 2004 case in the New England Journal of Medicine, a 60-year-old woman with a weakened immune system came to hospital short of breath. She turned out to have two serious conditions at once, a lung infection and a blood clot in her lungs. Searching for a single explanation could have meant missing one of them. The dictum matters most for older people, who often live with several long-term conditions.

A 2024 analysis of 220 complex case reports suggested the two rules are less opposed than they seem. Patients often had more than one diagnosis, but the extras followed predictable patterns: a long-standing condition, a harmless incidental finding, or a second disease caused by the first. Truly unrelated diseases arriving together were the rarest kind. The authors argued that a “unifying” diagnosis does not have to be a single disease. It can be a causal chain that links the problems together.

Medicine has also seen the reverse mistake: a simple, satisfying explanation that was wrong. For decades, stomach ulcers were blamed on stress and acid. The less intuitive answer, a bacterium, turned out to be the main cause (see The Bacterium in the Stomach).

Using the razor well

  • Count assumptions, not words. A short explanation can hide many assumptions. Conspiracy theories often sound simple (“they did it”) but require countless people to keep a secret perfectly, which is a lot of extra machinery.
  • Simple must still be sufficient. An explanation that leaves out part of the evidence is not simpler. It is incomplete.
  • Treat it as a tie-breaker. When two explanations fit the evidence equally well, prefer the one that assumes less, then look for evidence that can separate them.
  • Be suspicious of explanations that can fit anything. A theory that would have explained the opposite result just as easily is telling you very little.
  • Remember it is a guide, not a law. Nature is under no obligation to be simple. Sometimes the truth really is complicated.

The often-quoted line attributed to Einstein, “everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler”, is probably a later paraphrase rather than his exact words. It captures the idea perfectly anyway. The razor is for trimming away what isn’t needed, not for cutting into the evidence itself.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The famous wording of Occam’s razor does not appear in Ockham’s writings, though he used the idea.
  • Models with many adjustable parts can fit existing data well yet predict new data poorly.
  • Statistical tools such as the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian model comparison formally penalise unnecessary complexity.
  • In medicine, patients can have several diagnoses at once, particularly older patients with long-term conditions.

Still uncertain

  • How to measure simplicity in a way everyone agrees on; it can depend on how a theory is written down.
  • How to weigh fewer “things” against fewer or neater “rules” when they conflict.
  • Whether nature itself is simple, or whether simplicity is mainly a useful habit of human reasoning.
  • Which model-selection rule is best; different methods penalise complexity differently and can disagree.
