Occam’s Razor: Is the Simplest Explanation Always Best?

Prefer the simpler explanation, all else being equal. It is one of the most useful rules in thinking. It is also one of the most misunderstood.

7 min read·7 November 2026

When a noise comes from the attic, you might think of a ghost, a raccoon, or a loose pipe. Most people reach for the raccoon, and they are usually right. That habit has a name: Occam’s razor, the principle that when two explanations fit the facts equally well, we should prefer the one that assumes less. The phrase suggests a sharp tool that cuts away needless complications. But the principle is more subtle than its popular version, and the simplest explanation is not always the right one.

Where the name comes from

The razor is named after William of Ockham, an English philosopher and friar of the fourteenth century. The famous Latin phrase usually attached to him, entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem (entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity), does not appear in his surviving writings. It seems to be a later formulation, and the name “Occam’s razor” itself appeared centuries after his death. Ockham did use the idea, and he applied it aggressively against the proliferation of metaphysical entities, such as the universals that other philosophers thought existed in addition to particular things. But the attribution is a tidy story, and historians of philosophy have been clear that it is partly a legend.

What matters is the idea, which was widely shared in medieval logic. The principle is a preference, not a proof. It says that, given two explanations of equal power, you should not add unnecessary parts to one of them.

Why simpler theories can predict better

The most interesting justification for the razor comes from statistics, not philosophy. Imagine fitting a curve to a set of points. A straight line may miss a few points. A wiggly curve with many parameters can pass through every point exactly, but it may behave badly on new data, because it has learned the noise as well as the signal. A moderately flexible curve often predicts best.

In 1994, the philosophers Malcolm Forster and Elliott Sober argued that the statistician Hirotugu Akaike had given a precise way to see this. Akaike’s information criterion, published in the 1970s, estimates how well a model is likely to predict new data, and it penalises models for the number of parameters they use. Forster and Sober used it to argue that simplicity has a real epistemic role: it is not just an aesthetic taste, but something that can be connected to expected predictive accuracy. The approach has not gone unchallenged. Other statisticians have argued that AIC can be inconsistent in some settings, and that different criteria give different advice. But the basic point stands: complexity has a cost.

Extra parameters always improve the fit to the data you already have. The question is whether they improve the prediction of data you have not yet seen.

The Bayesian razor

A second justification comes from Bayesian probability. In 1992, William Jefferys and James Berger showed that Bayesian inference tends to favour simpler hypotheses without any explicit rule saying so. The reason is an effect now often called the “automatic Occam’s razor.” A complex model spreads its probability across many possible parameter values. When the data happen to fall where the simple model predicted them, the simple model gets more of the credit, because the complex model had to spread its bets thinly. The complex model is not penalised for being complex in a crude sense; it is penalised for predicting less sharply.

Psychologists have tested whether people reason this way. A 2018 study in Cognitive Science reported that people’s intuitive judgments about probability and explanation followed the pattern predicted by Bayesian reasoning, penalising hypotheses for flexibility as well as for the number of free parameters. The authors described this as a razor of ordinary people as well as of statisticians. It suggests that the intuition behind the razor is not just a cultural habit but has a rational basis, though whether it always matches what people do in harder cases is a separate question.

When the simplest answer misleads

None of this means the simplest explanation always wins. The razor can be misused in at least three ways.

First, simplicity is not the same as truth. A theory can be simple and wrong, or complex and right. The natural world has no obligation to be elegant. Many important scientific advances added complexity, and some of them were resisted for exactly that reason. Simplicity is a tie-breaker between explanations that fit the evidence equally well, not a substitute for evidence.

Second, what counts as simple depends on how you describe the problem. A theory that looks short in one language can be long in another, and “fewer entities” can mean very different things depending on which entities you count. The razor needs a clear measure of complexity, and such measures are often contested.

Third, and most important in everyday life, the razor can narrow your attention too early. In medicine, the principle is sometimes taught as diagnostic parsimony: look for one diagnosis that explains all the symptoms. A counterweight is known as Hickam’s dictum, traditionally stated as the claim that a patient can have as many diseases as they please. Hickam was a physician who taught in the mid-twentieth century, and the saying is often attributed to him, though the exact origin is uncertain. The point is that older or unwell patients often have several ordinary conditions at once, and the single tidy diagnosis may miss the others. The razor is a sensible default; it should not stop you from looking for the second cause.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Models with more free parameters fit existing data better but can predict new data worse, which gives a statistical basis for preferring simpler models.
  • Bayesian reasoning tends to favour simpler hypotheses when they fit the data equally well, through the automatic penalty for flexibility.
  • The exact wording “entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity” is not found in Ockham’s surviving works.

Still uncertain

  • How best to measure complexity, and whether different criteria, such as AIC and BIC, give consistent advice in all settings.
  • Whether people’s everyday use of simplicity matches the Bayesian ideal in complex or unfamiliar problems.
  • How often the simplest diagnosis is the right one in real clinical practice, which depends on the population and setting.

A better way to use the razor

The most useful version of Occam’s razor is a question rather than a verdict: what is the least I need to assume to explain what I see? Then the next question matters just as much: what would I expect to see if the simple story were false? If a simpler explanation survives tests that a complicated one must pass, it earns its place. If it fails to account for something important, the extra complexity is not a sin. It is the cost of accuracy.

Albert Einstein is often quoted as saying that scientific models should be as simple as possible, but no simpler. The line is usually reported in that form, and it captures the spirit of the principle better than the razor does. Simplicity is a target to aim for, subject to the evidence. It is not a reason to stop looking.

The lesson connects to other ideas on this site. A striking result still has to be weighed against what was likely before the evidence arrived, as explored in Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine. And the habit of accepting a tidy story can be undone by the failures we never see, as in Survivorship Bias: The Success Stories You Never Hear About. Good thinking uses simplicity as a starting point, and then lets the evidence decide how much complexity is needed.
