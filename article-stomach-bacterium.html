The Bacterium in the Stomach: How Ulcers Stopped Being About Stress

For decades, stomach ulcers were blamed on stress, spicy food and acid. Two researchers suspected a microbe, and the way their story is usually told is only half right.

5 min read·15 October 2026

Picture a busy executive in the 1970s with a burning pain in the upper abdomen. The diagnosis is a peptic ulcer, a raw sore in the lining of the stomach or the first part of the small intestine. The advice is familiar: reduce stress, avoid spicy food, take medicine to reduce stomach acid. The ulcer heals. Months later, it comes back. For many patients, this cycle repeated for years, and some eventually needed surgery.

The old consensus

The prevailing view was reasonable on its face. The stomach is filled with strong acid, so it seemed an unlikely home for bacteria. Ulcers were linked with acid, so treatments focused on neutralising or reducing acid. Stress and lifestyle filled in the rest of the explanation. And acid-reducing drugs did help ulcers heal, which seemed to confirm the theory.

But the theory had a gap: if acid was the whole story, why did so many ulcers return once treatment stopped?

What a pathologist noticed

In Perth, Australia, pathologist Robin Warren was examining stomach biopsies under the microscope. He repeatedly saw small, curved bacteria living on the stomach lining, especially where the tissue was inflamed. He teamed up with a young doctor in training, Barry Marshall, to investigate.

Growing the bacteria in the laboratory proved frustrating. According to Marshall’s own account, the breakthrough came partly by accident: some culture plates were left incubating for longer than usual over a holiday weekend, giving the slow-growing organism time to appear. The bacterium was eventually named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1984, Marshall and Warren published a study in The Lancet reporting that these bacteria were found in most of the patients they examined who had gastritis (stomach inflammation) or ulcers.

The famous drink

Finding bacteria alongside disease shows an association, not necessarily a cause. Perhaps the bacteria simply liked living on already-damaged tissue. To test whether the organism could cause disease, Marshall did something memorable: he drank a culture of it. Within days he developed symptoms, and a biopsy showed acute gastritis, which later resolved.

It’s a wonderful story, but notice its limits. It was one person. It showed the bacterium could cause stomach inflammation, not that it caused ulcers. Self-experiments make headlines; they rarely settle scientific questions on their own.

Dramatic moments make memorable stories, but scientific consensus usually changes through slower, less glamorous evidence: replication, randomised trials and the testing of predictions.

The myth of the lone heretic

The popular version of this story casts Marshall and Warren as lone heroes battling a stubborn establishment. There was real scepticism at first, and it was understandable: the claim contradicted long-held assumptions. But the fuller history is more encouraging.

Researchers around the world tested the idea. The decisive question was practical: if you eliminate H. pylori with antibiotics, do ulcers stop coming back? Trials showed that, in many patients, they did. By 1994, a United States National Institutes of Health consensus panel recommended that ulcer patients infected with H. pylori receive antibiotic treatment. The same year, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified the bacterium as a cause of cancer in humans, recognising its link to stomach cancer. In 2005, Warren and Marshall received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

A decade from surprising observation to changed medical guidelines is not instant, but it is not a tale of science refusing to listen either. It is closer to science working as designed: an unexpected claim met appropriate doubt, the evidence accumulated, and practice changed.

The puzzles that remain

The discovery raised as many questions as it answered. A large 2017 analysis estimated that roughly half of the world’s population carries H. pylori. Yet most infected people never develop an ulcer. Why? Researchers point to differences between bacterial strains, the person’s genetics and immune response, and environmental factors, but how these combine is still being worked out.

We also know that H. pylori is not the only cause of ulcers. Anti-inflammatory painkillers such as aspirin and ibuprofen (NSAIDs) are another major cause. And growing antibiotic resistance is making the bacterium harder to eliminate in many parts of the world, a reminder that a medical victory can be undermined over time.

Lessons for curious minds

  • Question “obvious” assumptions. “Nothing can live in stomach acid” felt like common sense, and it was wrong.
  • But demand evidence for new ideas, too. Healthy scepticism is not the villain of this story; it is what forced the evidence to become convincing.
  • Prior beliefs shape how we read new evidence. A surprising finding needs stronger support than an expected one, an idea explored in Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • H. pylori infection is a major cause of peptic ulcers, alongside NSAID use.
  • Eradicating the infection greatly reduces the chance of ulcers returning.
  • The bacterium is classified as a human carcinogen because of its link to stomach cancer.

Still uncertain

  • Exactly why most infected people never develop ulcers or cancer.
  • How best to manage rising antibiotic resistance worldwide.
  • Whether long-term infection has any meaningful protective effects, which some studies have suggested and others dispute.
