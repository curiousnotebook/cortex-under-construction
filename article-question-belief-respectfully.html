Who Said So?

How to Question a Belief Without Disrespecting the Person Who Holds It

Checking whether something our elders swore by is true is one thing on the page. Raising it with the grandmother who taught it to you is another. Research suggests there is a better path than staying silent or saying “that’s nonsense”.

10 min read·15 December 2026

It happens at the dinner table, or in the family chat. An uncle forwards a message about a miracle cure. A parent insists on a rule you are fairly sure has no basis. A grandmother repeats a warning she has believed all her life. You face two familiar options. You can say nothing and feel dishonest, or you can say “that’s not true” and watch the conversation, and perhaps the evening, go cold.

Every article in this series asks “who said so?” about something people have long believed. Some of those beliefs turn out to be myths; others have more logic behind them than we expected (see Don’t Sweep After Sunset). But real beliefs are held by real people, often people we love and owe a great deal to. This essay is about the conversation, not the fact-check: what research suggests about questioning a belief while keeping the relationship, and the person’s dignity, intact.

The good news: facts are not as useless as people say

A popular idea holds that correcting someone only makes them believe more strongly, the so-called “backfire effect”. If that were true, the kindest option would always be silence. Fortunately, it seems to be rare.

In a series of five experiments with more than 10,000 people, the political scientists Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter tested corrections on 52 contested issues, deliberately choosing ones where backfire seemed likely. They did not find a single correction that made people’s beliefs more wrong. On average, people moved towards the facts, even when the facts were awkward for their side. The Debunking Handbook 2020, written by a large group of researchers who study misinformation, reached a similar conclusion: backfire effects happen only occasionally, and fear of them is no reason to avoid correcting a falsehood.

A striking 2024 study shows how far careful conversation can go. Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand asked more than 2,000 people who believed a conspiracy theory to explain it in their own words, along with their evidence. Each then had a short conversation, about eight minutes on average, with an AI system that responded to their specific reasons with evidence, politely and patiently. Belief in the conspiracy fell by about 20% on average, about one in four participants no longer believed it afterwards, and the change was still there two months later. The effect was weaker when the belief was central to a person’s identity.

The lesson is not that machines should do our arguing for us. It is that people can change their minds when evidence addresses their actual reasons, in a conversation that does not humiliate them. The question is how to create that kind of conversation with someone you know.

Why a question can feel like an attack

Beliefs are rarely just pieces of information. They come bundled with identity, loyalty and memory. A remedy may have been taught by someone’s own mother. A rule may stand for years of caring for a family. To question it can sound like saying “you were foolish” or “the people you trusted were wrong”, and for an elder, “you no longer know better than me”.

Psychologists have tested what happens when that sense of threat is reduced. In a classic set of experiments, Geoffrey Cohen, Joshua Aronson and Claude Steele asked people with strong views on capital punishment to read evidence against their position. Before reading, some participants wrote about a value that mattered to them in an unrelated part of their lives. Those who had affirmed their values in this way judged the opposing evidence more fairly than those who had not. In a later review, Cohen and the psychologist David Sherman argued that when people feel secure in their overall worth, they have less need to defend every belief that is challenged.

In everyday terms, this suggests starting with what you genuinely respect. “You kept all of us healthy through some very hard years” is not flattery if it is true. It reminds the person that their worth does not depend on being right about this one thing.

People defend beliefs hardest when the belief feels like part of who they are. Making clear that you respect the person makes it easier for them to reconsider the idea.

Listen first, and properly

Most of us, when we disagree, listen only long enough to prepare our reply. Research suggests that real listening is not just polite. It changes how the speaker thinks.

In four experiments, the psychologists Guy Itzchakov, Avraham Kluger and Dotan Castro had people talk about their views on a topic to a listener who was either attentive and non-judgmental or distracted. People who were listened to well felt less anxious, became more aware of the different sides of their own views, and held less extreme attitudes, without anyone arguing with them. Feeling heard seems to lower the need to defend.

Listening also builds respect in the other direction. A 2021 study led by Emily Kubin and Kurt Gray found that in moral and political disagreements, people respected opponents more when they shared personal experiences, especially of harm, than when they offered facts. That study was about respect, not persuasion. But respect is what keeps the door open for the next conversation.

Field experiments on what is called “deep canvassing” show the two working together. In 2016, the political scientists David Broockman and Joshua Kalla reported that a single conversation of about ten minutes, in which canvassers encouraged people to actively take another person’s perspective, reduced prejudice towards a minority group, and the effect lasted at least three months. In later experiments built on the same approach, they found that a non-judgmental, two-way exchange of personal stories worked, while a version that delivered only facts and arguments did not.

Ask, and let them discover the gap

Telling someone they are wrong invites them to defend themselves. Asking them how something works invites them to think.

In a 2013 study, Philip Fernbach and colleagues asked people with strong opinions on policy questions to explain, step by step, how a particular policy would actually work. After trying, people rated their own understanding lower and their opinions became more moderate. Simply listing their reasons for a view had no such effect. The researchers called the underlying trap the illusion of explanatory depth: we often feel we understand things far better than we do, until we try to explain them.

Used kindly, a question like “How do you think it works?” or “Where did you first hear about this?” lets a person notice a gap for themselves, which is far less humiliating than having it pointed out. Used as a trap, it will be recognised as one. The aim is shared curiosity, not a cross-examination. And sometimes the answer will show that the belief has a sensible root you had not considered.

Speak to their values, not only yours

When we argue, we tend to use the reasons that persuade us. The sociologist Robb Willer and the psychologist Matthew Feinberg found that people trying to persuade political opponents mostly used arguments based on their own moral values, and that arguments became more persuasive when they were reframed in terms of the values the other side cared about. They call this moral reframing.

In a family, the shared values are often obvious: protecting the children, caring for the elderly, honouring tradition, not wasting money. A concern about a remedy might land better as “I want to make sure nothing interferes with your medicines, because we need you around” than as “that has no scientific evidence”.

Watch how you phrase it

Small choices of words signal whether you are open or closed. In a 2020 study, the behavioural scientists Michael Yeomans, Julia Minson and colleagues identified features of what they called conversational receptiveness, including acknowledging the other person’s view (“I understand why…”), presenting your own claims as your view (“I think…”) and softening certainty (“sometimes”, “in many cases”). People whose writing was more receptive were seen as better collaborators and their messages as more persuasive. Among Wikipedia editors, those who wrote more receptive posts early in a disagreement later received fewer personal attacks.

Receptive language is not the same as agreeing. You can say “I see why that makes sense to you, and I think the evidence points the other way” and still be completely honest.

When you do correct, give the truth a structure

The authors of the Debunking Handbook recommend a simple order for correcting a false claim:

  • Start with the fact, stated simply.
  • Mention the myth once, with a warning that it is not accurate.
  • Explain why it is wrong and why it seemed plausible, and offer a better explanation to fill the gap.
  • End with the fact again, so it is the last thing remembered.

For example: “Cracking your knuckles doesn’t seem to cause arthritis. People often warn that it does, and the loud crack does make it sound harmful. But the sound comes from a gas bubble in the joint fluid, and studies comparing knuckle-crackers with non-crackers haven’t found more arthritis. So it may annoy people, but it doesn’t seem to damage the joints.” (See Does Cracking Your Knuckles Cause Arthritis?)

The handbook also stresses who delivers the message: people tend to accept corrections from sources they trust. In a family, that may not be you. A trusted doctor, a respected relative or a source the person already reads may be more persuasive than you can be, and that is fine.

Lessons from the clinic

Health workers face this problem daily, and many use an approach called motivational interviewing. Instead of arguing against hesitancy, the practitioner listens, asks open questions, reflects back what the person says, and helps them voice their own reasons for change. In a study in Quebec, new mothers in maternity wards received a 15–20 minute motivational-interviewing conversation about their babies’ vaccinations. Vaccination coverage at seven months was modestly higher in the group that received the conversation than in a comparison group. The study was not fully randomised, so the result needs confirming, but the approach shows what respectful persuasion can look like in practice.

Respect has a second half: be willing to be wrong

Questioning a belief respectfully is not a technique for winning. It works best when you are genuinely open to learning something. Many beliefs passed down through families carry real wisdom, sometimes in an odd form. Others are wrong but harmless, and not worth a battle. Choosing which beliefs to question is part of respect.

There are limits, though. When a belief could cause real harm, such as stopping a prescribed medicine or delaying care for a serious symptom, clarity matters more than comfort. Kindness and firmness are not opposites. You can say, “I love you, and I’m worried about this, so I need to be honest,” and then say it.

Finally, expect change to be slow. People rarely change their minds in the middle of a conversation; they change them afterwards, quietly, when they no longer feel watched. A conversation that leaves the other person feeling respected and a little curious has done its job, even if they end it by disagreeing with you.

The goal is not to win the argument at the table. It is to leave the person feeling respected and a little more curious, so they can reconsider later, in their own time.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Corrections rarely make people believe falsehoods more strongly; on average they move beliefs towards the facts.
  • People evaluate challenging evidence more fairly when they feel less threatened.
  • Explaining how something works, rather than listing reasons for a view, tends to reduce overconfidence.
  • Clear corrections that offer an alternative explanation work better than simple denials.

Still uncertain

  • How well these findings, mostly from studies of strangers and political topics in Western countries, apply to conversations within families and across generations in other cultures.
  • How long changes in belief last after a single conversation, and how much repeated conversations add.
  • Whether the effects of listening and self-affirmation are large enough to matter for deeply held, identity-defining beliefs.
  • Which approach works best for which kind of belief and which kind of person.
