Why We Abandon New Habits After a Few Days

We start with energy and quit within the week. The problem is rarely laziness. It is that the first days of a habit are the hardest, least rewarding stretch, and most of us were told it would be over far sooner.

10 min read·13 December 2026

You buy the running shoes. You download the language app. You decide that from Monday you will wake at six, write in a journal and drink more water. For three days it goes well. On the fourth you are tired, on the fifth you skip, and by the second week the shoes are back in the cupboard.

This pattern is so common that businesses build their pricing around it. In a well-known study of American gyms, the economists Stefano DellaVigna and Ulrike Malmendier found that members on monthly contracts went about four times a month, paying an average of about $17 per visit when a ten-visit pass would have cost $10 a visit. Around 80% would have saved money by paying as they went. They signed up for the person they planned to become, and paid for the person they were.

Why do new habits collapse so early? The research points to a handful of predictable traps. None of them is a character flaw, and most of them can be planned for.

Trap 1: The burst of motivation is real, and temporary

New habits usually begin at a moment that feels like a fresh start: a Monday, the first of the month, a birthday, New Year. The behavioural scientists Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis called this the fresh start effect. Studying gym attendance at a university, they found that the chance of a student going to the gym was about 33% higher at the start of a week, 47% higher at the start of a semester and about 12% higher at the start of a year than on an ordinary day. They suggested that such landmarks let us draw a line under our past failures and feel like a new person.

But in their data the boost faded as each week, month and semester went on. The fresh start is a good launchpad. It is not fuel for the whole journey. A plan that only works on the energy of day one is a plan that will fail on day five.

Trap 2: The 21-day myth sets the wrong finish line

Many people start a habit believing it will become automatic in 21 days. That figure traces back to Psycho-Cybernetics, a 1960 self-help book by the plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz. He observed that his patients seemed to take a minimum of about 21 days to get used to a changed appearance. As the idea was retold, “minimum” fell away and an anecdote became a rule.

When researchers actually measured it, the timeline was longer and far more variable. In a 2010 study, the health psychologist Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London asked volunteers to choose a new daily behaviour, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or going for a run before dinner, and to rate each day how automatic it felt. For the 82 people who completed the 12-week study, the typical time for the behaviour to reach its peak level of automaticity was about 66 days. Individual times ranged from 18 days to an estimated 254 days, and exercise habits took about one and a half times as long as eating or drinking habits. About half the participants had not reached a firm habit by the end of the study.

A 2024 systematic review of health-habit studies found a similar picture: in the studies that measured it, median times ranged from about 59 to 66 days, with individual times anywhere from 4 to 335 days. And in a 2015 study of 111 new gym members, the psychologists Navin Kaushal and Ryan Rhodes found that people needed to exercise at least four times a week for about six weeks before it became habitual, and only about half got there.

This matters for quitting. If you expect the new behaviour to feel easy by week three, then week three, when it still feels like effort, looks like proof that you are failing. In reality you are right on schedule.

A new habit usually takes around two months to feel automatic, often longer, and the time varies hugely from person to person. Feeling that it is still hard after a week or three is normal, not a sign of failure.

Trap 3: The early days are all cost and little reward

The benefits most new habits promise, such as fitness, fluency or savings, arrive slowly. The costs, such as the early alarm, the sore legs or the boring vocabulary drill, arrive immediately. In the first week you are paying in full and receiving almost nothing.

The psychologists Kaitlin Woolley and Ayelet Fishbach have shown how much this matters. Across several studies, including one of New Year’s resolutions, they found that immediate rewards, such as enjoying the activity itself, predicted whether people kept going better than the delayed rewards they said they were aiming for, such as better health. In a related experiment at a gym, people who chose their exercises for enjoyment rather than for usefulness did more sets. Long-term benefits help us decide to start. Short-term rewards are what keep us going.

This fits what is known about how habits form. In a major review, the psychologists Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger describe habits as links between a situation and an action, built up through repetition and strengthened by reward. Early on, a little reward helps stamp in the link. Later, once the habit is established, it runs with much less need for reward or motivation. The trouble is that the reward is most needed at exactly the point where most new habits offer the least.

Trap 4: One missed day feels like the end

Many new habits do not fade gradually. They break at the first miss. A busy day interrupts the streak, and the thought follows: well, that’s ruined, I may as well stop. Psychologists studying dieting have given this pattern a memorable name, the “what-the-hell effect”: once a rule is broken, people often abandon it completely rather than simply resuming.

The evidence suggests a single miss matters far less than it feels. In Lally’s study, missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not noticeably affect how automatic it eventually became. What did lower the final level of habit was missing many times.

The most striking support comes from a 2021 “megastudy” led by Katherine Milkman, in which 30 scientists tested 54 different four-week programmes to encourage exercise among more than 61,000 gym members. Fewer than half of the programmes significantly increased gym visits, and experts were poor at predicting which ones would work. The most effective was a programme that offered small rewards for coming back after a missed workout. Getting back on track after a slip turned out to matter more than never slipping. Sobering too: only 8% of the programmes produced changes that could still be measured once the four weeks were over.

The lesson is to plan for the miss rather than to hope it never comes. An all-or-nothing approach turns every ordinary bad day into a reason to quit (see The Myth of the Perfect Routine).

Missing once barely affects a habit. Quitting after missing once is what ends it. The most important skill is coming back the next day.

Trap 5: The habit has no fixed place in the day

Habits are triggered by cues: a time, a place, something you were just doing. That is why “exercise more” rarely becomes a habit, while “walk for ten minutes after lunch” sometimes does. In Lally’s study, each behaviour was tied to a cue in the person’s daily routine. In Kaushal and Rhodes’s gym study, consistency, a simple routine and a pleasant setting all predicted whether exercise became a habit. And the 2024 review found that habits people chose for themselves, and habits done in the morning, tended to become stronger.

A study of students who moved to a new university shows the same principle in reverse. The psychologist Wendy Wood and colleagues found that students’ habits of exercising, reading the newspaper or watching television survived the move when the setting stayed similar, for example exercising in the same kind of place or with the same people. When the setting changed, even strong habits were disrupted, and behaviour fell back on the students’ conscious intentions. A new habit with no stable cue has to be started by willpower every single time, which is exactly the resource that fades after the first few days.

Trap 6: The goal is framed as something to avoid

How a goal is phrased may also matter. In a 2020 experiment with more than 1,000 people in Sweden, those whose New Year’s resolutions were framed as something to move towards were more likely to report success after a year than those whose resolutions were framed as something to avoid (think “start walking to work” rather than “stop taking the car”): about 59% against 47%. Interestingly, people who received a little extra support, such as naming someone to support them, did best, while those given the most detailed goal-setting programme did no better than those who got the least. The authors suggested that very specific, time-bound targets may simply create more chances to feel that one has failed.

One caution: this study relied on people’s own reports of success, and its participants chose to take part, so the true success rates are probably lower. But it is a useful reminder that adding more rules is not always better.

None of this means trying is pointless. In an older study of New Year’s resolutions, the psychologist John Norcross and colleagues followed people who made a resolution and people who wanted to change but had not formally resolved to. After six months, 46% of the resolvers had been continuously successful, compared with just 4% of the others. Starting deliberately helps. It is how we handle the weeks after the start that decides most outcomes.

What the evidence suggests

Putting these findings together gives a practical approach to the first fragile weeks:

  • Expect it to take months, not weeks. Judge progress against a realistic timeline of two months or more, not 21 days.
  • Start smaller than feels necessary. Simple behaviours become habits more easily, and a small habit done daily beats an ambitious one done twice.
  • Attach it to a cue. Same time, same place, or straight after something you already do every day.
  • Make it rewarding now. Choose the form of exercise you enjoy, or pair the habit with something pleasant, rather than relying on benefits months away.
  • Plan for the miss. Decide in advance that a skipped day means “start again tomorrow”, never “start again next month”.
  • Use fresh starts, but do not depend on them. A new week or month is a good time to begin, but the plan has to survive day five.
  • Frame it as something to do. “Walk after dinner” is easier to hold onto than “stop sitting so much”.

Many of these ideas, such as planning exactly when and where you will act, connect to a wider body of research on why motivation alone is a weak foundation for change (see Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy).

The first few days of a new habit are not a test of character. They are the steepest part of a long climb, made steeper by the myth that the top is only three weeks away. Knowing what the climb really looks like is half of what it takes to keep going.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The popular “21 days” rule has no scientific basis; studies that measure habit formation find typical times of around two months, with very wide variation.
  • Habits form through repetition in a consistent context, and are triggered by cues rather than by deliberate decisions.
  • Motivation rises at fresh-start moments such as a new week or year, then fades.
  • Missing a single day has little effect on habit formation; repeated misses do.
  • Most short programmes to change exercise behaviour have small effects that fade once the programme ends.

Still uncertain

  • How long habits take for different kinds of behaviour, and for different people; most studies are small, short and rely on self-report.
  • Whether techniques that help in experiments, such as rewards for returning after a miss, produce lasting change in everyday life.
  • How much goal framing (approach versus avoidance) matters outside self-selected study groups.
  • Why some people form habits in weeks and others take most of a year.
