Who Said So?

Period Myths: What Our Elders Swore By, and What Holds Up

Some period myths are harmless nonsense. Some have caused real restriction and shame. A few turn out to be partly true, which is exactly what makes them so hard to shake.

7 min read·5 November 2026

Don’t enter the kitchen. Don’t touch the pickle jar, or it will spoil. Don’t visit the temple. Don’t wash your hair. Don’t eat curd or anything sour. Don’t run, don’t swim, don’t sit with everyone else. Many girls in India, and around the world, grow up hearing some version of this list, usually from people who love them and sincerely believe it.

The rules are far from rare. A 2016 review pooled 138 studies covering about 97,000 adolescent girls in India. On average, 77% faced religious restrictions during their periods, about half were told not to touch certain people or foods, and around a quarter slept apart from the rest of the family. Only about half knew what menstruation was before their first period arrived, and roughly one in four missed school during their periods.

So which of these beliefs hold up? Some are pure myth. Some have done real harm. And a few contain a grain of truth, which is often why they last.

“Menstrual blood is impure, even toxic”

Verdict: myth. Menstrual fluid is blood mixed with the lining of the uterus, which builds up each month in case of pregnancy and is shed when no pregnancy happens. It is not poisonous, and it does not contaminate people, food or places.

Modern science once lent this belief a hand. Around 1919, the paediatrician Béla Schick reported that flowers handled by a woman on her period wilted, and proposed that menstruating women released a substance he called “menotoxin”. For decades, researchers tried to find it in sweat, blood and breast milk. The anthropologist Kate Clancy has described that work as resting largely on anecdotes and poorly controlled experiments, with contrary findings brushed aside. The idea eventually faded away.

The same goes for the pickle jar. Pickles spoil because of moisture and microbes getting in, not because of who opens the lid. There is no good evidence that curd, sour foods or cold water harm a menstruating body either.

Beliefs about impurity are not always harmless. In parts of Nepal, the practice of chhaupadi sends women to sleep in sheds or huts during their periods. Women and girls have died there from snakebites and from smoke inhaled while lighting fires for warmth. Nepal’s Supreme Court ruled against the practice in 2005, and in 2017 the country made forcing a woman into it a crime.

Some families explain the kitchen rule differently: as a way of giving women a few days off from heavy household work. Whatever the original reason, rest can be offered without shame attached to it.

“Don’t bathe or wash your hair”

Verdict: myth, and the opposite is closer to the truth. No good evidence shows that bathing or washing hair during a period causes harm. Hygiene matters more during menstruation, not less. In a study of 486 women in Odisha, those who used reusable cloth pads had more than twice the odds of urogenital infection symptoms as those using disposable pads. Women with more space for personal hygiene at home were less likely to have bacterial vaginosis, a common vaginal infection. Reusable products are not inherently unsafe; the findings may reflect how hard it can be to wash, dry and store cloths properly without enough water, privacy or sunlight. (Our elders were right about the value of a daily wash, as an earlier piece explored: see Do We Really Need to Bathe Every Day?)

“Don’t exercise; just rest”

Verdict: mostly myth, with some good instincts. Exercise during a period is safe, and may even help. A 2019 Cochrane review of nine small trials, including 632 women, found that regular exercise may substantially reduce period pain. The evidence was rated low quality, because the trials were small and varied, but it gives no reason to avoid movement.

Here is where the elders partly win. The hot water bottle that grandmothers press into your hands has real support. A 2019 review of self-care treatments found that applying heat produced a meaningful reduction in period pain, and in some trials it compared well with common painkillers. Resting when you feel unwell is also perfectly reasonable. The mistake is turning rest into a rule.

The useful parts of old period advice are about comfort: warmth, rest when needed and care. The harmful parts are about shame and exclusion.

“Women who live together get their periods together”

Verdict: probably a myth. This idea comes from a real study. In 1971, Martha McClintock reported in Nature that the periods of close friends living in a college dormitory seemed to drift closer together over the year. Later researchers struggled to repeat this. Studies following women in dormitories in Poland and in China, over months, found no synchrony at all.

The explanation is mostly arithmetic. If cycles last around four weeks and periods last about five days, two people’s periods will often overlap by chance. Because cycle lengths differ, periods drift together and then apart again. A short stretch of overlap is memorable, while the months they didn’t line up are forgotten (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller).

“Tampons and cups take away your virginity”

Verdict: myth. Virginity is about sexual experience, not a piece of tissue. The hymen, a thin membrane at the vaginal opening, varies greatly from person to person and has no known biological function. Doctors writing in Reproductive Health in 2019 concluded that examining it cannot reliably reveal whether someone has had sex. The World Health Organization has condemned so-called virginity testing as a violation of human rights. Clinicians at Boston Children’s Hospital advise that people who have never had sex can use tampons, and that doing so doesn’t change whether someone is a virgin.

Menstrual cups are a newer option in India. A 2019 review of 43 studies covering about 3,300 women and girls found they leaked no more than pads or tampons and appeared safe, though much of the evidence was of low quality.

There is one partly true worry. Tampons are linked to toxic shock syndrome, a rare but serious bacterial illness. It affects roughly 1 to 17 in every 100,000 menstruating people each year. Using the lowest absorbency you need and changing tampons at least every four to eight hours keeps the risk low.

“You can’t get pregnant on your period”

Verdict: partly true, but unreliable. Pregnancy is less likely during a period, but not impossible. In a well-known study that tracked 221 women’s hormones across nearly 700 cycles, less than 1% of women were in their fertile window on day 2 of their cycle and about 2% on day 4. By day 7, it was about 17%. Only around 30% of women had their whole fertile window within the “textbook” days 10 to 17. Sperm can survive for several days, and ovulation can come early, especially in shorter cycles. A period is not a reliable form of contraception.

“PMS is all in your head”

Verdict: myth. Changes in mood, energy and comfort in the days before a period are common and real. For a small group, they are severe enough to be a recognised medical condition: premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), which can cause intense depression, anxiety or irritability. A 2024 review of 44 studies with more than 50,000 participants estimated that about 1.6% of women and girls meet strict criteria for PMDD, and around 3.2% have a provisional diagnosis. It is treatable, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed.

Why these myths are so hard to shake

Silence keeps myths alive. When a subject is treated as shameful, people don’t ask questions, and wrong ideas are passed on unchallenged. Some myths also survive because they contain something sensible, like rest and warmth, wrapped inside something harmful, like exclusion. Others survive because we remember the times they seemed to come true.

The better approach is to keep the care and drop the shame. And some things are never “just part of being a woman”: period pain severe enough to stop daily life, very heavy bleeding, or periods that suddenly change are worth discussing with a doctor.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Menstrual fluid is blood and uterine lining. It is not toxic or contaminating.
  • Tampons and menstrual cups do not affect virginity, and the hymen cannot reveal someone’s sexual history.
  • Pregnancy is possible after sex during a period, though less likely in its first few days.
  • PMDD is a recognised condition affecting a small minority of women and girls.
  • Menstrual restrictions are widespread among adolescent girls in India, and many miss school during their periods.

Still uncertain

  • How much exercise and heat help compared with medicines; the trials are small and hard to blind.
  • Whether any form of menstrual synchrony exists; most careful studies find none.
  • Which reusable products and washing practices are safest in homes with limited water and privacy.
  • How best to change deeply held beliefs about menstruation within families and communities.
