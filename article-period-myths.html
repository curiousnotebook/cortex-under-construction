Who Said So?

Period Myths: What Our Elders Swore By, and What Holds Up

Some period myths are harmless nonsense. Some have caused real restriction and shame. A few turn out to be partly true, which is what makes them hard to shake.

7 min read·5 November 2026

Ask around and you will hear a familiar list. Don’t wash your hair during your period. Don’t go swimming. Don’t pickle anything. Your cycle will sync with your roommates. Tampons will take away your virginity. Most of these beliefs have been passed down with confidence and very little evidence. Some are merely odd. Others have restricted what women could do, eat and say for centuries. Looking at them one by one shows how beliefs about the body can outlast the reasons that created them.

Synchronised cycles: the most famous myth

The idea that women who live together gradually have their periods at the same time is widely believed, and it has a respectable origin. In 1971, Martha McClintock published a study in Nature of 135 students living together in a college dormitory. She reported that their menstrual onsets moved closer together over the months she tracked them, and she suggested that chemical signals, pheromones, might be responsible.

The study became famous, and it was hard to argue with a good story. Critics quickly pointed to problems. H. Clyde Wilson published reviews identifying statistical issues in how synchrony was measured, including the way initial differences in cycle timing were calculated and how data were selected. Other researchers who used McClintock’s methods reported negative results. In one study of 132 sorority members and their roommates, and another of 24 women in a cooperative house, Wilson and colleagues did not find the effect.

Reviews in 2006 and 2013 concluded that the evidence for menstrual synchrony in humans is weak. Part of the illusion is mathematical. Cycles vary in length, and when people are compared at random, some pairs will have onsets close together for ordinary reasons. Remembered anecdotes do the rest. A more recent app-based study reported in news coverage has also found no evidence that living in proximity changes cycle timing, though that work should be checked in its original publication before being cited.

It is easy to notice the times your periods line up, and hard to notice the many times they do not.

Exercise, swimming and the pool

Many people still believe they should rest, avoid sport or stay out of water during a period. The evidence points the other way for most people. Exercise is generally safe during menstruation, and regular activity is associated with less menstrual pain in several studies, though the effect varies. Moderate exercise can also help mood. Heavy bleeding, severe cramps or certain medical conditions are reasons to speak to a doctor, and people who feel unwell should rest. But “you must not exercise” is not a medical rule.

Swimming follows the same pattern. Period blood does not stop swimming, and a tampon or menstrual cup prevents leaks in water. The idea that menstrual blood attracts sharks or bears has no evidence behind it, and the idea that swimming stops a period is simply untrue.

Tampons, virginity and the hymen

The belief that using a tampon removes virginity rests on a misunderstanding of the hymen. The hymen is a thin ring of tissue at the vaginal opening, and it is variable in shape and elasticity. It can stretch or tear for many reasons, including exercise and everyday activity. It is not a reliable marker of sexual history, and the idea of virginity as a physical state is a social concept rather than a medical one. A tampon may or may not stretch the hymen, but nothing about a tampon decides whether someone has had sex. The myth persists because it links a sanitary product to a moral judgment about young women, not because of anything in the anatomy.

Pregnancy, period sex and the clock

Some people believe that sex during a period cannot lead to pregnancy. This is less safe than it sounds. Pregnancy depends on ovulation, and ovulation timing varies, especially for people with irregular cycles. Sperm can survive in the reproductive tract for several days, so intercourse near the end of a period may be followed by ovulation shortly after. Research on the timing of intercourse relative to ovulation has shown that fertile days cluster around ovulation, and that the window is not limited to a fixed part of the cycle. The lesson is not that period sex is dangerous. It is that a cycle is not a calendar you can rely on without tracking.

Dirty blood and menotoxins

Perhaps the oldest belief is that menstrual blood is dirty, toxic or unlucky. Ancient writers claimed it could spoil crops, sour wine or harm animals. In the twentieth century, some scientists proposed that menstruating women released a chemical that could wilt plants. These claims did not survive testing, and modern medicine treats menstrual blood as ordinary blood from the lining of the uterus, shed in the normal way. There is nothing in its composition that makes it unclean in a medical sense.

The myth matters because of what it allowed. Anthropologists Thomas Buckley and Alma Gottlieb reviewed menstrual practices across cultures and concluded that taboos are nearly universal, but that they carry very different meanings. In some communities, seclusion during menstruation offers rest and freedom from ordinary duties. In others, it means exclusion from food preparation, religious life and social contact. The belief that the body is impure at these times is a social idea, not a physiological fact, and it has real consequences for women’s freedom and health.

PMS and PMDD: real, but less common than the jokes

The idea that premenstrual symptoms are imaginary is also wrong. Many people experience changes in mood, energy, appetite or sleep in the days before a period, and some find these changes disruptive. A severe form, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, is recognised in the DSM-5 diagnostic manual.

The numbers depend on how the condition is defined. A 2024 meta-analysis of 44 studies, covering more than 50,000 participants, found that PMDD had a pooled prevalence of about 3.2 per cent when diagnosed with prospective confirmation over two cycles, but about 7.7 per cent when diagnosed provisionally. The authors argued that provisional diagnosis tends to inflate prevalence. Estimates of PMS in some regional reviews are much higher, often because of looser definitions. The honest answer is that symptoms are common, a severe disorder is much less common, and the difference matters for treatment.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Cycle synchrony between women who live together is not well supported by the evidence, and the original 1971 study has been widely criticised.
  • Exercise and swimming are generally safe during menstruation for most people, and regular activity is linked to less pain in several studies.
  • The hymen does not determine virginity, and a tampon does not remove it.
  • Menstrual blood is not toxic or dirty in a medical sense.

Still uncertain

  • Whether any human pheromone effect on cycles exists, given limited and contested research.
  • The true prevalence of PMS and PMDD across populations, which depends heavily on how the conditions are diagnosed.
  • How much cultural restriction around menstruation affects women’s health over time, which has been studied less than it should be.

Why the myths last

Period myths survive for three reasons. They are passed on by family and friends, who mean well. They are reinforced by the embarrassment that makes periods hard to discuss. And some of them are not entirely false: exercise can feel hard when you are in pain, and some people do feel more tired or irritable at certain times. A kernel of experience can be stretched into a rule. The solution is not to dismiss what older relatives say, but to ask what they have seen, what they assume, and what the evidence shows.
