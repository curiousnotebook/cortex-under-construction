Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy

Waiting to feel like it is one of the most common plans people make, and one of the least dependable. The research points to something more practical.

6 min read·30 October 2026

Most of us have made some version of the same promise. Next month, once work calms down, once I feel more energetic, I will start exercising, writing or studying properly. The plan rests on a quiet assumption: that the feeling of wanting to do something will arrive first, and the doing will follow. Psychologists have studied that assumption for decades, and it does not hold up well. Motivation is real and useful, but as a switch to wait for, it is unreliable.

The gap between meaning it and doing it

In 2016, Paschal Sheeran and Thomas Webb reviewed the research on what happens between intending to act and actually acting. Their summary is blunt: people often do not do the things they intend to do. Their review drew on a large body of meta-analyses, and the pattern was consistent. A strong intention is a useful predictor of behaviour, but it explains only part of what people end up doing. Many intentions never become action.

The gap has several sources. Intentions can be overwhelmed by competing goals. Some tasks are harder than they look. Situations change, and the cue that was supposed to prompt the action disappears. And an intention stated in general terms, such as "I want to be more active," does not say when, where or how the action will happen. The same review found that when people face obstacles they did not anticipate, a vague intention offers little help.

Feeling motivated is not the same as having a plan that works when motivation is absent.

Planning the moment, not the mood

One of the more practical responses to this gap comes from Peter Gollwitzer's research on what he calls implementation intentions. These are if-then plans: "If it is 7 a.m. and I am in the kitchen, then I will put on my running shoes." The plan specifies the situation and the action in advance, so that the cue, rather than a fluctuating feeling, does the prompting.

In 2006, Gollwitzer and Sheeran combined 94 independent tests of this idea. Forming an implementation intention had a positive, medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, with an average effect size of 0.65. The effect appeared across several kinds of goals, including health, academic work and interpersonal conduct. The authors also found that these plans helped people start acting, protect their goals from distractions, and disengage from strategies that were not working.

Two cautions are worth keeping in mind. An effect size is not a promise that a plan will work for you, and the strength of the effect depends on the kind of plan and the person's existing commitment to the goal. Still, the central finding is useful: specifying the moment of action does more than hoping to feel ready when it arrives.

When rewards replace the reason

Motivation can also be undermined by the wrong kind of help. In 1999, Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan published a meta-analysis of 128 experiments examining what happens when people are given external rewards for activities they already found interesting. Tangible rewards, paid for doing something or completing it, were associated with a drop in later free-choice engagement with the activity. The effect was small to moderate, but it was consistent across several types of reward.

The finding has been disputed. Other reviewers have argued that the effect depends heavily on how rewards are offered and how the studies were coded, and that for tasks people find dull, rewards can increase effort. Deci, Koestner and Ryan's own explanation is that rewards can change how people see their reasons for acting, shifting the sense of choosing the activity to a sense of doing it for the payment. For our purposes, the lesson is modest: a reward can change what an activity means to you, and that is not always what you wanted.

The habit that does not need a mood

A third route is habit. Habits are context-response associations that form when an action is repeated in a stable setting. Once a habit is established, the setting itself tends to trigger the action, and the trigger does not depend strongly on how you feel that day. A 2024 review in Current Directions in Psychological Science argued that changing intentions has limited effect on habit memory. Habit change works better through altering the context, adding friction to the old behaviour and making the new one easier, along with rewards that help a new routine form.

A 2026 review by Jan Voelkel, Katherine Milkman and Angela Duckworth in the Annual Review of Psychology frames behaviour change as three phases: building motivation, following through, and forming durable habits. The authors' point is that motivation is one part of the process, and that the later phases depend on different tools. Their emphasis on randomised trials with objective outcomes is a useful reminder that many popular techniques have far less evidence behind them than their confidence suggests.

Here, the research is clearer about what does not work than about what works best. Habits take time to form, and they are easier to build when the new behaviour is attached to an existing routine. But the direction is consistent: design the environment and the next action, rather than relying on the feeling of readiness.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Strong intentions often fail to become action, and the gap is large across many domains.
  • Specifying when, where and how to act improves goal attainment on average, across many studies.
  • Habits, once formed, are triggered by context more than by momentary motivation.

Still uncertain

  • How much rewards undermine interest in everyday life is disputed, and depends on how rewards are designed and which tasks are involved.
  • How long implementation intentions and habit effects last outside the laboratory, and for people with the most difficult goals, is less well mapped.
  • Whether motivation can be built by acting first, as popular advice suggests, is plausible in some settings but not yet tested widely enough to be a general rule.

What this means for everyday life

None of this means motivation is irrelevant. It helps people begin, and it can grow as progress is made. The problem is treating it as a precondition. If your plan depends on feeling ready, the plan will fail on the days you feel least ready, which are often the days that matter most.

A more reliable approach has three parts. First, decide the specific situation and action in advance, in the form "if this happens, I will do that." Second, make the action easy and the old alternative slightly harder. Third, repeat the action in the same context until the context itself starts to carry the behaviour. None of these depends on how you feel that morning.
