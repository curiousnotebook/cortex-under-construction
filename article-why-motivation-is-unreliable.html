Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy

Motivation is a wonderful feeling and a poor plan. The research on how people actually change suggests we should stop waiting for it and start building around it.

8 min read·30 October 2026

Every January, something predictable happens. In the United States, Google searches for the word “diet” jump by more than 80% at the start of a new year compared with an ordinary day. Students at one university gym were more likely to show up at the start of each week, each month and, most of all, each new semester. Researchers Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis called this the fresh start effect: new beginnings on the calendar make us feel ready to become better versions of ourselves.

Most of us know how the story usually ends. The feeling that carried us into the gym on the first Monday is gone by the third. The problem is not that we lack motivation. It is that motivation is a feeling, and feelings come and go on their own schedule. A plan that depends on feeling a certain way is a plan that will fail on the days we feel differently.

The gap between wanting and doing

Psychologists have a name for the distance between what we intend and what we do: the intention–behaviour gap. In a review of the research, Paschal Sheeran and Thomas Webb concluded that intentions are translated into action only about half the time. Most of the gap comes from people who sincerely mean to act and then don’t.

The most direct test comes from experiments that deliberately try to make people more motivated. In 2006, Webb and Sheeran pooled 47 such experiments. The interventions worked on motivation: on average they produced a medium-to-large increase in how strongly people intended to act. But the change in what people actually did was only about half as large. Making someone want something more is real progress. It is just not the same as getting them to do it.

Why motivation makes poor fuel

Motivation tends to be strongest when we are planning, and weakest at the moment of action. Deciding to exercise happens on a Sunday evening when we feel hopeful. Actually exercising happens on a Wednesday morning when we are tired, it is raining and the bed is warm. The person who made the plan and the person who has to carry it out are, in a sense, in different moods.

For years, a popular theory explained this with a battery: willpower was thought to be a limited resource that drains with use, an idea called ego depletion. In 2016, 23 laboratories ran the same preregistered experiment with more than 2,000 participants to test it. The overall effect was close to zero. The battery theory is now far less certain than it once seemed, and researchers still argue about it. But you do not need the theory to notice the everyday fact: relying on in-the-moment effort is unreliable.

What people with “good self-control” actually do

If willpower isn’t the secret, how do disciplined people manage? One answer came from a study by Wilhelm Hofmann and colleagues, who gave 205 adults beepers and asked them, several times a day for a week, about any desires they were feeling and whether they were trying to resist them. People who scored high in self-control did not win more of their battles with temptation. Instead, they reported weaker desires, less inner conflict, and needed to resist less often in the first place.

A second line of research suggests why. Across six studies involving more than 2,000 people, Brian Galla and Angela Duckworth found that people with high self-control tended to have stronger beneficial habits, for study, exercise, sleep and healthy eating. Those habits explained a large part of why self-control was linked to good outcomes such as better grades. The disciplined people were not white-knuckling their way through each day. Their good behaviour had become routine.

People who seem disciplined are often not resisting more. They have arranged their lives so that they need to resist less.

Plans beat feelings

One of the best-supported tools in behaviour-change research is surprisingly simple. Instead of a general goal (“I will exercise more”), you make an implementation intention: a specific plan for when, where and how you will act, often written as “if–then”. For example: “If it is 7 a.m. on Monday, Wednesday or Friday, then I will walk for 20 minutes before breakfast.”

In 2006, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined 94 tests of this idea and found a medium-to-large improvement in people reaching their goals, compared with simply intending to. One study shows the contrast especially clearly. Researchers in the UK gave 248 young adults information designed to motivate them to exercise. It worked on motivation: their intentions went up. But their actual exercise did not. Afterwards, only about 35 to 38% of people in the motivation-only and control groups exercised at least once. Among those who received the same information and also wrote down exactly when and where they would exercise, 91% did.

The plan works because it moves the decision. You no longer have to decide, in a moment of low motivation, whether to act. You decided earlier, and now the situation itself becomes the cue.

Habits: letting the situation do the work

A habit is what happens when a behaviour has been repeated so often in the same situation that the situation starts it for you. Brushing your teeth is not a daily act of willpower. In a diary study, Wendy Wood and colleagues found that about 43% of the everyday behaviours people recorded were done almost every day and usually in the same place, a sign that they were running largely on habit.

How long does building one take? You may have heard “21 days”. The best-known real-world study, by Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London, followed 96 volunteers who each chose a healthy behaviour to repeat daily. Among those whose progress could be clearly measured, the median time for the behaviour to feel close to automatic was 66 days, with a huge range from 18 to 254 days. Two other findings matter just as much. Only about half of the participants showed a clear plateau at all, so habit-building is slow and uneven. And missing a single day did not meaningfully set people back. One slip is not a reason to quit.

Rewards: useful, if used with care

Rewards can help get a new routine started. In a pair of experiments, economists Gary Charness and Uri Gneezy paid students to go to the gym several times over a month. Attendance stayed higher after the payments stopped, and the whole effect came from people who had not been regular gym-goers before. Another study tested temptation bundling: 226 people could listen to gripping audiobooks only while at the gym. At first they went 51% more often than a control group, though the effect faded after a holiday break interrupted the routine.

Rewards also carry a known risk. A large review led by Edward Deci found that expected, tangible rewards for doing something people already enjoy tend to reduce their interest in doing it for its own sake. Positive feedback, by contrast, tended to increase it. A sensible reading is that rewards are best used to start something you do not yet enjoy, not to replace enjoyment you already have. Rewards tied too closely to a single number can also backfire in other ways (see When a Measure Becomes a Target).

Even the best tricks fade

The most sobering evidence comes from a huge “megastudy” led by Katherine Milkman and published in 2021. Researchers tested 54 different four-week programmes, designed by 30 scientists, on more than 61,000 members of an American gym chain. About 45% of the programmes increased gym visits, by 9% to 27%. The best performer offered small rewards for returning to the gym after a missed workout. But only about 8% of the programmes produced changes that lasted after the four weeks ended. Experts asked to predict which programmes would work could not reliably pick the winners.

This does not mean nothing works. It means a burst of effort, however clever, is not the same as a lasting change. Strategies have to keep running after the initial enthusiasm is gone.

What to do instead of waiting to feel motivated

  • Decide in advance. Write down when and where you will act, as an if–then plan. Let the situation, not your mood, start the behaviour.
  • Attach it to something you already do. A habit forms more easily when the same cue comes before it every time, such as after morning tea or as soon as you get home.
  • Make it easier, not just more inspiring. Reduce the effort and the temptations around the behaviour. Disciplined people mostly avoid battles rather than winning them.
  • Plan for the missed day. A single slip barely affects habit-building. What matters is coming back quickly, which is exactly what the most successful megastudy programme rewarded.
  • Use rewards to start, not to replace interest. Pair a dull task with something you enjoy, and lean on encouragement more than prizes for things you already like doing.
  • Use fresh starts, but don’t trust them alone. A new month or a birthday is a good moment to begin. Make sure the plan is built to survive the second week.

A note of caution about advice itself: the most confident productivity tips often come from people for whom one method happened to work, while the many who tried the same thing and gave up are rarely heard from (see Survivorship Bias). And as with studying, what feels productive is not always what works (see Forgetting Is Not the Enemy).

Motivation is a welcome visitor. It just makes an unreliable employee. The people who keep going are rarely the ones who feel inspired every day. They are the ones who built a life where showing up doesn’t depend on it.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Intentions turn into action only part of the time, and increasing motivation produces a smaller change in behaviour than in intention.
  • Specific if–then plans improve follow-through across many studies and settings.
  • People high in self-control experience fewer temptations and rely more on habits than on resisting.
  • Habits form gradually through repetition in a stable situation, with wide differences between people.
  • Most short behaviour-change programmes lose much of their effect once they end.

Still uncertain

  • Whether willpower is a limited resource. The classic ego depletion effect failed a large replication, but the debate continues.
  • How long habits really take to form. The well-known 66-day figure comes from one study of fewer than 100 volunteers, and only about half showed a clear result.
  • When rewards build lasting habits and when they undermine interest; the answer seems to depend on the task, the person and the reward.
  • Which strategies keep working over months and years rather than weeks.
