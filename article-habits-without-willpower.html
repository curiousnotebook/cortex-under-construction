How to Build Habits Without Depending on Willpower

We treat willpower as the engine of good habits. Research suggests the people with the most self-control use it the least. What they do instead is quieter, and much easier to copy.

12 min read·31 December 2026

Tonight, millions of people will make a promise to themselves. Tomorrow they will wake early, skip the sweets, put the phone away and finally open that book. Most of these plans share a hidden assumption: that the person who keeps a good habit is the person who tries hardest, who grits their teeth through each temptation and wins.

It is an appealing picture, and it is mostly wrong. Over the past fifteen years, psychologists and economists have looked closely at how people with good habits actually live. The surprise is that they do not seem to resist temptation more often than the rest of us. They seem to meet it less often. Their secret is not a stronger will but a different strategy, and the strategy can be learned.

The people with the most self-control use it the least

In a 2012 study, researchers in Germany gave 205 adults smartphones that beeped at random moments through the day for a week. Each time, people reported whether they were feeling a desire, whether it clashed with their goals, and whether they tried to resist it. Across 7,827 reported desires, people felt some want or urge in about half of the moments sampled. About 42% of these desires were actively resisted.

The most interesting finding concerned people who scored high on a standard questionnaire measure of self-control. If self-control meant winning more battles, they should have been resisting more often. They were not. They reported fewer conflicted desires, weaker desires overall, and they resisted less often than people low in self-control. They were not better fighters. They simply had fewer fights.

A 2015 series of six studies with 2,274 participants, from teenagers to adults, pointed to one reason. People with high self-control had stronger beneficial habits: regular study routines, consistent sleep, healthy snacking, exercise. Those habits, rather than effortful resistance, largely explained why self-control predicted better outcomes, including school grades and staying on in college.

Self-control looks like resisting temptation, but in practice it mostly means arranging life so that temptation rarely gets a vote.

Is willpower a tank that runs dry?

For years, a popular theory held that willpower works like a muscle or a fuel tank. Use it on one task, the idea went, and you have less left for the next. This “ego depletion” effect appeared in hundreds of studies, and some even suggested a sugary drink could top up the tank.

Then came large, carefully pre-planned replications. In 2016, 23 laboratories tested the effect with 2,141 participants using an agreed protocol. The pooled effect was close to zero. In 2021, a second project across 36 laboratories and 3,531 participants, designed with the theory’s original supporters, again found no reliable effect in its main test. A 2016 analysis of the sugar studies concluded that their evidence was too weak to draw any conclusions about glucose and self-control.

The debate is not fully closed. Some researchers think tiredness and effort still matter under certain conditions, and the second project hinted at a slightly larger effect among people who reported feeling more fatigued. But for practical purposes, the lesson is the same either way. Whether willpower runs out or simply wavers, a plan that depends on winning the same fight every day is fragile.

What a habit really is

If willpower is unreliable, what drives everyday behaviour? A great deal of it is habit. In diary studies in which students noted what they were doing every hour, between a third and nearly half of their actions were things they did almost every day, usually in the same place. During these habitual actions, their thoughts were more often somewhere else entirely. Habits, in other words, run without much conscious supervision.

A habit is not just something done often. It is an action that has become linked to a cue: a place, a time, an object or the end of another action. Once the link is strong, the cue starts the action almost by itself, whether or not we currently want the result.

A memorable 2011 experiment showed how far this goes. Researchers gave cinema-goers either fresh popcorn or popcorn that was a week old. People who rarely ate popcorn at the cinema ate less of the stale batch, as you would expect. But people with a strong cinema-popcorn habit ate roughly the same amount whether it was fresh or stale, even though they said they disliked the stale popcorn. The habit was responding to the cinema, not to the taste.

Two small changes broke the spell. When the same popcorn was served in a meeting room while people watched music videos, habitual eaters suddenly noticed it was stale and ate less. And in a second study, asking people to eat with their non-dominant hand made some habitual eaters more sensitive to the taste. Change the cue, or make the routine slightly awkward, and the automatic pull weakens.

Habits are tied to situations more than to intentions. That is bad news for willpower, and good news for anyone willing to change their situation.

Strategy 1: Change the situation before temptation arrives

Psychologists Angela Duckworth, Tamar Gendler and James Gross have argued that self-control strategies can be sorted by when they act. A temptation tends to grow stronger as it develops: from the first sight of the sweet shop, to the smell, to the plate in front of you. Resisting at the last moment is the hardest place to fight. Strategies that act early, by choosing or changing the situation, stop the impulse before it builds. Their examples are simple: sit far from the drinks table, ask the waiter not to bring the dessert trolley, leave the phone at home when you go to the library.

Small changes to effort can have measurable effects. In a hospital cafeteria salad bar in the United States, moving a food about 25 centimetres further from customers, so they had to lean slightly further to reach it, reduced how much of it people took by roughly 9% to 13%. Serving a food with tongs instead of a spoon cut the amount taken by about 16%. Nobody was forbidden anything. The choice simply became a little less convenient.

The same logic works in both directions. Make the habits you want slightly easier: the walking shoes by the door, the book on the pillow, the water bottle on the desk. Make the habits you want to drop slightly harder: the phone charging outside the bedroom, the snacks in a high cupboard rather than on the counter. Each change is small, but it is paid once, rather than every day in willpower.

Strategy 2: Hitch the new habit to an old one

Since habits grow from cues, a new habit needs a reliable cue of its own. One of the most dependable cues is the end of something you already do every day.

A small British study tested this with dental floss. Fifty people who rarely flossed were asked to floss daily for four weeks. Half were told to floss just before brushing their teeth, and half just after. Eight months later, the after-brushing group had stronger habits and were flossing more often. The researchers suspected that finishing a well-established routine made a clearer trigger for the next step than starting it did. The study was small and exploratory, but it fits the wider pattern: a habit attached to a fixed point in the day has something to hold on to.

Consistency of the cue also matters for bigger habits. A 2023 study used machine learning on years of data from more than 30,000 gym members and over 3,000 hospital workers. Hand-washing at work became habitual within weeks, but going to the gym typically took several months. About two-thirds of gym-goers stuck to particular days of the week, and the longer the gap since someone’s last visit, the less likely they were to return. (Why new habits so often collapse in the first few days is the subject of Why We Abandon New Habits After a Few Days.)

Strategy 3: Make a deal with your future self

Sometimes we know in advance that our future self will waver. Economists call a tool for handling this a commitment device: a choice made today that makes it costly or impossible to give in tomorrow. Some of the clearest evidence comes from India.

In Mysore, economists worked with a data-entry firm employing 102 workers. Workers’ output rose as payday approached, a sign that motivation was stronger when the reward was close. When workers were offered contracts in which they set their own daily targets, and earned less if they missed them, they chose such targets about 28% of the time, even though these contracts could only reduce their pay. Offering the choice raised output by about 2% on average, and by about 9% among workers whose effort rose most sharply near payday. Self-set targets were rarely missed.

In Chennai, a study of 229 low-income rickshaw drivers offered some of them payments only on days when a breathalyser showed they were sober. When given a choice, more than a third preferred these smaller, sobriety-linked payments to larger payments with no conditions, effectively paying for help with their own behaviour. Daytime drinking fell by 13 percentage points, and savings rose by more than half, although some drinking simply moved to the evening.

Outside India, a bank in the Philippines offered smokers a savings account they would forfeit unless they passed a nicotine urine test after six months. Only about 11% of those offered it signed up, but the group offered the account was 3 to 6 percentage points more likely to pass the test, and the difference was still there after a year. In the United States, a 2022 study of about 2,000 smartphone users found that simply letting people set daily limits on their own app use cut use by 22 minutes a day, about 16%.

The pattern across these studies is consistent. Many people know their own weak spots, and given a way to tie their hands in advance, a good number will use it. The effects are usually modest, but they work without requiring anyone to win an argument with themselves in the moment.

Strategy 4: Make the good thing pleasant now

Another approach attaches an immediate pleasure to a task whose rewards come later. In a 2014 study called “Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym,” participants were given gripping audiobooks they could listen to only while exercising. At first they visited the gym 51% more often than a comparison group. The effect faded over the weeks, especially after a holiday break, but afterwards 61% of participants chose to pay for gym-only access to such audiobooks. They valued the trick enough to buy it.

The fading is an important warning. In 2021 a “megastudy” tested 54 different four-week programmes designed by 30 scientists to get 61,293 members of a US gym chain exercising more. About 45% of the programmes increased weekly visits, by 9% to 27%. The single best one paid small rewards for returning to the gym after a missed workout. But only 8% of the programmes produced changes still measurable after the four weeks ended. Short-term boosts are easy to produce. Lasting change usually needs the boost to be turned into a routine tied to a stable cue.

Strategy 5: Let defaults do the work

The most powerful willpower-free strategy is the one that requires no repeated decision at all. In a classic study at a large US company, when new employees had to sign up for a retirement savings plan themselves, 37% of recent hires had joined. When the company switched to enrolling them automatically, with the freedom to opt out, participation among comparable employees jumped to 86%. The plan was identical. Only the default changed.

We can set defaults for ourselves too: an automatic monthly transfer into savings on salary day, a recurring calendar block for exercise, a grocery list that simply does not include the snack aisle. A 2019 review of self-control strategies by Duckworth, the economist Katherine Milkman and David Laibson grouped such tools into those we impose on ourselves and those designed by others, such as employers and governments, and into those that change our situation versus those that change our thinking. Its central message was in its title: the most effective strategies go beyond willpower. A commentary published alongside it added a useful caution, noting that many of the supporting studies were small or short, so how well each strategy works at scale and over years is still being tested.

Putting it into practice

None of this means effort is pointless. Starting a habit takes some deliberate work, and plans still matter (see Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy). The aim is to spend that effort once, on design, instead of every day, on resistance.

  • Pick one habit and give it a fixed cue. “After I brush my teeth at night” is a better trigger than “sometime in the evening.”
  • Remove friction from the good habit and add friction to the bad one, before the moment of temptation.
  • Use a commitment if you know you will waver: a friend you have promised to meet, an app limit you cannot easily switch off, a target with a small cost attached.
  • Pair something you dread with something you enjoy, but expect the novelty to fade and use the time to build the routine.
  • Use life changes. A new job, a new home or a new term breaks old cues. An English study of 800 residents found an intervention to encourage greener habits worked better among people who had recently moved house, with a tentative sign that the window was strongest in the first three months.
  • Think in months, not weeks, and when you miss a day, return quickly. Long gaps, more than single slips, are what tend to end a habit.

Willpower is real, but it is better kept for emergencies than used as a daily strategy. The people who seem most disciplined have usually stopped relying on it. They let their surroundings, their routines and their earlier decisions do most of the work.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • A large share of everyday behaviour is habitual and triggered by familiar situations, often with little conscious thought.
  • People high in self-control report fewer and weaker temptations and rely more on habits than on constant resistance.
  • Changing the situation, such as making an option slightly harder to reach, can shift behaviour without any change in willpower.
  • Defaults have large effects on behaviour, as automatic enrolment in savings plans shows.
  • Many people will choose commitment devices when offered, and these usually produce modest improvements.

Still uncertain

  • Whether willpower genuinely “runs out” under some conditions; large replications have found little or no effect, but debate continues.
  • How long the effects of most habit-building techniques last; many studies run for weeks, not years.
  • Exactly how long a habit takes to become automatic; it varies widely by person and behaviour.
  • How well findings from mostly Western, short-term studies carry over to daily life in India and elsewhere.
