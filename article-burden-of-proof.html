The Burden of Proof: Who Has to Prove What?

“Can you prove it isn’t true?” sounds like a fair challenge. Usually it isn’t. Deciding who has to prove what is one of the quietest, most powerful choices in any argument, courtroom or law.

8 min read·25 November 2026

A message arrives on a family group: a common kitchen ingredient cures a serious disease, and doctors are hiding it. Someone gently asks for the source. The reply comes back: “Can you prove it doesn’t work?”

It feels like a reasonable question. But it quietly flips the conversation. The person who made the claim is now asking everyone else to do the work of disproving it. This move is so common that philosophers, lawyers and scientists have spent centuries thinking about it. The question at its heart is simple: who has to prove what?

The teapot in space

In 1952, the philosopher Bertrand Russell offered a memorable illustration. Suppose, he wrote, I claimed that a small china teapot is orbiting the Sun somewhere between Earth and Mars, too small to be seen by even the most powerful telescope. Nobody could prove me wrong. But it would be absurd to say that you must believe in the teapot until you can disprove it. The responsibility, Russell argued, lies with the person making the claim, not with the doubter.

This principle is ancient. A Latin legal saying puts it neatly: quod gratis asseritur, gratis negatur, roughly “what is asserted for free may be denied for free”. The writer Christopher Hitchens gave it a modern form: “What can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence.”

There is a good reason for the rule. There are endlessly many things that could be true and cannot be disproved: teapots in space, invisible dragons, hidden cures. If every unprovable claim had to be accepted until someone disproved it, we would have to believe almost everything, including claims that contradict each other.

The person who says “this is true” carries the burden. “You can’t prove it’s false” is not evidence that it is true.

In the courtroom

Courts make the burden of proof explicit. In 1935, a young English farm labourer named Reginald Woolmington was convicted of murdering his wife. He said the gun had gone off by accident. The trial judge told the jury that, since he had shot her, it was up to him to prove it was an accident. The House of Lords overturned the conviction. Lord Sankey said that throughout English criminal law “one golden thread is always to be seen”: it is the prosecution’s duty to prove the prisoner’s guilt. Woolmington was freed days before his scheduled execution, and the “golden thread” became one of the most quoted statements of the presumption of innocence.

Indian law sets out the same principle. Section 104 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, which replaced the Indian Evidence Act from 1 July 2024, says that whoever wants a court to rule in their favour on the basis of certain facts must prove those facts. Its own illustration is plain: if A wants B punished for a crime, A must prove that B committed it.

Courts also decide how much proof is needed. In criminal cases, guilt must be proved “beyond reasonable doubt”. In civil disputes, a claim generally needs to be shown only on the “balance of probabilities”, meaning more likely than not. The difference reflects which mistake a society fears more. The eighteenth-century English jurist William Blackstone wrote that it is “better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer”. Convicting an innocent person is treated as worse than letting a guilty one go, so the bar is set high.

In science

Science works the same way. A new claim, that a drug works, a particle exists or a habit causes disease, must be supported by evidence. Until then, the default position is that the effect hasn’t been shown. Researchers formalise this with a “null hypothesis”: the starting assumption that there is no effect, which the data must overturn.

How much evidence is enough? The astronomer Carl Sagan popularised the phrase “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence”, building on ideas going back at least to the philosopher David Hume in the 1700s. The more a claim conflicts with everything else we know, the stronger the evidence it needs. A friend saying they had tea this morning needs no proof. A friend saying they levitated needs a great deal. This is closely related to thinking in terms of prior probability (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine) and to not adding assumptions without need (see Occam’s Razor).

The principle has critics. Who decides what counts as “extraordinary”? A high bar can protect established ideas from challenge. The discovery that most stomach ulcers are caused by a bacterium was at first treated as an extraordinary claim, and it took years of evidence to win acceptance (see The Bacterium in the Stomach). Demanding strong evidence is sensible. Refusing to look at it is not.

“No evidence” is not the same as “evidence of no”

There is an important trap on the other side. When someone says “there is no evidence that X is harmful”, they may mean that careful studies found no harm, or simply that nobody has looked properly. Those are very different.

In a well-known 1995 note in the BMJ, the statisticians Douglas Altman and Martin Bland warned that “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence”. They pointed to research showing that many clinical trials reported as “negative” were simply too small to detect a real effect. In one review of 71 such trials, only 30% had a good chance of detecting even a large difference between treatments. A small study that finds nothing has not shown that there is nothing to find.

The big question: who must prove safety?

The burden of proof becomes most important when something new could cause harm. Should a company have to prove its product is safe before selling it, or should regulators have to prove it is harmful before stopping it? The answer has changed history.

For decades, leaded petrol was treated as safe until proven harmful. The industry’s leading medical adviser argued that critics had to show clear proof of damage. It took a long fight, and the work of scientists like Clair Patterson, before lead was removed from petrol (see The Man Who Dated the Earth).

Medicines followed a different path. After the thalidomide disaster of the early 1960s, in which a drug given to pregnant women caused severe birth defects in thousands of babies, the United States passed the 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendments. From then on, a company had to show that a new drug was not only safe but also effective before it could be sold. The burden sat firmly with the company.

Dietary supplements in the US offer a striking contrast. Under a 1994 law, they do not need approval before sale. The regulator generally has to show that a product poses a significant risk before it can remove it. When concerns grew about ephedra, a supplement used for weight loss and energy, it took about a decade, and more than 150 deaths, before a ban took effect in 2004.

India has its own example. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act of 1954 bans advertisements claiming to cure dozens of listed conditions, including cancer, diabetes and tuberculosis, and bans advertising “magic remedies” such as charms and amulets. In effect, it treats bold health claims as guilty until proven otherwise. Critics point out that it is rarely enforced.

The precautionary principle, and its limits

In 1992, the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development set out what is often called the precautionary principle: where there is a threat of serious or irreversible environmental damage, lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason to delay cost-effective protective measures. Some later versions go further, placing the burden of proof on whoever proposes a new activity.

The legal scholar Cass Sunstein has argued that strong versions of the principle can be paralysing, because inaction carries risks too. Delaying a vaccine over a rare side effect, for example, may cause more harm from the disease itself. The burden of proof is not a magic answer. It is a decision about which errors we are more willing to risk, and that decision should depend on how serious the possible harms are on each side.

Common tricks to watch for

  • Shifting the burden. “Prove me wrong” in response to a request for evidence. The person making the claim still owes the evidence.
  • The argument from ignorance. “No one has proved it’s false, so it must be true,” or the reverse, “no one has proved it’s true, so it must be false.” Lack of proof on its own settles neither.
  • Unfalsifiable claims. Carl Sagan described a dragon in his garage that was invisible, floated, breathed heatless fire and left no footprints. If no possible test could ever reveal it, the claim says nothing at all.
  • “Do your own research.” Investigating for yourself is good. Being asked to disprove someone else’s claim before they have offered evidence is not.
  • “There’s no evidence of harm.” Ask whether anyone has looked, how carefully, and how large the studies were.

Questions worth asking

  • Who is making the claim, and what evidence have they offered?
  • How surprising is the claim, given what we already know?
  • What would it cost to be wrong in each direction?
  • Is “no evidence” a careful finding, or just a lack of looking?

The burden of proof is not about winning arguments. It is about fairness: not forcing others to disprove whatever we choose to say, and not dismissing genuine evidence just because it is inconvenient. Placing the burden in the right place, at the right height, is one of the most practical habits in clear thinking.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • In law, the person asking a court to act on certain facts must prove them, and criminal guilt must be proved beyond reasonable doubt.
  • A failure to find evidence, especially in small studies, does not show that something is absent.
  • New medicines in many countries must be shown to be safe and effective before they can be sold.
  • Where the burden of proof is placed has shaped major public health outcomes, from leaded petrol to supplements.

Still uncertain

  • How strong the evidence should be for a claim to count as “extraordinary”; there is no agreed threshold.
  • How to apply the precautionary principle without causing harm through delay.
  • How regulators should balance speed and safety for new technologies, such as gene editing or artificial intelligence.
  • How well health advertising laws in India are enforced in practice, especially online.
