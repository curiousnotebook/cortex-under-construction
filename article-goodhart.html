When a Measure Becomes a Target

From colonial rat bounties to heart-rhythm drugs, one simple idea explains why chasing a good number can lead to a bad outcome.

5 min read·6 October 2026

Numbers are wonderful servants. They let us compare, track progress and notice problems we would otherwise miss. But they make dangerous masters. Something strange tends to happen when a number stops being a way of seeing and becomes the thing we are rewarded for. The number improves, and the reality it was meant to reflect does not, or even gets worse.

A law with several parents

In 1975, the British economist Charles Goodhart was writing about monetary policy. He observed that statistical relationships governments relied on tended to break down once those statistics became targets for control. The idea is now known as Goodhart’s law, and it is most often quoted in a crisp version by the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern:

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Marilyn Strathern (1997), summarising Goodhart’s idea

Around the same time, the American social scientist Donald Campbell reached a similar conclusion about social programmes: the more a quantitative indicator is used to make important decisions, the more it becomes subject to corruption, and the more it distorts the very activity it is meant to monitor. Different fields, same insight.

The rats of Hanoi

One vivid historical example comes from French colonial Hanoi in the early 1900s. Worried about rats and the diseases they could spread, authorities paid a bounty for each rat killed. To avoid handling piles of dead rats, they asked for proof in the form of a tail.

According to historian Michael Vann, who studied the colonial archives, officials soon began seeing tailless rats running through the city. Some people were apparently catching rats, cutting off their tails for the reward, and releasing them to breed. There were even reports of rats being farmed. The measure (tails collected) went up. The goal (fewer rats) was not achieved.

You may also have heard of the “cobra effect,” a story about a bounty on cobras in British-ruled India that supposedly led people to breed cobras. It is often repeated, but reliable historical documentation for it is thin, so it is best treated as a parable rather than established history. The Hanoi case, by contrast, is grounded in archival research.

A measure captures only part of what we care about. Under pressure, people and systems find ways to improve the part that is measured, sometimes at the expense of everything else.

The medical version: when a better number means more deaths

Medicine has a sobering example that every pharmacology student learns. After a heart attack, some patients develop extra, irregular heartbeats. Doctors knew that people with these irregular beats were at higher risk of sudden death. Drugs such as encainide and flecainide could suppress the irregular beats very effectively. The logic seemed airtight: fix the abnormal rhythm, and you should prevent deaths.

The Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST) put this logic to the test in a randomised, placebo-controlled trial. The results, published in 1991, were alarming. Patients taking encainide or flecainide had a higher death rate than those taking placebo. Those parts of the trial were stopped early.

The drugs had improved the number, the heart rhythm on a monitor, while worsening the outcome that actually mattered: staying alive. In medical language, the irregular beats were a surrogate endpoint, a measurable marker standing in for a real outcome. Some surrogates, such as certain blood pressure or cholesterol targets, have been validated by trials showing that improving them improves health. Others have not. CAST is a permanent reminder that a marker and an outcome are not the same thing.

Why it happens

  • Gaming: people find shortcuts that improve the number without improving reality, like cutting off rat tails.
  • Tunnel vision: attention shifts to what is measured and away from what isn’t. Studies of performance targets in public healthcare in England, for example, have documented both genuine improvements and various forms of gaming.
  • Mistaking a sign for a cause: a marker can be associated with a bad outcome without being what causes it, so changing the marker may not change the outcome, as CAST showed.

Goodhart in your own life

Once you know the pattern, you’ll see it everywhere. Exam marks are a measure of learning, but cramming can raise marks while leaving little lasting understanding (see Forgetting Is Not the Enemy). Step counts measure activity, but not its quality. Likes and followers measure attention, not value. Hours at a desk measure presence, not useful work. In academia, citation counts and journal rankings can encourage chasing metrics rather than asking important questions.

So what should we do?

Abandoning measurement isn’t the answer; without numbers, we fly blind. Instead, a few habits help:

  • Keep asking, “What is this number standing in for?”
  • Use several measures rather than one, so it is harder to improve one at the expense of everything else.
  • Prefer measures that are close to the real outcome people experience.
  • Combine numbers with human judgement and direct observation.
  • Revisit measures regularly, especially when results look suspiciously good.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • In CAST, encainide and flecainide suppressed irregular beats but increased mortality compared with placebo.
  • Gaming of performance targets has been documented in real public services.
  • The Hanoi rat bounty and its unintended consequences are documented in archival research.

Still uncertain

  • Whether the popular “cobra effect” story actually happened as told.
  • How to design targets that resist gaming while still motivating improvement.
  • For many newer treatments, whether the surrogate markers used to approve them reliably predict real benefit.
