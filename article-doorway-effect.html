The Doorway Effect: Why You Forget What You Came In For

You get up with a clear purpose, walk into the kitchen, and stand there blankly. You are not losing your mind. Your brain may simply have turned the page.

6 min read·21 November 2026

It happens to almost everyone. You are sitting in the living room, remember you need something from the bedroom, get up and walk there. Then you stop in the middle of the room. Kya lene aaya tha? What did I come here for? You go back to where you started, and suddenly it comes back to you.

It is easy to blame tiredness, age or a busy mind. Some psychologists have proposed a more surprising culprit: the doorway itself.

The experiments

In 2006, Gabriel Radvansky and David Copeland asked volunteers to move through a virtual building on a computer screen. At each table, they put down the object they were carrying and picked up a new one. Every so often, the researchers tested them: was this the object you are carrying, or the one you just put down? Information about the objects became less available after people moved into a new room. A later set of experiments sharpened the comparison: memory was poorer after walking from one room into another than after walking the same distance within a single large room.

In 2011, Radvansky and his colleagues tried it in the real world. They built a set of rooms with tables and had people carry objects hidden inside shoeboxes, so they couldn’t simply look. Once again, memory errors were more common after passing through a doorway than after covering a similar distance without one.

Then came the most interesting test. If forgetting happens because the new room “doesn’t match” the place where you formed the memory, then walking back into the original room should help. So the researchers compared three routes: through one doorway into a new room, through two doorways into a third, unfamiliar room, and through two doorways back to the starting room. Going back to the original room did not improve memory. What seemed to matter was simply crossing boundaries.

Doorways as chapter breaks

Radvansky’s explanation draws on a broader idea called event segmentation. Life does not arrive as one continuous stream. Our brains chop it into chunks, or events, much as a book is divided into chapters. Psychologist Jeffrey Zacks and colleagues have argued that we constantly predict what will happen next, and when the situation changes enough that our predictions stop working, the brain marks an event boundary and starts a new chunk.

A doorway is an obvious boundary. The room, the lighting and what you can see all change at once. Your brain may update its working model of “what is going on now”, and the details tied to the previous chunk, including why you got up, become a little harder to reach. In everyday terms, the intention belonged to the last chapter.

This forgetting may not be a flaw. A memory system that kept every detail of every room equally active would quickly be overwhelmed. Letting go of information that is probably no longer needed is usually efficient. It just fails in those moments when the reason you got up is exactly what you needed to carry across.

Doorways don’t erase memories. They may tell the brain that one chapter has ended, and the reason you got up was written in the last one.

Not so fast: the doorway effect is fragile

Like many neat findings in psychology, the doorway effect looks less tidy on closer inspection. In 2021, a team led by Jessica McFadyen tried to repeat it in four experiments, using immersive virtual reality, first-person videos and real walking through curtained boundaries. In most of the experiments, doorways made no meaningful difference to memory. An effect appeared only when people were also kept busy counting backwards aloud, and even then it showed up as more mistaken “yes” answers rather than simple forgetting.

The authors concluded that the doorway effect is real but more limited than often claimed. It seems to appear mainly when our working memory is already stretched. That fits ordinary experience rather well: the moments we forget are usually the ones when we were thinking about three other things on the way.

A related classic has had a similar fate. In 1975, a famous study found that divers remembered words better when tested in the same place they learned them, underwater or on land. When Jaap Murre repeated that experiment with divers in 2021, the same-place advantage did not appear. Reviews of many studies suggest that context can affect memory, but the effects are often modest and depend heavily on the situation. Findings like these are a reminder to treat single studies with care (see The Replication Crisis).

Boundaries can help memory, too

Event boundaries are not only bad news. Research suggests that breaking information into chunks can improve memory for what happens within each chunk. In 2021, Matthew Logie and David Donaldson ran four experiments in which people learned word lists in a virtual environment. Separating the lists with breaks helped people recall more words, and recall them in order. Interestingly, doorways weren’t necessary: simple gaps in time worked as well.

This fits what we know about learning. Studying in separate sessions, with breaks in between, tends to produce more durable memory than one long cram (see Forgetting Is Not the Enemy). Remembering is always a reconstruction from cues, which is why the right cue can bring something back in an instant (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller).

Keeping your purpose across the threshold

Psychologists who study prospective memory, remembering to do things in the future, have found that intentions are fragile when we are interrupted or distracted, and that cues help a great deal. A few simple habits can make the walk to the kitchen more successful:

  • Say it or picture it. Before you get up, say the purpose out loud or form a vivid image: “scissors, top drawer”.
  • Carry a cue. If you are going to fetch a charger, pick up the phone. An object in your hand keeps the purpose attached to you.
  • Pause at the threshold. A one-second reminder to yourself as you pass through the doorway can carry the intention into the new “chapter”.
  • Do one thing at a time. The doorway effect seems strongest when your mind is busy. Resist planning dinner while walking to get your glasses.
  • Retrace your steps, in your head or on foot. Going back to the original spot often feels helpful. The research on whether physically returning helps is mixed, but mentally picturing where you were when you had the thought is a well-supported way to jog memory.

Occasional “why am I here?” moments are normal at any age, and usually say more about a busy mind than a failing one. If forgetting starts to interfere with daily life, or is noticeably getting worse, it is worth talking to a doctor.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • People naturally divide experience into separate events, and boundaries between events influence what they remember.
  • Forgetting intended actions is common, especially when people are distracted or interrupted.
  • Reminder cues and specific plans make it more likely that intentions are carried out.
  • Breaking learning into separated chunks can improve later recall.

Still uncertain

  • How strong and reliable the doorway effect is; a careful set of replications found it only under extra mental load.
  • Whether doorways matter specifically, or whether any noticeable change in situation has the same effect.
  • Whether physically returning to the original room helps memory.
  • How these effects change with age and in people with memory problems.
