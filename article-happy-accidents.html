Happy Accidents? The Real Stories Behind Everyday Inventions

Sticky notes, super glue, non-stick pans and the microwave oven all began with something going wrong. But some of the most famous “happy accident” stories, from potato chips to penicillin, are exaggerated or simply untrue.

6 min read·5 December 2026

We love a good accident story. A scientist spills something, forgets something or makes the wrong thing, and the world gets a product it now cannot live without. These stories are satisfying because they suggest genius can strike anyone, at any moment.

Some of them are true. But when you look closely, two things stand out. The true accidents usually needed years of stubborn work before they became useful. And some of the most famous accident stories turn out to be exaggerated, or invented long after the fact.

Mistakes that really happened

The sticky note. In 1968, Spencer Silver, a chemist at the American company 3M, was trying to make stronger adhesives. Instead, he made one that was strong enough to hold paper but weak enough to peel off cleanly. Nobody knew what to do with it, and Silver spent years trying to interest colleagues. In the mid-1970s, his colleague Art Fry, annoyed that his paper bookmarks kept falling out of his hymn book at choir practice, realised the weak glue could make a bookmark that stayed put without damaging the page. After early test sales went poorly, 3M handed out free samples in one city, and Post-it Notes went on sale across the United States in 1980, about twelve years after Silver’s “failed” glue.

Super glue. In 1942, the chemist Harry Coover was working on clear plastics for gun sights during the Second World War. One group of chemicals, cyanoacrylates, was useless for the job because it stuck to everything it touched. It was set aside. Around 1951, Coover, by then at Eastman Kodak, met the same chemicals again while working on heat-resistant materials for jet canopies. This time he and his colleague Fred Joyner saw the stickiness as the point. Kodak began selling it in 1958.

The non-stick coating. On 6 April 1938, Roy Plunkett and his assistant Jack Rebok at the chemical company DuPont went to use a cylinder of a gas they had made for refrigerant research. Nothing came out, even though the cylinder weighed as much as it should. When they cut it open, they found a white powder: the gas had turned into a solid plastic, now known as PTFE and sold as Teflon. It was slippery, heat-resistant and almost nothing stuck to it. Its first major use was not in kitchens but in the wartime atomic bomb project, which needed materials that could resist extremely corrosive chemicals. Non-stick cookware came years later.

The microwave oven. In the mid-1940s, Percy Spencer, a largely self-taught engineer at the defence company Raytheon, was working with magnetrons, the devices that produce microwaves for radar. He noticed that a sweet in his pocket had melted. Accounts differ on whether it was a chocolate bar or a peanut candy bar. He then tried popcorn, which popped. Raytheon filed a patent in October 1945. The first commercial “Radarange” was about as tall as a person and cost around $5,000. A countertop model for homes came only in 1967.

The Slinky. In 1943, Richard James, a naval engineer, saw a spring fall from a shelf and “walk” as it tumbled. He spent about a year finding the right steel wire to make a spring that would reliably step down stairs. His wife, Betty, is credited with naming it. At a Philadelphia department store in November 1945, the first 400 sold in about 90 minutes.

The first artificial sweetener. In 1878, the chemist Constantin Fahlberg, working in Ira Remsen’s laboratory at Johns Hopkins University, noticed that the bread he ate at dinner tasted strangely sweet. He traced the taste to a chemical he had spilled on his hands in the lab: saccharin. The two published their work together in 1879, but Fahlberg later patented it in his own name, which Remsen publicly protested. (Tasting unknown laboratory chemicals is, needless to say, no longer standard practice.)

In nearly every true “accident” story, the accident took a moment. Turning it into something useful took years.

Stories that grew in the telling

The potato chip. The legend says that in 1853, at a resort in Saratoga Springs, New York, a customer, often said to be the railway tycoon Cornelius Vanderbilt, kept sending back his fried potatoes for being too thick. The cook, George Crum, sliced them paper-thin out of spite, and accidentally invented the crisp. Historians doubt almost every part of this. A recipe for “potatoes fried in slices or shavings” appeared in William Kitchiner’s cookbook The Cook’s Oracle in 1817, decades earlier. Fried potato slices were already known in Saratoga by 1853, Vanderbilt was reportedly in Europe at the time, and the Vanderbilt version seems to have been spread by a 1973 advertising campaign by a company that made chip packaging. Crum may well have helped make Saratoga chips famous, but he did not invent them by accident.

The chocolate chip cookie. The popular story is that, around 1938, Ruth Wakefield, who ran the Toll House Inn in Massachusetts, ran out of baking chocolate, threw in chopped pieces of a chocolate bar and expected them to melt. Instead, they held their shape. But Wakefield was a trained home-economics teacher and dietitian, and accounts of her own words describe a deliberate experiment to give her customers “something different”. The accident version may simply be a better story, and it may have helped sell chocolate.

Penicillin. The most famous accident in science is real in outline. In 1928, the bacteriologist Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to find mould growing on one of his bacterial plates, with the bacteria nearby dying. But the familiar version, a spore drifting through an open window and a lone genius changing medicine overnight, leaves out a lot. Fleming’s published account omitted key details, and historians have long argued about exactly what happened; one leading theory suggests the mould came from a laboratory downstairs, and that an unusual spell of cool weather was needed for the effect to appear at all. More importantly, Fleming did not turn penicillin into a medicine. That took a team at Oxford led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, with Norman Heatley, who purified it around 1940, and then large-scale production in the United States. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

This pattern of a neat story hiding a messier reality is common in science (see The Bacterium in the Stomach). Sometimes an accident is even the start of something much bigger: Clair Patterson’s struggle with lead contaminating his laboratory led him to discover how much lead was in the air everyone breathed (see The Man Who Dated the Earth).

What the accidents have in common

In 1854, the chemist Louis Pasteur told an audience at the University of Lille that “in the fields of observation, chance favours only the prepared mind.” The real accident stories bear him out.

  • Someone noticed. Plenty of people have had sweets melt in their pockets. Spencer understood why.
  • Someone kept going. The sticky note took twelve years; super glue was dismissed once before it was taken seriously.
  • Someone found a use. Silver’s glue was a solution looking for a problem until Fry’s hymn book supplied one.

There is also a hidden side. We only hear about the accidents that turned into products. Countless spills, failed batches and odd results led nowhere, and nobody tells their stories. That is a form of survivorship bias: by focusing on the winners, we can overestimate how often luck pays off.

So the next time you hear that something was invented by accident, it is worth asking two questions. Is the story true? And if it is, what did someone have to do afterwards to make that accident matter?

What we know, and what we don’t

Well documented

  • Post-it Notes began with an unexpectedly weak adhesive made at 3M in 1968.
  • PTFE (Teflon) was discovered in 1938 when a gas cylinder unexpectedly turned out to contain a solid.
  • Fried potato slices were described in a cookbook decades before the 1853 George Crum legend.
  • Penicillin became a usable medicine through the Oxford team’s work around 1940, not through Fleming alone.

Still uncertain

  • The exact details of Percy Spencer’s melted sweet, which vary between accounts.
  • Exactly how Fleming’s famous plate came to be contaminated, and when.
  • What Ruth Wakefield intended when she first added chocolate pieces to her cookies.
  • How many other inventions’ “accident” stories were polished later for marketing.
