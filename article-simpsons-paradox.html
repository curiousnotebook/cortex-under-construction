Simpson’s Paradox: When Every Group Improves but the Total Gets Worse

A treatment can win in every group of patients and still lose overall. A university can favour women in most departments and still seem to favour men. The numbers are not lying, but they are not telling the story you think.

11 min read·9 December 2026

Imagine a national test taken by 17-year-olds. Over twenty years, the average score of every major group of students goes up. Surely the national average went up too? It need not. In the United States between 1992 and 2012, the long-running national maths assessment for 17-year-olds showed exactly this: scores rose slightly for white, Black and Hispanic students, yet the overall average dipped by about a point.

The opposite can happen as well. In 2013 the journalist Floyd Norris pointed out that the typical American worker’s inflation-adjusted weekly wage had risen by about 0.9% since 2000. Yet over the same period it had fallen for high-school dropouts (down 7.9%), high-school graduates (down 4.7%), people with some college (down 7.6%) and people with a degree (down 1.2%). Every group did worse, and the total did better.

This is Simpson’s paradox: a pattern that holds in every group can disappear, or even reverse, when the groups are combined. It is named after the British statistician Edward Simpson, who described it in 1951, although similar effects had been noted decades earlier, notably by Udny Yule in 1903. It is not a trick or a rare curiosity. It turns up in university admissions, hospital records and pandemic statistics, and it has fooled experts as well as the public.

Two surgeries and a pile of kidney stones

One of the most quoted real examples comes from a 1986 study in the British Medical Journal that compared ways of removing kidney stones. Two of the methods were traditional open surgery and a less invasive keyhole procedure called percutaneous nephrolithotomy, done through a small cut in the back.

Overall, the keyhole procedure looked better. It succeeded in 289 of 350 patients (83%), against 273 of 350 (78%) for open surgery. But when statisticians later split the same patients by the size of their stones, the picture flipped:

  • Small stones: open surgery succeeded in 93% of patients (81 of 87); the keyhole method in 87% (234 of 270).
  • Large stones: open surgery succeeded in 73% (192 of 263); the keyhole method in 69% (55 of 80).
  • All patients together: open surgery 78%, the keyhole method 83%.

Open surgery did better for small stones and for large stones, yet worse overall. How?

Look at who got which treatment. About three-quarters of the open-surgery patients had large stones, which are harder to treat whatever you do. Fewer than a quarter of the keyhole patients did. Open surgery was handed the difficult cases, so its overall success rate was dragged down by the mix of patients, not by the surgery itself. The overall figure is really a comparison of two different groups of patients.

An overall average is a weighted blend of the groups inside it. If two things being compared contain very different mixtures of easy and hard cases, their averages can mislead, even when every number is correct.

Berkeley, 1973: was the university biased against women?

In autumn 1973, the University of California, Berkeley received complete applications for graduate study from 8,442 men and 4,321 women. About 44% of the men were admitted, but only about 35% of the women. With numbers that large, the gap was far too big to be chance. It looked like clear evidence of discrimination.

The statistician Peter Bickel and two colleagues looked closer, and published their analysis in Science in 1975. Admission decisions at Berkeley were made by individual departments, so they examined each department separately. Out of 85 departments, only four showed a significant bias against women, while six showed a significant bias against men. Taken department by department, the data showed a small bias in favour of women.

The six largest departments, a subset often used in textbooks, show the pattern clearly. Combined, they admitted about 45% of men and 30% of women. Yet in four of the six, women were admitted at a higher rate than men. In the most extreme case, one department admitted 82% of its women applicants and 62% of its men.

The explanation was the same as with the kidney stones. Women had applied in greater numbers to departments that were very hard to get into, often in the humanities, where many applicants competed for few places. Men applied more often to departments that admitted a large share of applicants, often in science and engineering. Women were rejected more often overall because of where they applied, not because each department treated them worse.

Two notes of caution keep this story honest. First, it is often said that Berkeley was sued over these figures. As far as anyone has been able to trace, it was not; the university was worried that it might be. Second, Bickel and his colleagues did not conclude that women faced no disadvantage. They suggested the disadvantage lay earlier, in the schooling and social pressures that steered women towards fields that were more crowded and less well funded. The paradox did not make the inequality disappear. It moved the question somewhere else.

The smokers who seemed to live longer

Medicine offers a more startling case. In 1972–74, researchers surveyed one in six residents of Whickham, a town near Newcastle in England. Twenty years later, a follow-up recorded who had died. Among the 1,314 women who were either current smokers or had never smoked, about 24% of smokers had died, compared with about 31% of non-smokers. Taken at face value, smoking seemed to protect women.

It did not. When the women are grouped by their age at the first survey, smokers were more likely to have died within the younger, middle and older age bands alike. The reason for the reversal was age. Women of the oldest generation in the survey had rarely smoked, and of course they were the most likely to die within twenty years. Smokers were, on average, younger, and their youth hid the harm that smoking was doing.

The same virus, two countries, opposite answers

The Covid-19 pandemic produced a high-profile example. Early in 2020, the share of confirmed cases who died, the case fatality rate, was about 4.4% in Italy, roughly double the 2.3% reported from China. Yet when researchers lined up the two countries by age, Italy’s fatality rate was lower than or equal to China’s in every age group.

Again, the mix explains it. Around four in ten confirmed cases in the Italian data were aged 70 or over, compared with about one in eight in the Chinese data. Older people are far more likely to die of Covid-19, so a country whose recorded cases were mostly older people would show a higher overall rate, even if it did slightly better at every age. That difference in age mix could itself reflect older populations, who was infected and who was tested, which is why raw national comparisons from that period needed great care.

Hospital league tables

Hospitals are often compared using a single number, such as a standardised mortality ratio: how many patients died compared with how many would be expected, given how ill they were. These measures adjust for each patient’s risk, so they might seem immune to Simpson’s paradox.

They are not. In a 2014 analysis in BMJ Quality & Safety, Perla Marang-van de Mheen and Kaveh Shojania showed that a hospital’s overall score can still mislead even if the risk adjustment were perfect. If one hospital treats a larger share of very high-risk patients, and outcomes in that small group differ from what the model predicts, the overall ratios of two hospitals can diverge sharply, even when the hospitals perform identically in every group of patients. They recommended publishing such figures with a description of each hospital’s case mix and a clear caution against simple league-table rankings.

This matters because league tables shape where patients go and how hospitals are judged. A hospital that accepts the hardest cases can look worse than one that quietly avoids them, the same trap as the kidney stones. And when a number becomes a target, there is an incentive to manage the mix of patients rather than the quality of care (see When a Measure Becomes a Target).

Why it happens

All these cases share two ingredients.

  • The groups differ in their baseline rates. Large stones are harder than small ones. Older people are more likely to die. Some departments admit far fewer applicants.
  • The things being compared contain different mixtures of those groups. Open surgery got more large stones. Italy’s cases were older. Women applied more to selective departments.

When both are true, the overall figure mostly reflects the mixture rather than the thing you wanted to compare. The wage example works the same way in reverse. Over that period, a growing share of American workers held degrees, the best-paid group, so the overall median could rise even while each group’s wages fell. The national maths scores fell for a mirror-image reason: the share of students from groups with lower average scores grew, so the average could dip while every group improved.

If you remember one thing, make it this: the overall average depends on the size of each group as well as on how each group performs. Change the sizes, and you can change the total without anyone doing better or worse.

So which number is right?

The obvious response is “always split the data”. That is often right, but not always, and this is where the paradox becomes a question of reasoning rather than arithmetic.

The computer scientist Judea Pearl argues that the data alone cannot tell you whether to combine or split. You need to know how the variables are causally related. If the third factor affects both which group someone is in and their outcome, and comes before the thing you are studying, you should split by it. Stone size influenced which surgery doctors chose and also how well any surgery worked, so the split tables give the fairer comparison. Age at the start of the Whickham survey plays the same role.

But if the third factor is itself a result of the thing you are studying, splitting by it can hide part of the effect you care about. Suppose a drug lowers blood pressure, and lower blood pressure is how it prevents strokes. Comparing patients only within groups that have the same blood pressure would strip out the very route by which the drug works. In that case the combined figure tells the truer story.

Berkeley sits between the two. If your question is “Did admissions committees treat men and women differently?”, then department-by-department comparisons are the right ones. If your question is “Did women, overall, have a harder path into graduate school?”, then the combined figure is part of the answer, because the choice of department was partly shaped by gender. Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions.

There is a further warning. Almost any data set can be split in many ways, and with enough splitting, some pattern will reverse by chance. Choosing the split that tells the story you prefer is a close cousin of the selective analysis that drives the replication crisis (see The Replication Crisis). The split should be chosen because of a causal reason given in advance, not because of the result it produces.

Neither the combined number nor the split numbers are automatically correct. The right one depends on the question you are asking and on what causes what, which is why statistics alone cannot settle the paradox.

Protecting yourself from the paradox

You do not need equations to spot the danger. When you see two overall rates being compared, ask:

  • What is in the mix? Do the things being compared contain the same kinds of people, cases or situations?
  • Is there a hidden factor that affects both? Severity of illness, age, difficulty, or which option people chose.
  • Did the group sizes change? A national average can move simply because the population changed, even when nobody’s results changed.
  • Does the factor come before or after? Split by things that come before the choice or treatment; be careful about splitting by things it causes.
  • Was the split chosen in advance? Or was it found after searching for an interesting result?

These questions are cousins of others in this journal. Like base rates (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine) and survivorship bias (see Survivorship Bias), Simpson’s paradox reminds us that a number on its own is never the whole story. What matters is what was counted, who was included, and how the pieces were put together.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • A relationship that holds in every subgroup can vanish or reverse when the subgroups are combined. This is a mathematical fact about weighted averages, not a statistical error.
  • Real examples exist in admissions, surgery, epidemiology, earnings and test scores, and they have misled both the public and experts.
  • In the Berkeley data, the overall gap between men and women largely reflected the departments they applied to, not how departments treated individual applicants.
  • Comparisons of hospitals or countries can mislead when their mix of patients or cases differs, even after risk adjustment.

Still uncertain

  • In many real data sets, it is not obvious which factors are confounders and which are effects of the thing being studied, so experts can reasonably disagree about whether to split or combine.
  • How often full reversals occur in everyday data is unclear; smaller distortions from mixing are probably far more common than complete reversals.
  • How the Berkeley findings should be read for fairness remains debated: fair committees can coexist with unequal paths into the most competitive fields.
