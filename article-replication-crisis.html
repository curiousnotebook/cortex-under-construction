The Replication Crisis: When Scientific Findings Don’t Hold Up

A result that appears in a top journal is not yet a fact. When teams try to repeat famous experiments, many results shrink or disappear. What that tells us, and what it doesn’t, is more useful than either panic or dismissal.

7 min read·3 November 2026

Science is supposed to be self-correcting. A claim is published, others test it, and the truth eventually emerges. In the last fifteen years, that process has been put under the microscope, and the results have been uncomfortable. Across several fields, when researchers have deliberately repeated published studies, a worrying share of the original findings has not held up. Scientists call this the replication crisis.

The psychology test

The most influential attempt came in 2015. The Open Science Collaboration, a group of around 270 researchers coordinated by the Center for Open Science, selected 100 studies from three leading psychology journals published in 2008 and tried to repeat each one using the original materials and, where possible, larger samples. In the original papers, 97 per cent of results were statistically significant. In the replications, only 36 per cent were. Replication effects were also typically smaller than the originals. Only about 39 per cent of the replications were judged subjectively to have reproduced the original result.

The authors were careful. They did not claim that every original finding was false. They showed that the published literature was far less reliable than its apparent certainty suggested. Some findings were stronger than others, and replications tended to succeed more often in areas where the original effects were large.

The conclusion was contested. A critique published in Science the following year argued that the replications were themselves imperfect, and that the data are compatible with a rather higher level of reproducibility than the headline figure suggests. The debate about how to measure replication, what counts as success, and how much a single failed replication should weigh, is still live. That disagreement is part of the story, not an embarrassment to it.

Cancer biology: a more dramatic picture

Outside psychology, the alarm was first raised by industry. In 2012, C. Glenn Begley and Lee Ellis reported in Nature that scientists at the pharmaceutical company Amgen had tried to confirm the findings of 53 “landmark” preclinical cancer papers. They could reproduce the key findings of only six, about 11 per cent. A Bayer team had earlier reported that it could validate only about a quarter of the academic studies it examined. Neither company published its full list of papers, which made the claims hard to check, and critics pointed out the lack of transparency. Still, the figures prompted serious concern.

To test those claims openly, the Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology set out in 2013 to repeat experiments from 53 high-impact papers published between 2010 and 2012. It turned out to be far harder than expected. Many original experiments lacked the detail needed to repeat them, and some materials were no longer available. The team completed only 50 of the 193 experiments it had planned, a little over a quarter. Of those, the replications found effects that were, for positive results, a median of 85 per cent smaller than the originals. Most of the original claims were, in effect, weaker than they first looked.

The problem is not only that some studies are wrong. It is that the published size of an effect is often larger than the real one.

Social science under test

In 2018, Colin Camerer and colleagues took 21 experimental studies in the social sciences published in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015. These were among the most prestigious journals in science, and the studies had been widely cited. The replications used larger samples and analysis plans agreed in advance. The team found a significant effect in the same direction as the original for 13 of the 21 studies, about 62 per cent. On average, the replicated effect sizes were about half the originals. Before the replications were run, researchers had been asked to predict which studies would replicate, and prediction markets did well: the community could anticipate many of the failures.

That last finding matters. It suggests that scientists often have a sense of which results are fragile, and that this knowledge is not always reflected in how studies are read or cited.

Why does this happen?

Several reasons have been proposed, and they are not mutually exclusive. The most famous theoretical argument came in 2005, when John Ioannidis published “Why Most Published Research Findings Are False” in PLoS Medicine. His model suggested that the chance a claimed finding is true depends on the study’s statistical power, on how many relationships researchers test, on how much flexibility they have in analysis, and on how likely the hypothesis was to be true before the study began. In fields where studies are small, effects are modest, and many tests are run, false positives can outnumber true ones.

Ioannidis's argument has been criticised. Steven Goodman and Sander Greenland argued that his model assumed a low prior probability for most hypotheses, and that it did not show the strong conclusion claimed in the title. The mathematics depends heavily on assumptions that cannot be checked directly, which is why the essay is best read as a warning about incentives and design rather than as a measured estimate of the literature.

The practical causes are easier to see. Journals prefer striking, positive results. Researchers face pressure to publish and to find significance, and small choices about which outcome to report, which participants to exclude, or when to stop collecting data can increase the chance of a false positive. Negative results often remain in file drawers. Replications are rarely rewarded, and many were not designed in advance with pre-registered plans. Each of these pressures is reasonable taken alone, and the combination can be corrosive.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • When large groups have repeated published studies, a substantial share of significant findings have not reproduced, and effect sizes are often smaller on repetition.
  • Replication is harder than it looks, and original studies often lack the detail needed to repeat them exactly.
  • Publication pressures, flexible analysis and selective reporting make false positives more likely.

Still uncertain

  • What proportion of the whole literature is unreliable. Estimates depend heavily on which studies are sampled and how success is defined.
  • Whether the failures are concentrated in some fields, designs or subfields more than others.
  • Which reforms, such as pre-registration, open data and larger samples, best improve reliability in practice.

What the crisis does and doesn’t mean

It is tempting to conclude that science cannot be trusted. That conclusion is wrong. The replication projects themselves were scientific successes: they used transparent methods, shared their data, and produced evidence that could be checked. They show that the system can examine itself. Many fields have since adopted pre-registration, larger samples and open materials, and these changes are beginning to show results.

A more useful reading is that single studies are the beginning of a conversation, not the end. A surprising finding in one lab deserves replication before it changes practice. Evidence built from many independent teams is far more reliable than the headline from one. And the size of an effect matters as much as whether it is statistically significant. A finding that drops by half on repetition may still be real, but it is not the miracle the press release described.
