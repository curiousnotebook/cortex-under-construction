The Replication Crisis: When Scientific Findings Don’t Hold Up

When other teams repeat published studies, a surprising number of results shrink or vanish. The story of how scientists discovered this, and what they are doing about it, is one of the most important in modern research.

8 min read·3 November 2026

In 2011, three psychologists published an experiment showing that listening to the Beatles song “When I’m Sixty-Four” made people younger. Not feel younger: actually younger. Students who heard the song were, on average, nearly a year and a half younger than students who heard a different track, and the result was statistically significant.

Of course, it wasn’t true. Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn ran the study to make a point. They had used choices that many researchers at the time considered perfectly normal: collecting a bit more data if the first batch looked promising, choosing which measurements to report, adjusting for extra factors. Each choice seemed harmless. Together, their computer simulations showed, such choices could push the chance of a false “discovery” from the 5% researchers assume to as high as 61%. If you could prove the impossible this easily, how many ordinary published findings were also mirages?

What replication means

A replication is an attempt to repeat a study with new participants or new samples, to see whether the same result appears. It is one of the foundations of science. A single study can be a fluke; a finding that appears again and again, in different labs, is much harder to explain away. For a long time, though, replications were rarely done and even more rarely published. Journals wanted new discoveries, not repeats. So many findings were built upon, taught and cited without anyone checking whether they held up.

Psychology’s big test

In 2015, more than 270 researchers working together as the Open Science Collaboration published the results of a huge effort. They had repeated 100 studies from three leading psychology journals. In the originals, 97% had reported a statistically significant result. In the repeats, only 36% did. On average, the effects found in the replications were about half the size of the originals. Studies of thinking and memory fared better than studies of social behaviour, but both shrank.

A 2018 project tested 21 social science experiments published in Nature and Science, two of the world’s most prestigious journals, between 2010 and 2015. Thirteen of the 21, about 62%, replicated, and again the effects were on average about half as large. Intriguingly, when other researchers were asked to bet on which studies would hold up, their predictions closely tracked the real results. Many scientists, it seemed, already had a sense of which findings were shaky.

Some famous ideas did not survive. One was “ego depletion”, the theory that willpower runs down like a battery. When 23 labs ran the same carefully planned test in 2016, the effect was close to zero (see Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy).

Not just psychology

The problem is not confined to studies of human behaviour. In 2012, C. Glenn Begley, then a scientist at the biotech company Amgen, and Lee Ellis reported that when Amgen’s researchers tried to confirm 53 “landmark” cancer studies before building drug programmes on them, they could confirm only 6. Their data were never published in detail, so the claim was hard to check, but it alarmed the field.

A more transparent test followed. The Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology set out to repeat 193 experiments from 53 high-profile cancer papers. It hit a wall almost at once. Not one of the 193 experiments was described in enough detail to repeat without asking the original authors for more information, and for 68% of the experiments the team could not obtain the underlying data. In the end, they managed to repeat just 50 experiments from 23 papers. For positive results, the median effect in the replications was 85% smaller than in the originals. Depending on how “success” was defined, fewer than half held up.

A national effort in Brazil, involving 56 laboratories and reported as a preprint in 2025, tested common biomedical lab methods. After excluding replications that strayed from the planned protocol, roughly 15% to 45% of results were reproduced, depending on the method and criteria. The authors stressed that vague reporting of methods explained much of the inconsistency, which does not necessarily mean the original findings were wrong.

Why does this happen?

Very little of the replication crisis is about fraud. Most of it comes from ordinary pressures and habits that quietly bend the published record.

  • Publication bias. Studies that find something are more likely to be published than studies that find nothing. In 2021, Anne Scheel and colleagues compared ordinary psychology papers with registered reports, a format in which a journal agrees to publish a study before the results are known. In ordinary papers, 96% of first hypotheses were supported. In registered reports, only 44% were. The published literature is a collection of survivors (see Survivorship Bias).
  • Flexible analysis. As the Beatles study showed, small decisions made after seeing the data can turn noise into a “finding”. This is often called p-hacking, though it is usually done without any intent to deceive.
  • Small studies. A study with few participants can only produce a statistically significant result if the effect it finds is large. So when small studies do reach significance, they tend to overestimate the true effect, which then shrinks on repetition.
  • Incentives. Careers depend on publishing new, striking results in prestigious journals. When a measure like publication count becomes a target, it can reward speed and surprise over reliability (see When a Measure Becomes a Target).
  • Honest differences. A replication can fail because of real differences in participants, materials, lab conditions or simply chance. A failed replication is evidence against a finding, not proof that it was false.

In 2005, the epidemiologist John Ioannidis brought these forces together in a paper provocatively titled “Why most published research findings are false”. He argued that when studies are small, effects are modest, designs are flexible and a field is competitive, a published positive result is more likely to be wrong than right. The argument is essentially about base rates: if most ideas tested are long shots, even a “significant” result leaves the odds stacked against them (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

A single study is a clue, not a verdict. Confidence should grow when independent teams find the same thing again.

How bad is it, really?

Not everyone agrees on the size of the problem. In 2016, a group led by Daniel Gilbert argued that some of the psychology replications differed from the original studies in important ways, and that the crisis had been overstated. The original team replied that their conclusions still stood. The debate is a reminder that there is no single “replication rate”. It depends on the field, which studies are chosen and how success is defined, as the cancer project’s range of figures shows.

Scientists themselves take the problem seriously. In a 2016 Nature survey of more than 1,500 researchers, most said they had at some point failed to reproduce an experiment, and 52% said there was a “significant crisis”.

Science repairing itself

The encouraging part of this story is that the problem was uncovered by scientists, using science. Several reforms are now spreading:

  • Preregistration: writing down the hypothesis and analysis plan before collecting data, so that results cannot quietly reshape the question.
  • Registered reports: journals agreeing to publish based on the question and method, whatever the result.
  • Sharing data and methods so that others can check and repeat the work.
  • Large, multi-lab studies that test the same question in many places at once.

Medicine offers a striking example. Since 2000, large clinical trials funded by the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute have had to register their main outcome in advance. Robert Kaplan and Veronica Irvin found that 57% of such trials published before 2000 reported a benefit for the treatment, compared with only 8% afterwards. The authors were careful to say they could not prove registration caused the change, but the pattern is hard to ignore.

Reforms need checking too. In 2023, a high-profile paper reported that studies following best practices replicated very well. In 2024 the journal retracted it, partly because its analyses had not been preregistered in the way the paper claimed. Even the reformers are not exempt from the problems they study, which is exactly why independent checking matters. A similar story unfolded this year with a celebrated trial on the timing of cancer treatment (see Does the Time You Take a Medicine Matter?).

What this means for the rest of us

None of this means science is broken or that we should distrust everything. It means that individual studies, especially new and surprising ones, deserve a little patience. A few questions help when you read about a new finding:

  • Has an independent team found the same thing?
  • How many people, animals or samples were studied?
  • Was the study planned and registered in advance?
  • Is the effect surprisingly large for something so simple?
  • Does the headline match what the study actually measured?

The replication crisis is uncomfortable, but it is also science working as it should: checking itself, admitting mistakes and building better tools. The findings that survive this scrutiny are worth trusting more than ever.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • In large replication projects, many published findings in psychology, social science and preclinical biology fail to replicate, and effects that do replicate are usually smaller.
  • Positive results are far more common in ordinary journal articles than in formats that commit to publishing before the results are known.
  • Flexible choices in collecting and analysing data can make false positives much more likely than the nominal 5%.
  • Many published papers do not describe their methods in enough detail for others to repeat them.

Still uncertain

  • The “true” replication rate in any field, which depends on which studies are chosen and how success is defined.
  • How much failure reflects false original findings, and how much reflects real differences between labs, methods and populations.
  • How much reforms such as preregistration have improved reliability so far.
  • Whether experts’ ability to predict which findings will replicate can be put to routine use.
