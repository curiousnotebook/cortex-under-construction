Why We Get Attached to People Who Hurt Us

“Why doesn’t she just leave?” is the wrong question. The bond to someone who hurts us is built from ordinary machinery: an infant’s need to stay close, the pull of unpredictable kindness, and everything a person stands to lose.

13 min read·7 December 2026

Most of us know someone in this situation. A friend who keeps going back to a partner who belittles her. A grown son still hungry for the approval of a father who has only ever mocked him. An employee who defends the boss who humiliates her in meetings. From the outside the answer looks obvious, and the people involved often know it too. They can list everything that is wrong, and they still feel pulled back.

It is tempting to explain this as weakness, foolishness or a hidden wish to suffer. The research points somewhere else. The bond to a person who hurts us seems to be built from ordinary psychological machinery, the same machinery that keeps babies close to their parents and makes unpredictable rewards so gripping, running in a situation it was never designed for.

This essay is mostly about relationships that involve abuse: violence, threats, humiliation or control. Ordinary rough patches between people who treat each other decently are a different matter.

A baby cannot afford to run from its mother

In 2000, the neuroscientist Regina Sullivan and her colleagues published a short paper in Nature with an uncomfortable finding. They exposed very young rat pups to a smell paired with a mild electric shock. Older animals in this kind of experiment learn to avoid the smell. The youngest pups did the opposite: they learned to approach it.

They were not numb to the shock. By the researchers’ measures, the youngest pups found it just as unpleasant as slightly older ones did. Yet the pain taught them attraction rather than fear. Around ten days after birth, about when pups begin to walk and leave the nest, the pattern flipped and the same pairing produced avoidance. The brain’s fear centre, the amygdala, took part in the learning only after that switch. And a pup that had learned the preference early kept approaching the smell even at an age when it could have learned to avoid it.

One way to make sense of this is to think about what an infant needs. A helpless newborn survives only by staying close to its caregiver. A learning system that taught it to avoid its only source of warmth and food whenever something hurt would be a dangerous one. For a short window, pain linked to the caregiver seems to strengthen the bond instead of breaking it.

Rats are not people, and this experiment could never be run on children. But the pattern is not limited to rats. A wide-ranging 1978 review of attachment across many species pointed out that young animals form bonds to all sorts of unlikely objects, and even to sources of maltreatment, which was a puzzle for the main theories of the time. In humans, reviews of research on maltreated children find that, on average, they are less securely attached to their mothers than other children. The bond does not disappear; it becomes anxious and unstable.

The British psychiatrist John Bowlby, who founded attachment theory, argued that fear and threat make a child seek its caregiver more urgently, not less. That works well when the caregiver is the safe place. It becomes a trap when the caregiver is also the source of the fear: the alarm drives the child towards the very person who set it off. Human babies are among the most dependent newborns in the animal world (see Born Unfinished), which may help explain why this system is so strong in us.

The psychologist Jennifer Freyd has taken the idea further. Her betrayal trauma theory proposes that a child who depends on a caregiver may have good reason not to register betrayal fully, because seeing it clearly would threaten a relationship the child cannot do without. She calls this “betrayal blindness”. It is an influential idea, though the parts of it concerned with forgotten memories of abuse remain debated.

For a dependent infant, staying close to a caregiver matters so much that the brain may keep the bond even when the caregiver causes pain. Adults carry a version of that same system.

Unpredictable kindness is the strongest glue

In 1981 the psychologists Donald Dutton and Susan Painter gave this pattern in adult relationships a name: traumatic bonding. They proposed that two features of abusive relationships, rather than anything about the victim’s character, create unusually strong attachments. The first is a power imbalance, in which one person dominates and the other comes to feel smaller and more dependent. The second is intermittent treatment: periods of warmth, apology or even tenderness, broken by episodes of abuse, in a rhythm the victim cannot predict or control.

In 1993 they tested the idea. They interviewed 75 women who had recently left abusive partners, and assessed them again six months later. The women’s emotional attachment to their former partner did not vanish after leaving. It was best predicted by the dynamics of the relationship, especially how intermittent the abuse had been and how the balance of power had shifted. After six months, attachment had fallen by only about a quarter, and features of the relationship explained more than half of the differences between women at that point.

Why would intermittent treatment bind people more tightly than constant cruelty? Learning research offers a clue. In the laboratory, a behaviour that is rewarded every time tends to stop quickly once the rewards stop. A behaviour that has been rewarded only some of the time keeps going much longer, because a run of disappointments has always ended in a reward before. Psychologists call this the partial reinforcement extinction effect. It is the same principle that keeps people feeding a slot machine, and it helps explain why superstitious habits are so hard to drop (see Don’t Sweep After Sunset).

In a relationship with unpredictable kindness, every bad spell has, so far, eventually ended in a good one. Hope is reinforced exactly when it is most tested. The good periods are also felt against the background of fear, so their arrival brings relief as well as affection, and relief is a powerful reward.

There may be a statistical illusion at work too. After an explosion, a calmer period often follows simply because extreme moments tend to be followed by more ordinary ones, a pattern called regression to the mean (see Why Praise Seems to Fail and Punishment Seems to Work). Each calm stretch can look like evidence that the person is changing, even when nothing about the pattern has changed.

A craving, not just a choice

People who have left a harmful relationship often describe missing the person in terms that sound like withdrawal. Brain imaging gives this description some support, with caveats.

In a 2010 study, Helen Fisher and colleagues scanned the brains of 15 people who had recently been rejected by a partner but said they were still intensely in love. When they looked at a photograph of the person who had left them, regions involved in reward, motivation and craving became active, including areas that are also active when people addicted to cocaine crave the drug.

This was a small study, about rejection rather than abuse, and “love is like an addiction” is an analogy, not a diagnosis. But it fits what many survivors report: the pull towards a former partner can feel physical and compulsive, quite separate from what they think of the person. Knowing that can make the feeling less frightening. A craving is not a message that the relationship was good, and cravings fade with time and distance.

The longer you stay, the more there is to lose

Not all of the pull is emotional. Some of it is arithmetic.

The social psychologist Caryl Rusbult developed what she called the investment model of commitment. It holds that how committed we are to a relationship depends not only on how satisfied we are, but on how much we have put into it and how good our alternatives look. In 1995, Rusbult and John Martz applied the model to women arriving at a shelter for battered women. Women felt more committed to their partners when they had poorer economic alternatives and more invested in the relationship, such as being married, and when the abuse they reported was less severe. That commitment strongly predicted who went straight back to her partner after leaving the shelter.

In other words, staying is often less about love than about what leaving would cost: money, a home, children, standing in the family and community. Each year in the relationship adds to that cost, which is one reason the bond can tighten over time.

There may be a further mental twist. A famous 1959 experiment by Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills found that people who went through an embarrassing “initiation” to join a discussion group later rated a deliberately dull discussion more favourably than people who had joined easily. The explanation offered was effort justification: we find it uncomfortable to conclude that something we suffered for was not worth it. Applied to relationships, the thought “I have given up so much for this person” makes it harder to admit the relationship is harming us. A note of caution, though: a 2013 attempt to repeat the experiment did not find the same effect, so this mechanism is less certain than textbooks often suggest.

Personal qualities that are usually strengths can also play a part. In a 2022 study of 345 women who had sought help at two centres for victims of domestic and sexual violence in Nigeria, those with higher empathy scored higher on a standard measure of trauma bonding. That measure covers excusing the partner and blaming oneself, psychological damage such as losing one’s sense of self, and dependence on the partner’s love. A plausible reading is that the ability to understand an abusive partner’s pain makes it easier to explain away what they do. The study was cross-sectional and relied on self-report, so it cannot show that empathy causes the bond.

Why this matters close to home

None of this is rare. In India’s most recent National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), about 29% of ever-married women aged 18 to 49 reported having experienced physical or sexual violence from a husband. Only around one in seven women who had experienced physical or sexual violence from anyone had sought help.

The investment model helps explain some of that silence. Financial dependence, children, the expectation that a marriage should be endured and worry about the family’s reputation all raise the cost of leaving, whatever a person feels. Understanding the bond as the product of these forces, rather than as a personal failing, is the first step towards responding to it with less shame.

What about Stockholm syndrome?

The popular name for this pattern comes from a 1973 bank robbery in Stockholm, after which some of the hostages were described as sympathetic towards their captors. The label has spread far beyond hostage situations, but its scientific footing is weak. A 2008 systematic review found only 12 relevant papers, most of them individual case reports. The term was used inconsistently, no validated diagnostic criteria existed, and it appeared in no international classification system. It is better seen as a vivid popular label than as a diagnosis.

“Traumatic bonding” is the more useful description, because it names specific ingredients that can be studied. Even so, it has been tested in relatively few studies, mostly using questionnaires at a single point in time. The research has not caught up with how widely the term is now used online.

Leaving is a process, not a moment

Because attachment does not switch off when someone walks out of the door, leaving often happens in stages, sometimes with returns along the way. (You may have seen the claim that it takes seven attempts on average. That figure is widely repeated, but it is hard to trace to any published study.)

Leaving can also be a dangerous time. A large study across 11 cities in the United States compared 220 women who had been killed by a partner with 343 women who had been abused but not killed. Separating from an abusive partner after living together was linked to a higher risk of being killed, especially when the partner was highly controlling. This is not a reason to stay. It is a reason to plan leaving carefully, ideally with people trained to help.

What helps

Understanding why the bond is so strong does not mean excusing the harm. Feeling attached to someone is not evidence that the relationship is good, or that the person has changed. With that in mind:

  • Name the pattern. Recognising that unpredictable kindness, fear, dependence and investment create a pull can make it feel less like fate and more like something that can be understood and resisted.
  • Expect the pull to fade slowly. In the 1993 study, attachment was still strong six months after leaving. Missing someone is not a sign that leaving was a mistake.
  • If you are the friend or family member, judgement adds shame without changing any of the forces described above. What changes the balance is what the investment model points to: alternatives. A place to stay, practical and financial help, and someone who is still there after the third or fifth return.
  • Get skilled support. In India, call 112 in an emergency. The National Commission for Women helpline, 14490 (or 7827170170), supports women aged 18 and over affected by violence, and the national mental health helpline Tele-MANAS is free and available round the clock on 14416. Elsewhere, local domestic violence services can help with safety planning.

The same mechanisms reach beyond romance. Unpredictable approval from a parent, a mentor, a friend or a manager can create the same tug, and the same understanding applies.

The pull towards someone who hurts you is not proof of love, and it is not proof of weakness. It is what attachment, unpredictable reward and heavy investment produce together, and it can loosen with time, distance and support.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Young animals of many species can form and keep bonds with caregivers or objects that cause them pain. In very young rats, pain paired with a smell produces attraction rather than avoidance.
  • Behaviour that has been rewarded only some of the time persists longer, once the rewards stop, than behaviour rewarded every time.
  • Commitment to a relationship, including an abusive one, rises with what a person has invested and falls as their alternatives improve.
  • Attachment to an abusive partner can persist for months after leaving.
  • Separating from a violent, controlling partner is a period of raised risk, which makes safety planning important.

Still uncertain

  • How well “trauma bonding” can be measured, and how much of it comes from learning rather than fear, dependence or practical constraints.
  • How closely the findings in infant rats apply to human infants and adults.
  • Whether effort justification is as strong as the classic 1959 experiment suggested.
  • Why some people form intense bonds with abusive partners and others do not. Empathy and early attachment history are leads, mostly from studies that cannot show cause and effect.
  • How many attempts leaving typically takes. The popular “seven times” figure has no clear published source.
  • “Stockholm syndrome” is not a recognised diagnosis, and the evidence for it as a distinct condition is thin.
