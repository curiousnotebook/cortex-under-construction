Who Said So?

Don’t Sweep After Sunset: The Logic Behind Household Superstitions

No sweeping after dark. No cutting nails at night. No washing hair on certain days. And if a black cat crosses your path, wait. Some of these rules hide a sensible old reason. Others are pure habit. All of them say something about how minds work.

7 min read·17 November 2026

Almost every Indian household has a list of small rules that nobody quite remembers learning. Put the broom away once the lamps are lit. Never cut your nails after dark. Don’t wash your hair on a Tuesday, or a Thursday, or a Saturday, depending on whose grandmother you ask. And if a black cat crosses the road in front of you, stop and let someone else go first.

Ask why, and you usually get one of three answers: “it brings bad luck”, “it drives away Lakshmi”, or simply “that’s how it’s done”. This journal’s question is always the same: who said so, and is there anything behind it?

“Don’t sweep after sunset”

Verdict: no supernatural effect, but a plausible practical past.

In Hindu tradition, the broom is often linked with Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity, who is said to favour clean homes. Sweeping in the early morning is believed to welcome her. Sweeping after dark, the belief goes, sends good fortune out of the door, or lets misfortune in.

Strikingly similar rules appear far from India. During the Chinese New Year, many families avoid sweeping for the first day or several days, so as not to sweep away the good luck that has just arrived. Different gods, same idea: something precious might leave with the dust.

There is a down-to-earth way to read this. Before electric lights, homes after sunset were lit by lamps or not at all. Sweeping in the dark makes it easy to throw out something you can’t see, a dropped earring, a coin, a grain of rice, or to disturb a scorpion or snake sheltering in a corner. A rule that says “sweep in daylight” protects small valuables and bare feet. That explanation is a reasonable guess rather than a documented origin, but it fits the pattern of rules that keep their wisdom long after the reason is forgotten.

“Don’t cut your nails at night”

Verdict: no luck involved, but once genuinely sensible.

This rule is common across India, and it appears in Japan too, where cutting nails at night is said to mean you won’t be with your parents when they die. The spiritual explanations differ from place to place. The practical logic is the same everywhere.

For most of history, nails were trimmed with knives, blades or sharp scissors, not the safe clippers we use today. Doing that in dim lamplight is an easy way to cut skin, and a small wound could become infected. Nail clippings scattered in a dark room can also end up in food or bedding. Today, with good lighting and clippers, there is no reason to wait for morning, though the old instinct to tidy up the clippings is still a good one.

Many household superstitions are safety rules that outlived their reasons. The rule was remembered; the reason was not.

“Don’t wash your hair on these days”

Verdict: myth, with a useful clue hiding in plain sight.

Rules about which weekdays are wrong for washing hair are often tied to astrology and to particular planets or deities. The most telling thing about them is how much they vary. One family avoids Tuesdays, another Thursdays, another Saturdays. Some rules apply only to women, or only to married women. Chinese tradition has its own version: no hair washing on the first day of the New Year, in case good fortune is washed away.

If a rule were about the body, it would not change from one family or region to the next. Hair and scalp behave the same way on a Tuesday as on a Sunday. Variation like this is a sign that a rule is cultural rather than biological. There is no evidence that washing hair on any particular day causes harm.

There is one sensible piece of advice often bundled with these rules: don’t go to bed or out into the cold with very wet hair in winter, when it takes a long time to dry. That is about comfort and temperature, not the day of the week. When rules like these fall mainly on women, it is also worth asking whose convenience they serve (see Period Myths).

“If a black cat crosses your path, stop”

Verdict: myth, and one with real victims.

The black cat is perhaps the most global superstition of all, and also one of the most inconsistent. In much of Europe, black cats were linked with witchcraft in the Middle Ages, and that association helped make them symbols of bad luck. Yet in Britain, a black cat crossing your path is traditionally good luck. In Japan, black cats are often considered lucky. Sailors once wanted black cats on board to bring them safely home. The same animal is a bad sign in one culture and a blessing in another. In India, the usual advice is to wait until someone else has crossed the cat’s path first, but where the belief came from is unclear.

A cat cannot know which way a person is travelling, and no study has found that black cats change anyone’s fortunes. What the superstition can change is the cat’s. A study led by Lori Kogan at Colorado State University examined years of shelter records and found that black cats took several days longer to be adopted than cats of other colours. In another study, people who scored higher on a measure of superstition showed stronger bias against black cats. Other factors may matter too, including that black cats are hard to photograph well and that people find their expressions harder to read.

Why sensible people keep these rules

None of this means people who follow superstitions are foolish. There are good reasons the habits survive.

  • They cost almost nothing. Waiting until morning to sweep or cut your nails takes no real effort. The biologists Kevin Foster and Hanna Kokko have argued that when acting on a belief is cheap and ignoring a real danger is costly, it can make sense to act even when you are usually wrong. Evolution may have built us to over-detect patterns for this reason.
  • We see patterns in coincidence. In a famous 1948 experiment, B. F. Skinner fed hungry pigeons at regular intervals, no matter what they did. Six of the eight pigeons developed repetitive “rituals”, like turning in circles or bobbing their heads, as if their actions had produced the food. People do something similar: one bad day after a cat crossed the road is remembered; the hundred ordinary days are forgotten (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller).
  • They feel like control. In a 2002 study by Giora Keinan, people who wanted a strong sense of control knocked on wood more often when they were under stress. Rituals are most tempting when life feels uncertain.
  • They are part of belonging. Many household rules are passed on with love, and following them is a way of honouring the people who taught them.

Do superstitions actually help? A widely reported 2010 study suggested that good-luck charms and “fingers crossed” improved people’s performance on tasks such as putting a golf ball. When another team repeated one of its key experiments more carefully, the effect did not appear (see The Replication Crisis). Feeling calmer may help some people, but the evidence that luck rituals improve results is weak.

Keep the wisdom, drop the fear

A sensible way to treat these rules is to ask what problem they might once have solved. Sweeping in daylight and trimming nails in good light are still good habits, for practical reasons. Rules with no practical core, like hair-washing days, are harmless if you enjoy them and safe to ignore if you don’t. And when a belief causes harm, to an animal, a person or a choice, it deserves to be let go.

The next time a black cat crosses your path, it is worth remembering that the cat is the only one taking a risk.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • There is no evidence that sweeping, nail cutting or hair washing at particular times affects luck or prosperity.
  • Beliefs about black cats differ widely between cultures, from bad luck to good luck.
  • Black cats can stay longer in shelters before adoption, and superstition is linked with bias against them.
  • Humans and other animals readily form superstitious behaviours from coincidental events.

Still uncertain

  • The true historical origins of these household rules; practical explanations are plausible but rarely documented.
  • Where the Indian black cat belief came from.
  • How much superstition, rather than photography or appearance, explains black cats’ lower adoption.
  • Whether rituals that reduce anxiety can ever improve performance; the best-known study did not replicate.
