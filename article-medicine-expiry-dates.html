What Really Happens to a Medicine After Its Expiry Date?

The US military found that many stockpiled drugs were still potent years past expiry, saving it a fortune. So is the date on your strip of tablets meaningless? Not quite. It is a promise, not a cliff edge, and the difference matters.

10 min read·17 December 2026

Almost every home has one: a drawer of old medicine strips, half-used bottles of cough syrup and a tube of ointment from some forgotten rash. Late one night you have a headache, find a strip of painkillers, and notice the expiry date passed last spring. Does the medicine turn useless at midnight on that date? Does it become poisonous? Or is the date just a way of making you buy more?

The honest answer is: none of the above, exactly. To understand why, it helps to know how that date is set, and what happened when the US military decided to test it.

What the date actually promises

Before a medicine is approved, its manufacturer must show how stable it is. Under international guidelines agreed by regulators in Europe, Japan and the United States, known as ICH Q1A, samples from production batches are stored for long periods under controlled conditions, typically 25°C with 60% humidity, or 30°C with 65% humidity, and tested at regular intervals. Other samples are stored at a harsher 40°C and 75% humidity to speed up any breakdown and reveal weaknesses early.

The tests check not only how much active ingredient remains, but also whether harmful breakdown products build up, whether tablets still dissolve properly, and whether the medicine looks and behaves as it should. A loss of 5% of the active ingredient from its starting value counts as a “significant change”.

The expiry date is the point up to which the manufacturer has shown, with data, that the medicine will stay within its approved specification if it is stored as the label says. It is a guarantee, backed by testing, and it stops where the testing stops. It does not tell you when the medicine actually fails. A company that has tested a product for three years can only promise three years, even if the medicine would have lasted much longer.

An expiry date marks the end of a guarantee, not the moment a medicine goes bad. Before that date, the manufacturer promises quality. After it, nobody does.

The military experiment

Armies keep huge stockpiles of medicines for wars and emergencies, and most of those medicines are never used. Throwing them away on their expiry dates was enormously expensive. So in 1986, after a request from the US Air Force, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Department of Defense began the Shelf Life Extension Program. Every year, batches from the stockpile are sent to the FDA for testing. If a batch is still within specification, its expiry is extended.

In 2006, researchers working on the programme published an analysis of 20 years of the programme: 122 different medicines and 3,005 individual batches. Of those batches, 88% were extended by at least a year beyond their original expiry date, and the average extension was 66 months, about five and a half years. Some products failed early, and the extra shelf life varied widely, even between batches of the same medicine. The authors concluded that many medicines can last well beyond their labelled dates if stored properly, but that this can only be confirmed by testing each batch.

The money is striking. According to a 2017 investigation by the news organisation ProPublica, the Department of Defense reported that in 2016 the programme cost about $3.1 million and spared it from replacing about $2.1 billion worth of medicines. The FDA has used similar reasoning in emergencies. For example, it has said that certain stockpiled batches of the antiviral Tamiflu may be used up to 20 years after manufacture, if they were stored as labelled.

There is an important catch. These stockpiles are kept in controlled warehouses, and every batch is tested before its life is extended. The programme is open only to government stockpiles. It is a scientific process, not a general licence to ignore expiry dates.

Medicines found 30 years too late

Two small studies from California show what can happen when old medicines are simply left on a shelf.

In 2012, a team led by the pharmacist Lee Cantrell analysed eight prescription medicines that had expired between 28 and 40 years earlier and had been left unopened in their original containers. Of the 14 active ingredients they measured, 86% of the results still showed at least 90% of the labelled amount, which is within the normal acceptable range. Some did not hold up: aspirin and amphetamine consistently fell below 90%. Three ingredients were actually above their labelled strength, which the researchers suggested may reflect looser manufacturing standards before modern quality rules.

In 2017, a similar team tested 40 adrenaline auto-injectors, the pens people with severe allergies carry for emergencies, which had expired between one and 50 months earlier. All still contained at least 80% of the stated dose, and most contained at least 90%. But the amount of adrenaline fell steadily the longer a pen was past its date. For a device that may have to save a life in minutes, that decline matters.

So why not just ignore the date?

Because the studies above describe medicines that were stored well, tested carefully, or both. Your drawer is a different story. Four problems stand out.

1. You do not know how your medicine was stored

Heat, moisture and light speed up chemical breakdown. The FDA warns that a humid space such as a bathroom cabinet can weaken a medicine even before its expiry date. In a hot, humid climate like much of India’s, a strip that has spent a summer in a car, a kitchen near the stove or a damp bathroom may have degraded far faster than one in a climate-controlled warehouse. The expiry date assumes you followed the storage instructions on the box. Once you haven’t, the date itself is less reliable.

2. Some medicines really do break down

Not all medicines are equally sturdy. Solid tablets and capsules are generally the most stable. Liquids are more fragile: they are less protected than tablets and can become contaminated with bacteria or fungi once opened. Insulin and many other biological medicines need refrigeration and can lose potency if they get warm, often with no visible sign. In the military programme, some products failed testing, including the asthma medicine albuterol (salbutamol), an antihistamine skin spray and a local anaesthetic. Aspirin, as the Californian study showed, breaks down more readily than many other drugs.

3. For some medicines, “slightly weaker” is not good enough

For a mild headache, a painkiller at 85% strength is a minor inconvenience. For other medicines, it can be serious. A weakened antibiotic may fail to clear an infection, and the FDA warns that this can lead to more serious illness and help bacteria become resistant. An emergency medicine such as an adrenaline pen needs its full dose at the moment it is used. And for medicines where small changes in dose make a big difference, a drop in strength can upset a carefully balanced treatment.

4. Rarely, old medicine can cause harm

Most expired medicines simply become weaker. But there is one famous exception. In 1963, a doctor reported serious kidney damage, a condition called Fanconi syndrome, in a patient who had taken old, degraded tetracycline, an antibiotic. The harm was blamed on substances formed as the drug broke down. It remains the best-known example of an expired medicine becoming toxic, and it is often cited precisely because such cases are rare. Rare is not the same as impossible.

Most old tablets lose strength rather than becoming dangerous. But you cannot see how much strength is left, and for antibiotics, insulin, emergency medicines and liquids, that uncertainty matters.

Opened is not the same as unopened

The printed expiry date applies to a sealed pack. Once you open some products, a second, shorter clock starts. Eye drops, many syrups and suspensions, and insulin pens often carry instructions such as “discard a set number of days after opening”, because once the seal is broken, air, moisture and germs can get in. A bottle of eye drops opened months ago may be within its printed date and still unsafe to use. Always check the label or leaflet for an “after opening” instruction.

A system built for safety, at a price

Expiry dates were made compulsory on US medicines in 1979, and similar rules exist almost everywhere, including India, where every pack must show its manufacturing date, expiry date and batch number. The system is deliberately cautious. That caution protects patients, but it also creates waste. The ProPublica investigation estimated that US hospitals throw away hundreds of millions of dollars of expired medicines each year, and asked whether more of them could be tested and kept, as the military does. The FDA has not adopted that idea for ordinary use.

The trade-off is the familiar one: safety and simplicity on one side, cost and waste on the other. Somebody pays either way (see The Economics of Free Things). For an individual patient, though, there is no testing laboratory, and the printed date is the only guarantee available.

Getting rid of old medicines safely

Expired medicines should not simply be tossed into the bin or poured down the drain, where they can harm children, animals or the environment. In May 2025, India’s Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) issued guidance on disposing of expired and unused medicines. It encourages “drug take-back” sites where people can return old medicines, lists certain medicines that should be flushed away because they are especially dangerous if found by others, and requires pharmacies and wholesalers to return or properly dispose of expired stock. Where no take-back option exists near you, ask your pharmacist what to do.

A practical guide

  • Do not use expired antibiotics, insulin, emergency medicines such as adrenaline pens or inhalers, liquids or eye drops. These are the cases where weakness or contamination matters most.
  • Store medicines in a cool, dry place, in their original packaging, away from bathrooms, stoves, sunny windows and car dashboards. Refrigerate only if the label says so.
  • Follow “after opening” instructions, which can be much shorter than the printed expiry date.
  • Check dates before you need them. Go through your medicine box every few months, especially emergency medicines, and replace what has expired.
  • Never buy or accept a medicine that is already past its date, and check the date at the pharmacy counter.
  • If you are unsure, ask a pharmacist. If you have taken an expired medicine by mistake, most of the time the main risk is that it worked less well, but a pharmacist or doctor can tell you whether anything needs to be done.

The research tells a reassuring story about chemistry: many medicines are far tougher than their dates suggest. But it also explains why the date still deserves respect. It is the only promise anyone has made about the medicine in your hand.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Expiry dates are set by stability testing and mark the end of the period the manufacturer has proven, not the point at which a medicine fails.
  • When stored under controlled conditions, many medicines remain within specification for years beyond their labelled dates, but this varies between batches.
  • Heat and humidity speed up degradation; liquids, insulin and other biological medicines are more fragile than tablets.
  • Most degraded medicines lose strength rather than becoming toxic, though rare harmful cases have been documented.

Still uncertain

  • How long specific medicines last in real homes, especially in hot, humid climates; most studies tested stockpiles or sealed packs.
  • How much potency loss is clinically important for each medicine.
  • Whether testing-based extensions could safely be used more widely, for example in hospitals, to cut waste.
