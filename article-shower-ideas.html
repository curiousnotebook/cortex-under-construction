Why Ideas Come in the Shower: The Science of Stepping Away

You struggle with a problem for hours, give up, and the answer arrives while you are washing your hair. Research on “incubation” suggests why stepping away can help, and why it only works if you did the hard thinking first.

7 min read·29 November 2026

Almost everyone has had this experience. You spend an evening stuck on a problem, a sentence that won’t come out right, a bug in your code, a decision you can’t make. You give up and go to bed. The next morning, in the shower or on the walk to the bus stop, the answer simply appears.

It feels like magic, or like your mind was working while you weren’t. Psychologists have a name for this pause: incubation. And over the past century, they have tested whether it really helps, and why.

Famous moments, and a four-stage map

The most famous example is probably not true. The story of Archimedes leaping from his bath shouting “Eureka!” after realising how to test a king’s crown comes from the Roman writer Vitruvius, who told it roughly two centuries after Archimedes died. Archimedes himself never described it, and historians suspect the tale was embellished.

A better-documented case comes from the French mathematician Henri Poincaré. In an essay published in 1908, he described how, after a long stretch of work on a problem, he left on a geological excursion and stopped thinking about mathematics. “At the moment when I put my foot on the step” of a bus, he wrote, the key idea came to him, “without anything in my former thoughts seeming to have paved the way for it.”

In 1926, the English writer Graham Wallas, drawing partly on Poincaré’s account, described creative thinking in four stages:

  • Preparation: working hard on the problem, gathering information and trying solutions.
  • Incubation: setting the problem aside and not consciously working on it.
  • Illumination: the sudden “aha” moment when an idea arrives.
  • Verification: checking whether the idea actually works.

Wallas’s map was based on stories, not experiments. The question for later researchers was whether the second stage really does anything.

Does stepping away actually help?

On average, yes, modestly. In 2009, the psychologists Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod pooled 117 laboratory studies of incubation. People given a break before returning to a problem did better than people who kept working without one. The effect was real but small to moderate, not a miracle.

The details mattered. The benefit was larger when people had spent longer working on the problem before the break, and it was strongest for open-ended tasks, such as thinking of as many uses as possible for a brick, where there is no single right answer. And what people did during the break mattered: a mentally demanding task reduced the benefit, while a light, undemanding activity often helped.

Incubation is not a replacement for hard thinking. It works best after it.

Why might a break help?

Researchers have several explanations, and the evidence has not settled on one.

  • Letting go of a wrong path. When we get stuck, we often keep returning to the same unhelpful approach, a problem called fixation. Stepping away can let that misleading idea fade, so we return with a fresh look. Experiments that deliberately planted misleading hints found that people were more likely to solve the problem once they had forgotten the hints.
  • Unconscious work. The mind may keep processing the problem in the background, connecting ideas that conscious effort missed. Some studies found that even a break taken immediately, before people could get stuck, still helped on open-ended tasks, which fits this explanation.
  • Secretly thinking about it. A simpler idea is that people keep thinking about the problem during the break. Experiments designed to detect this have generally not found much evidence for it.

These explanations may all be partly right, for different kinds of problems.

So why the shower?

Showers, walks and washing dishes share something: they keep part of the mind busy without demanding much from it. That leaves room for the mind to wander.

A 2012 study by Benjamin Baird, Jonathan Schooler and colleagues became famous for supporting this idea. People who did an easy, mind-wandering task during a break came up with more uses for everyday objects than people who did a demanding task, rested, or had no break. But later research was less kind. In a larger test published in 2024, with 443 participants across two studies, Samuel Murray, Paul Seli and colleagues found no evidence that mind wandering during the break improved creativity. As the replication crisis has shown, striking single findings often shrink when retested.

The philosopher Zac Irving and his colleagues proposed a refinement, which they called “the shower effect”. In their study, people watched either a boring video of two men folding laundry or a moderately engaging clip from a film before returning to a creative task. Mind wandering was linked to more ideas only during the engaging video. The authors suggest that the best activities hold our attention lightly, like a shower, rather than boring us completely. It is an interesting idea, but it comes from one study with videos, not actual showers.

Walking may work in a similar way. In 2014, Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz at Stanford found that people produced more creative ideas while walking, even on a treadmill facing a blank wall, than while sitting. But walking did not help with problems that have one correct answer.

What about “sleeping on it”?

Sleep has some of the most striking evidence. In a 2004 study in Nature, Ullrich Wagner, Jan Born and colleagues gave people a number task with a hidden shortcut. After a night’s sleep, more than twice as many people discovered the shortcut as after staying awake for the same time. Crucially, sleep did not help people who had not practised the task beforehand.

In 2021, researchers in Paris took inspiration from a habit attributed to Thomas Edison, who was said to nap holding an object so that he would wake as it dropped. Participants held a light bottle while resting. Those who drifted into the very first, lightest stage of sleep were far more likely to find the hidden shortcut later, about 83% compared with about 31% of those who stayed awake. People who fell into deeper sleep did no better than those who stayed awake. The groups were small, though, and the participants had been chosen because they fell asleep easily.

Not every study agrees. Several experiments have found that sleep gives no extra benefit for some kinds of problems, beyond a break of equal length. The safest conclusion is that sleep helps some kinds of insight, in some conditions.

A related claim has fared worse. A popular idea from the mid-2000s held that people make better complex decisions after being distracted than after careful thought. A large replication with 399 participants, together with a review of earlier studies, found no reliable evidence for this. Stepping away may help creative ideas surface, but it is not a shortcut to better judgement.

How to use it

  • Do the hard part first. Every line of evidence points the same way: incubation helps after real effort, not instead of it.
  • Take a real break when you are stuck. If you keep circling the same failed approach, stepping away may help more than pushing on.
  • Choose something light. A walk, a shower or washing up seems better than scrolling or another hard task.
  • Catch the idea. Keep a notebook or your phone nearby. Ideas that arrive suddenly can disappear just as fast.
  • Check it later. Wallas’s final stage matters. A shower idea feels brilliant, but it still needs testing.

This connects to how learning works more generally: spacing out study and coming back to material often beats cramming (see Forgetting Is Not the Enemy). And it is not the same as waiting for inspiration to strike; that strategy tends to fail (see Why Motivation Is an Unreliable Strategy).

The shower is not magic. It is simply one of the few moments in a busy day when we stop pushing, after having pushed hard enough for something to surface.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • On average, taking a break from a problem modestly improves later problem solving in laboratory studies.
  • The benefit is larger after a longer period of initial work, and for open-ended tasks.
  • Demanding tasks during the break tend to reduce the benefit.
  • Walking improves performance on open-ended creative tasks, though not on single-answer problems.

Still uncertain

  • Why incubation works: forgetting wrong paths, unconscious processing, or both.
  • Whether mind wandering itself is the active ingredient; key findings have not replicated well.
  • How much sleep, and which stage of sleep, helps insight, and for which problems.
  • Whether laboratory tasks reflect the larger, real-life problems people solve at work or in research.
