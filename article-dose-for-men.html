Why the Dose on the Box Was Set for Men

For decades, medicines were developed mainly in men and male animals. A sleeping pill taken by millions shows what that could cost, and why the question is more interesting than “women are smaller”.

7 min read·13 November 2026

Zolpidem, sold in the United States as Ambien, is one of the world’s most widely used sleeping pills. For two decades after its approval, men and women were given the same starting dose. Then, in January 2013, the US Food and Drug Administration told manufacturers to cut the recommended dose for women in half: from 10 mg to 5 mg for the standard tablet, and from 12.5 mg to 6.25 mg for the slow-release version.

The reason was the morning after. Driving-simulator and laboratory studies measured how much zolpidem was still in people’s blood eight hours after a dose. On the old standard dose, about 15% of women, but only 3% of men, still had levels high enough to impair driving. With the slow-release version, the figures were 33% of women and 25% of men. Women clear the drug from their bodies more slowly, so many were getting behind the wheel with a sleeping pill still at work.

It is a striking story. But it is not really a story about one pill. It is about how medicines have been tested, and who they were tested on.

How women went missing from drug trials

In the mid-twentieth century, drugs such as thalidomide had caused serious harm to babies exposed in the womb. Regulators became understandably cautious. In 1977, the FDA issued guidance that kept “women of childbearing potential” out of the earliest stages of drug testing, the phase I and early phase II trials where doses are first worked out. In practice, this excluded most women between their teens and their fifties, whether or not they were pregnant or planned to be.

The intention was protective. The effect was that much of the basic information about how a new drug behaves in the body, how fast it is absorbed, broken down and cleared, came mostly from men. Women could join later trials, but they were often underrepresented there too. It was not until 1993 that the FDA reversed its guidance, and the same year the US NIH Revitalization Act required federally funded clinical research to include women.

The male mouse problem

The bias began even before human trials. Most medicines are first studied in animals, usually mice and rats, and researchers have long preferred males. One reason given was that female hormone cycles would make results more variable. When Annaliese Beery and Irving Zucker reviewed studies published in 2009 across ten biological fields, they found a male bias in eight of them. In neuroscience, studies using only male animals outnumbered those using only females by 5.5 to 1. The worry about variability, they noted, was not supported by the evidence.

In 2016, the US National Institutes of Health began requiring funded researchers to consider sex as a biological variable, using both males and females unless there was a strong reason not to. A follow-up study found that by 2019, 49% of studies across these fields included both sexes, up from 28% in 2009. That is real progress, though many studies that included both sexes still did not report whether the results differed between them.

A study that includes women is not the same as a study that checks whether the results differ for women.

What happened when medicines reached everyone

In 2001, the US General Accounting Office (now the Government Accountability Office) looked at the ten prescription drugs withdrawn from the American market between 1997 and 2000. Eight of them posed greater health risks for women than for men. For four, this may partly have been because women were prescribed them more often. For the other four, which were widely used by both sexes, women still had more serious side effects. Three of those caused a dangerous heart rhythm problem called torsades de pointes, to which women are more vulnerable, partly because of natural differences in the heart’s electrical timing.

A larger pattern emerged in 2020. Irving Zucker and Brian Prendergast examined 86 medicines for which there was clear evidence that men’s and women’s bodies handled the drug differently. For 76 of them, women had higher levels of the drug in their blood or cleared it more slowly. For almost all of those drugs, 96%, women also had higher rates of side effects. The authors argued that this pattern was not simply explained by differences in body weight, and suggested that for drugs like these, women could be started on lower doses and increased only if needed.

Same pill, different result

Differences are not only about dose. They can be about whether a medicine works at all.

In 1989, the Physicians’ Health Study of 22,071 American male doctors found that taking aspirin every other day reduced the risk of a first heart attack by 44%. For years, aspirin was widely recommended to prevent heart disease. In 2005, a trial in almost 40,000 women aged 45 and over gave a different answer. In women, a low dose of aspirin did not reduce heart attacks overall. It did slightly reduce strokes, and it increased serious stomach bleeding. The same pill, studied properly in women, had a different balance of benefit and harm.

The gaps have not fully closed. A 2020 analysis of 740 cardiovascular trials run between 2010 and 2017 found that women made up about 38% of participants. That is a large improvement on the past, but heart disease is a leading cause of death in women, and for conditions such as heart failure and coronary heart disease, women remained underrepresented compared with how often they actually have them.

Is it just about size?

An obvious objection is that women are, on average, smaller, so of course a fixed dose hits harder. Body weight is part of the story, and some researchers have argued it explains much of the zolpidem case. Others disagree. Studies of zolpidem have found that women clear the drug more slowly even after accounting for weight, and Zucker and Prendergast concluded that weight did not explain the wider pattern.

The most likely answer is that several things matter at once: body size, body fat, liver enzymes, kidney function and hormones. Some of these differ on average between men and women, but they also vary enormously between individuals. A small man and a large woman may be more alike than two women. Sex is a useful clue, not a complete explanation, which is exactly why it needs to be studied rather than assumed away.

None of this means every medicine needs a separate dose for women. For many drugs, the differences are small or haven’t been found. The problem is that for a long time, nobody looked carefully enough to know. Even well-designed research can miss what it never measures (see The Replication Crisis).

What this means for you

  • Never change a dose on your own. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist if you think a medicine is too strong or not working.
  • Mention side effects, even if they seem minor. Next-morning drowsiness, dizziness or palpitations are worth reporting, especially with sleeping pills, sedatives or heart medicines.
  • Ask whether your weight, age or other medicines should affect the dose. It is a reasonable question for any long-term medicine.
  • Take extra care with sleeping tablets. Don’t drive or do anything that needs full alertness until you know how the medicine affects you the next morning.
  • Report suspected side effects. In India, patients can report them to the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India on the free helpline 1800-180-3024 or through the “ADR PvPI” app. These reports help regulators spot problems that trials missed.

The timing of a dose can matter for some medicines too (see Does the Time You Take a Medicine Matter?). And how a medicine is presented and expected to work can shape what people feel (see The Placebo Puzzle).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Women were excluded from early-stage drug trials in the US from 1977 until 1993.
  • Male animals have been studied far more often than females in many fields of biology.
  • Women clear zolpidem more slowly, and the recommended dose for women was halved in 2013.
  • For many drugs with known sex differences in how they are handled, women experience more side effects.
  • Some treatments, such as aspirin for preventing heart disease, have different benefits and harms in women and men.

Still uncertain

  • How much of each difference is due to sex itself, and how much to body weight, body fat and other factors.
  • Which medicines would benefit from different starting doses for women and men.
  • Whether weight-based dosing would be simpler and fairer for many drugs.
  • How well these findings, mostly from Western populations, apply to people in India and elsewhere.
