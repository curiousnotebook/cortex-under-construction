The Placebo Puzzle: What Sugar Pills Can (and Can’t) Do

Placebos are often described as “mind over matter.” The real story is stranger, more limited, and far more interesting.

6 min read·18 October 2026

In almost every clinical trial, something curious happens. A group of patients is given a tablet with no active drug in it, a “sugar pill,” and many of them get better. Their pain eases, their nausea settles, their mood lifts. It is tempting to conclude that belief alone can heal. But when researchers look closely, the picture becomes more subtle, and more useful.

Not everything that improves is a placebo effect

Imagine you develop a bad headache and take a pill. An hour later the headache is gone. Did the pill work? Maybe. But headaches often fade on their own. This is the first trap: natural history. Many conditions get better, or fluctuate, with time.

The second trap is called regression to the mean. People usually seek treatment, or join a trial, when their symptoms are at their worst. Simply by chance, a bad day tends to be followed by a less bad one. Measure people at their peak and you will often see improvement later, whatever you give them.

The third trap is how we report. Patients who know they are being watched, or who want to please a kind clinician, may describe their symptoms a little more positively.

So when patients improve in the placebo group of a trial, researchers call that the placebo response. Only part of it is the placebo effect: the improvement actually caused by the experience of being treated, such as the ritual, the expectation and the care. To tell the two apart, you need a third group that receives no treatment at all.

A placebo group getting better does not prove that placebos are powerful. It shows that people get better for many reasons, and only some of them are caused by the act of treatment itself.

When the placebo effect is real, it can be biological

That said, some placebo effects are genuine and measurable. One of the most famous experiments was published in 1978. Researchers studied people with pain after dental surgery. Some patients reported less pain after receiving a placebo. When those patients were then given naloxone, a drug that blocks the body’s opioid receptors, their placebo pain relief faded. The implication was striking: expecting relief seemed to trigger the brain’s own opioid-like chemicals.

Since then, brain imaging and pharmacology studies have mapped how expectation and learning (for example, having taken real painkillers before) can change pain processing in the brain. This is not “imagined” relief. For pain in particular, expectation appears to switch on real biological machinery.

Where placebos fall short

The most thorough attempt to measure the true placebo effect is a Cochrane systematic review that pooled trials comparing placebo with no treatment across many conditions. Its authors found no evidence that placebos generally have large clinical effects. There may be modest benefits for outcomes that patients report themselves, such as pain and nausea, though even these are hard to separate from reporting bias.

A clever 2011 asthma trial shows why this distinction matters. Patients received, on different visits, a real inhaled drug (albuterol, also called salbutamol), a dummy inhaler, sham acupuncture, or nothing at all. Patients said they felt considerably better after both placebo treatments, almost as much as after the real drug. But when their lung function was measured objectively, only the real medicine produced a substantial improvement.

In other words, the placebos changed how people felt about their breathing, but not how well their airways actually worked. For a condition like asthma, that gap can be dangerous: feeling better while your lungs remain narrowed could delay the treatment you need.

The surprise of honest placebos

Can a placebo work if you know it is a placebo? A small 2010 trial in people with irritable bowel syndrome tested exactly this. Participants were openly told they were receiving inert pills, but that placebos had shown benefits in earlier studies. After three weeks, this group reported more improvement in symptoms than a group that received no pills.

It’s a fascinating result, and it has inspired more research. But it deserves caution. The trial was small and short, its outcomes were self-reported, and participants couldn’t be “blinded,” because everyone knew what they were getting. Whether open-label placebos have lasting, meaningful benefits in everyday care is still an open question.

The placebo’s shadow: nocebo

Expectation works in both directions. When people expect harm, they can experience real symptoms, a phenomenon called the nocebo effect. In many trials, some participants taking dummy pills report side effects such as headache or tiredness, often resembling the side effects they were warned about. This creates a genuine dilemma for clinicians: patients must be told about possible side effects, yet the way this is done may influence what they experience.

Why this matters

Understanding placebos explains why modern medicine relies on randomised, blinded, placebo-controlled trials. Without a comparison group, the natural course of illness, regression to the mean and expectation would all masquerade as drug effects. It also reminds us that the context of care, including trust, clear communication and kindness, is not trivial. It can genuinely shape how patients feel, even if it cannot replace effective treatment.

There is a deeper lesson, too. Our sense of whether something “worked” is built from memory and expectation, which are less reliable than they feel (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller). And interpreting any single result means weighing it against what was likely beforehand (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Improvement in placebo groups has several causes, including natural history and regression to the mean.
  • In some settings, placebo pain relief involves the body’s own opioid system and can be reversed by naloxone.
  • Placebo effects are largest for self-reported symptoms, such as pain and nausea, not objective measures.
  • Negative expectations can produce real symptoms (nocebo effects).

Still uncertain

  • How big the “true” placebo effect is once bias is fully removed.
  • Whether open-label placebos provide lasting, worthwhile benefit in routine care.
  • Why some people respond strongly to placebos and others hardly at all.
