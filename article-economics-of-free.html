The Economics of Free Things: Is Anything Really Free?

Free apps, free trials, free payments, free data. Someone always pays for “free”, but not always the person you expect, not always in money, and not always in a way that is bad for you.

11 min read·11 December 2026

In the late 1800s, many American saloons put out a “free lunch”: a midday spread of food for anyone who bought a drink. The food was not really free. It was a way to bring customers through the door and sell them more drinks, and people at the time knew it. By 1938, an American newspaper fable had boiled the whole of economics down to a single line: there ain’t no such thing as a free lunch. The novelist Robert Heinlein popularised the phrase in 1966, and the economist Milton Friedman used it as the title of a 1975 book.

The saying is usually taken to mean that free things are a trick. The real lesson is more useful. Everything that is produced uses up resources, so someone pays. The interesting questions are who pays, in what currency, and when. Sometimes the answer is a bad deal for you. Sometimes it is a very good one.

Zero is not just a low price

Start with a strange fact about our minds. In a 2007 study, the behavioural economists Kristina Shampanier, Nina Mazar and Dan Ariely set up a stall at a university and offered passers-by one chocolate each: a cheap Hershey’s Kiss or a much better Lindt truffle. When the Kiss cost 1 cent and the truffle 15 cents, about 73% of those who took a chocolate chose the truffle. Then the researchers cut both prices by exactly 1 cent. The Kiss became free and the truffle cost 14 cents. The difference in price was unchanged, yet now about 69% chose the Kiss.

Standard economics says an equal cut to both prices should not change which one you prefer. But zero behaved like a special price. The researchers’ evidence suggested that “free” does not just lower the cost of something in our minds; it adds a little glow of positive feeling, as if there were no downside at all. When participants were asked to think through the choice carefully first, the effect shrank.

Businesses know this. A product priced at zero attracts far more people than one priced at almost zero, which is why so much of modern commerce begins with the word “free”. Price shapes what we expect from something in other ways too (see More Than the Molecule).

“Free” is not just the bottom of the price scale. It switches off our sense of a downside, which is exactly why it is used to get our attention.

Four ways to pay for a free thing

If the price at the counter is zero, the cost has gone somewhere else. It usually goes to one of four places.

1. You pay with attention and data

Search engines, social media and most free apps are paid for by advertisers, who are buying access to the people using them. In 2025, Meta, the company behind Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, reported revenue of about $201 billion, of which about $196 billion, or nearly 98%, came from advertising. It reported an average of 3.58 billion people using at least one of its apps each day in December 2025. Dividing one by the other gives a rough sense of scale: on the order of $56 of revenue a year for every daily user.

This idea is older than the internet. A 1973 short film by the artists Richard Serra and Carlota Fay Schoolman, Television Delivers People, argued that the real product of commercial television was its audience, sold to advertisers. In 2010 a commenter on the website MetaFilter put it in the form now quoted everywhere: if you are not paying for it, you are not the customer; you are the product being sold.

The currency here is attention. As the economist and psychologist Herbert Simon observed back in 1971, information consumes the attention of the people who receive it, so a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. Your time and focus are limited, and many free services are designed to capture as much of them as possible.

2. You pay later

The free trial is the classic version. The first month costs nothing, and then the subscription renews automatically unless you remember to cancel. Many people do not.

A 2023 study by the economists Liran Einav, Benjamin Klopack and Neale Mahoney used a large set of payment-card data to measure how much of subscription revenue depends on people simply not paying attention. One clever test used the moment when a customer’s bank card was replaced. A card replacement forces people to re-enter their details, which makes them notice the subscription. At that moment, many people cancelled. In their data, retention after a year fell from about 75% to about 52% when cards were replaced. Depending on the setting, they estimated that inattention raises sellers’ revenue by anything from about 14% to more than double.

3. You pay somewhere else on the bill

The saloon’s free lunch was a loss leader: something given away, or sold below cost, to sell something else. “Free delivery above a certain amount” works the same way, nudging you to add items to reach the threshold. So does an app that is free to download but needs in-app purchases to be useful.

Some of these practices cross the line into deception. In November 2023, India’s Central Consumer Protection Authority notified guidelines listing 13 “dark patterns”, online designs that trick or pressure users. They include the subscription trap, which makes cancelling a subscription impossible or unreasonably hard, such as asking for payment details for a “free” plan and then debiting automatically; and drip pricing, which reveals the true cost only gradually, such as advertising an app as free without saying that continued use requires paid purchases.

4. Someone else pays

Many free things are paid for by people who are not using them at that moment: taxpayers, other customers, or investors willing to lose money for a while.

India’s UPI payments are a clear example. Since January 2020, UPI payments to merchants have carried no merchant discount rate (MDR), the small fee that card payments usually carry to cover the cost of running the system. The system still costs money. Part of the gap has been filled by the central government, which pays banks an incentive on small payments to small merchants: a scheme worth ₹1,500 crore was approved for 2024–25, and the Union Budget for 2026–27 set aside ₹2,000 crore for UPI and RuPay incentives. Banks and payment companies have absorbed the rest, which is why industry groups have pressed for a fee to return.

The arrangement has been under pressure. In September 2026, a fee of 0.4% on larger UPI payments to merchants, above ₹2,000 and capped at ₹300, was announced for 15 October. Ordinary transfers between people, and payments of ₹2,000 or less, were to stay free. In mid-October the National Payments Corporation of India postponed the fee until after Diwali, with a new date to be announced. However it ends, the debate is about the question this essay keeps returning to: if a service costs money to run, who should pay for it?

Free as a weapon: India’s data revolution

Sometimes “free” is used to win a market. When Reliance Jio launched its 4G mobile service in September 2016, it offered free calls and data, an offer later extended to the end of March 2017. A fierce price war followed across the industry.

The results changed how India uses the internet. According to the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, the average price of a gigabyte of mobile data fell from about ₹269 in 2014 to under ₹12 by 2018, a drop of about 95%, while the amount of mobile data used rose more than fiftyfold. Over the same years, several operators exited the market or merged. Consumers gained enormously from cheap data. The cost was carried first by the investors who funded years of low prices, and in the longer run by a market with fewer competitors left to keep prices in check.

Free does not mean worthless

Because free things carry no price, they are almost invisible in official statistics such as GDP, which mostly count what is bought and sold. That can make them seem unimportant. They are not.

In 2019, the economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Avinash Collis and Felix Eggers ran huge online surveys asking people how much money they would need to be paid to give up a free digital service for a year. In their US results, the typical person would have needed about $17,500 to give up search engines, about $8,400 to give up email and about $3,600 to give up digital maps. For Facebook, typical answers in the US were around $40 to $50 to give it up for a month. These are survey answers, not market prices, and people may overstate them. But they suggest that the value people get from free services can far exceed what those services cost to provide.

When free beats cheap

A popular belief holds that people do not value what they get for free, so even a small charge makes them use things more carefully. In some markets that may be true. In public health, careful experiments have often found the opposite.

In Kenya, the economists Jessica Cohen and Pascaline Dupas offered pregnant women insecticide-treated bed nets, which protect against malaria, at prices ranging from free to modest charges. Raising the price from zero to just $0.60 cut take-up by about 60 percentage points. Women who paid were no more likely to actually use their nets than women who got them free, and paying did not steer the nets towards women who needed them most. Because bed nets also protect neighbours by reducing malaria in the community, free distribution came out as the more cost-effective approach.

A similar study of deworming pills for schoolchildren in Kenya, by Michael Kremer and Edward Miguel, found that introducing even a small fee cut treatment rates by about 80%. The jump from free to a small price mattered far more than the size of the price. This is the zero-price effect working in the public interest.

For goods that benefit the wider community, such as bed nets or deworming pills, making them free can be the smartest use of money. “People don’t value free things” is a claim to test, not a law.

The “freebies” debate

The same questions sit at the heart of a long-running political argument in India over “freebies”: free electricity, free bus travel, cash transfers, loan waivers and similar promises made by governments and parties.

A 2022 article in the Reserve Bank of India’s bulletin noted that there is no precise definition of a freebie. It distinguished them from public and “merit” goods, such as the public distribution system, employment guarantee schemes, and support for education and health, which it said bring wider economic benefits. For items it classed as freebies, it estimated costs of up to 2.7% of a state’s economic output, described them as a source of fiscal risk, and recommended shifting spending towards health, education and infrastructure. In 2022, the Supreme Court of India referred petitions on election-time freebies to a three-judge bench, noting both the fiscal concerns raised and the role of voters in judging parties’ promises.

Supporters of many of these schemes argue that what critics call a freebie is often welfare by another name, with real benefits for health, education and women’s independence, and that similar subsidies to businesses are rarely labelled freebies. Critics argue that spending without clear goals or limits can burden future budgets and displace investment. The economic tools in this essay do not settle the argument, but they sharpen it: who pays, when, at what cost to other uses of the money, and what benefits flow to people beyond the direct recipients?

How to think about the next free offer

“There is no such thing as a free lunch” is a good starting point, as long as it leads to better questions rather than automatic suspicion:

  • Who is paying? Advertisers, other customers, taxpayers, investors, or you at a later date?
  • In what currency? Money, attention, personal data, or time spent cancelling?
  • When? Now, after a trial, or once the competition has gone?
  • How easy is it to leave? A free service you can walk away from is very different from one built to trap you.
  • Who else benefits? Some free things, such as vaccines and bed nets, are worth giving away because their benefits spread to everyone.

Nothing is free in the sense of costing nothing. But plenty of things are free in the sense that matters to you: you get more than you give. The skill is telling the two apart.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • A price of zero changes behaviour far more than a tiny positive price, in shops and in public-health programmes alike.
  • Most “free” online services are funded by advertising, which depends on users’ attention and data.
  • Automatically renewing subscriptions earn significant revenue from customers who are not paying attention.
  • For some health products with community-wide benefits, free distribution increases use without reducing how well people use them.

Still uncertain

  • How much free digital services are really worth to people. Survey answers about what it would take to give them up may be overstated.
  • Whether results from Kenyan health programmes carry over to other products, places and prices.
  • The best way to fund UPI over the long term, and how the new merchant fee will play out.
  • Which government schemes are wise investments and which are unsustainable giveaways. This depends on values and on evidence that is still being gathered.
