Penicillin’s Forgotten Second Discovery

Everyone knows the mould on Fleming’s plate. Far fewer know that penicillin then sat unused for a decade, until a small Oxford team, working with bedpans, milk churns and eight mice, discovered it all over again.

10 min read·19 December 2026

The story is told in almost every science textbook. In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned to his laboratory at St Mary’s Hospital in London and noticed that a mould had contaminated one of his dishes of bacteria. Around the mould, the bacteria had died. From that lucky accident came penicillin, the first great antibiotic, and the start of modern medicine’s war on infection.

All of that is true, as far as it goes. What the textbook version usually skips is that, for about ten years after Fleming’s discovery, penicillin cured almost nobody. It was a laboratory curiosity. Turning it into a medicine took a second discovery, made by a different team in a different city, in the middle of a world war. That second discovery is the reason penicillin saved millions of lives, and it is the half of the story most people have never heard.

The first discovery, and why it stalled

Fleming described his findings in a paper in 1929. He showed that liquid from the mould culture, which he called penicillin, stopped the growth of several dangerous bacteria, including staphylococci and streptococci, that it did not seem to harm rabbits or mice when injected, and that it did not irritate human tissue. He also noted that its activity faded over time.

Strikingly, the main practical use his paper proposed was not as a treatment but as a laboratory tool: because penicillin killed some bacteria and not others, it could help researchers grow the ones they wanted to study. Fleming was a bacteriologist, not a chemist. Penicillin was fragile and present in tiny amounts in the mould juice, and extracting it in a pure, stable form needed chemical skills he did not have. In 1932, the chemist Harold Raistrick and colleagues in London studied how the mould produced the substance, but no one turned it into a usable drug.

There was one forgotten early success. In 1930, Cecil George Paine, a doctor in Sheffield, used crude mould filtrate to treat eye infections in two babies and a miner, apparently with success. He never published the results and kept few records, and his work only came to light decades later. But Paine did mention his results to a professor of pathology who arrived at Sheffield in 1932: an Australian named Howard Florey.

Oxford, 1939: starting again

By the late 1930s, Florey was head of pathology at the Sir William Dunn School in Oxford. With the biochemist Ernst Chain, who had left Nazi Germany, he decided to study natural substances that kill bacteria. At first they expected penicillin to be an enzyme, not a drug. By 1940, the project had grown to about a dozen scientists, working with little money as Britain went to war.

The problem was the same one that had defeated everyone else: getting enough penicillin, pure enough, to test in a living body. The person who solved it was a young biochemist named Norman Heatley.

The bedpan factory

Penicillin falls apart when exposed to heat, strong acid or strong alkali, the usual tools chemists use to purify things. Heatley devised a gentler method called back-extraction. Penicillin was drawn out of the cooled mould juice into a solvent, then drawn back into slightly alkaline water. Freeze-drying the result gave a brown powder that was only about 1% pure, but strong enough to work with.

The mould itself had to be grown on the surface of liquid in shallow containers, and the team tried everything: pie dishes, biscuit tins, baths. Hospital bedpans turned out to work best, so Heatley designed a stackable ceramic culture vessel based on them, and hundreds were made. He built an extraction machine from milk churns, bottles and tubing. A small group of young women, nicknamed the “penicillin girls”, were employed to tend the cultures. The Dunn School became, in effect, a penicillin factory.

Fleming discovered that penicillin existed. The Oxford team discovered how to make enough of it, pure enough, to prove it could cure infection in a living body. Without the second discovery, the first would have stayed a footnote.

Eight mice

On 25 May 1940, the team ran the experiment that changed everything. Eight mice were injected with a lethal dose of streptococcus bacteria. An hour later, four of them were also given penicillin. Heatley stayed in the laboratory through the night to watch. By the early hours of the morning, all four untreated mice were dead. The four that had received penicillin were alive.

Hundreds more mice were tested that summer, with consistent results. In August 1940, the team reported their findings in The Lancet in a paper titled “Penicillin as a chemotherapeutic agent”. It was the first clear demonstration that penicillin could treat an infection inside a living animal, rather than just killing bacteria on a dish.

The policeman

Mice are small, and people are not. Treating one adult would need thousands of times more penicillin than the team could easily make. In January 1941, a first injection in a terminally ill woman caused a bad reaction, which Edward Abraham, another team member, traced to impurities in the drug.

The first person treated for an infection was Albert Alexander, a police constable in his forties. He had developed a severe infection that spread to his face, eyes and lungs. The popular version of his story says it started with a scratch from a rose thorn; more recent research suggests he was injured in a German bombing raid on Southampton, where he had been sent to help the local police. On 12 February 1941, at the Radcliffe Infirmary in Oxford, he received penicillin.

Within a day he began to improve. But the supply was tiny. Within days it ran out, and the team took the extraordinary step of recovering penicillin from his urine to give it to him again. It was not enough. He relapsed, and died on 15 March 1941. Several of the next patients treated in 1941 made remarkable recoveries. The results, published in The Lancet in August 1941, showed beyond doubt that penicillin worked in people. The challenge now was making enough.

The American scale-up and the mouldy melon

Britain, under bombing and short of everything, could not build a penicillin industry quickly. In July 1941, Florey and Heatley flew to the United States, carrying the mould. They were sent to a US Department of Agriculture laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, which had experience with large-scale fermentation. There, scientists found that growing the mould submerged in deep, aerated tanks, fed with corn-steep liquor, a cheap by-product of processing maize, produced far more penicillin than growing it on the surface in bottles or bedpans.

They also hunted for better moulds. In 1943, a mouldy cantaloupe from a Peoria market yielded a strain that turned out to be the best producer of all, and the ancestor of the strains used commercially afterwards. It is often credited to a laboratory worker named Mary Hunt, nicknamed “Mouldy Mary”, though historians are not certain who actually found it. Heatley stayed in America to help drug companies scale up. Drug companies such as Pfizer solved engineering problems, such as keeping huge tanks sterile while pumping air through them.

In March 1942, a woman named Anne Miller lay dying of a bloodstream infection in a hospital in New Haven, Connecticut. She was given penicillin supplied by Merck. Her fever broke the next day, and she lived to the age of 90. By the time of the D-Day landings in 1944, penicillin was available in quantity for wounded Allied soldiers. According to the US Department of Agriculture, its price fell from about $20 per dose in July 1943 to 55 cents a few years later.

How Fleming became the hero

So why does the popular story centre on Fleming? Part of the answer is the press. In August 1942, after The Times wrote about penicillin, Fleming’s hospital wrote to the paper pointing to his original discovery. Journalists flocked to St Mary’s, and Fleming became the face of the story. Florey, by contrast, largely refused to speak to reporters. A simple story of one man and a lucky mould was easier to tell than a story of a dozen scientists, bedpans and solvents. Fleming himself is said to have kept a scrapbook of press cuttings that he labelled the “Fleming myth”, and he often pointed to the Oxford team’s work.

In 1945, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was shared by Fleming, Florey and Chain. Nobel rules allow no more than three winners. Norman Heatley, whose ingenuity had made the Oxford work possible, was left out. His contribution was formally honoured only in 1990, when Oxford University awarded him an honorary Doctorate of Medicine, the first given to someone who was not a medical doctor in its 800-year history. He was 79.

Others belong in the story too. In 1945, the Oxford crystallographer Dorothy Hodgkin used X-rays to work out penicillin’s structure, confirming that it contained a distinctive chemical ring that Chain had proposed. Knowing the structure opened the way to designing related antibiotics. She later won a Nobel Prize of her own.

Fleming’s warning

Fleming does deserve credit for more than the original observation. When he accepted his Nobel Prize in 1945, he warned that people taking penicillin without guidance might use too little, and that doses too small to kill bacteria could allow them to become resistant. In his words, “the ignorant man may easily underdose himself”. Eighty years later, antibiotic resistance is one of the most serious problems in medicine, and his warning reads as a prophecy.

Why second discoveries are forgotten

The penicillin story shows a pattern that runs through the history of science. We remember the moment of insight because it makes a good story: one person, one accident, one flash of understanding. We forget the slow, unglamorous work that turns an insight into something useful, because it is harder to tell and spread across many people. Yet that second stage is often where the real difficulty lies (see The Bacterium in the Stomach for another discovery whose popular version is only half right).

Fleming saw something remarkable and wrote it down carefully. Paine quietly showed it could help patients. Florey and Chain had the nerve to revisit a forgotten observation. Heatley made it physically possible. A dying policeman and a handful of children showed it worked in people. American engineers and a mouldy melon made it available to millions. Penicillin was not discovered once. It was discovered, again and again, by everyone who moved it one step closer to the patient.

Discoveries are rarely a single moment. The leap from “this is interesting” to “this saves lives” often needs different people, different skills and years of work, and that second discovery is the one history most often forgets.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Fleming observed and described penicillin’s antibacterial effect in 1928–29 but did not develop it into a treatment.
  • The Oxford team led by Florey and Chain, with Heatley’s methods, purified penicillin and showed in 1940–41 that it cured infections in mice and people.
  • Mass production was achieved mainly in the United States, using deep-tank fermentation and higher-yielding mould strains.
  • Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the 1945 Nobel Prize; Heatley was not included.

Still uncertain

  • How Albert Alexander’s infection began: the rose-thorn story versus a bombing-raid injury.
  • Who actually found the mouldy cantaloupe that gave the best production strain.
  • Details of Cecil Paine’s 1930 treatments, which were never published.
  • How much the popular focus on Fleming came from the press, from his hospital and from his own actions.
