Does the Time You Take a Medicine Matter?

For some drugs, the hour of the day changes how well they work or how well you tolerate them. For others, the question turns out to matter much less than the headlines promised.

7 min read·1 November 2026

Many prescription labels say simply “once a day”. That leaves a question millions of people quietly wonder about: does it matter whether I take it with my morning tea or just before bed? The body is not the same machine at 8 a.m. as it is at midnight. Blood pressure, hormone levels, digestion and immune activity all rise and fall on a roughly 24-hour cycle, the circadian rhythm. So it is reasonable to ask whether medicines work differently depending on when they meet that rhythm.

The study of this question is called chronopharmacology, and the idea of using it to time treatment is called chronotherapy. It is a genuinely interesting field. It is also a field where some of the most exciting claims have not survived closer testing.

Two different kinds of timing

It helps to separate two questions. The first is whether a drug works better at a particular point in the body’s daily cycle. The second, far more common in everyday prescribing, is whether a drug should be taken at a particular time relative to something else: food, sleep, or other medicines. Most timing instructions on a label are about the second question. They are often less about the body clock than about absorption, comfort and side effects.

Blood pressure: the biggest test so far

Blood pressure normally dips during sleep and rises on waking, and people whose pressure doesn’t fall at night tend to have more heart problems. That made bedtime dosing an appealing idea. In 2019, a Spanish study called the Hygia Chronotherapy Trial reported that among more than 19,000 people, taking blood pressure medicines at bedtime rather than in the morning was linked to about 45% fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths. The European Society of Cardiology publicised it with a cheerful headline that bedtime was the best time.

Many experts were sceptical. An effect that large from simply moving a pill to the evening seemed implausible, and critics raised questions about how patients were allocated, how outcomes were checked and how few people had dropped out. In 2020 the journal issued an expression of concern, a formal warning to read the results with caution while it reviewed the study. The review did not lead to a retraction, but doubts about the size of the effect remained.

The real answer came from larger trials designed to test the idea directly. The UK’s TIME study randomly assigned 21,104 adults to take their usual blood pressure medicines in the morning or the evening and followed them for about five years. Heart attacks, strokes and deaths occurred in 390 people in the morning group and 362 in the evening group, a difference small enough to be explained by chance. Canada’s BedMed trial, published in 2025 with 3,357 people in primary care, found the same: no meaningful difference. A companion trial in 776 frail nursing-home residents also found no clear difference in its main outcome.

Timing did change the experience of treatment a little. In TIME, the evening group reported feeling faint or falling slightly less often, but needed to get up to urinate more often.

For most blood pressure medicines, the best time to take them is the time you will reliably remember.

Statins: a reason, and a modest effect

Statins lower cholesterol by blocking an enzyme the liver uses to make it, and the body makes more cholesterol at night. Some statins, such as simvastatin, are cleared from the body within a few hours, so taking them in the evening lines them up with that night-time production. A 2007 review found enough evidence to support evening dosing of simvastatin. Longer-acting statins such as atorvastatin and rosuvastatin stay active for most of a day, and for these, the time seems to matter little.

A Cochrane review in 2016 pooled five short trials and found no clear overall difference in cholesterol between morning and evening dosing. The evidence was rated low quality, the trials lasted no longer than 14 weeks, and none measured heart attacks or deaths. So the advice for short-acting statins is reasonable, but the benefit is modest and the evidence thin.

Sometimes it’s about meals and sleep, not the clock

Many timing instructions exist for practical reasons.

  • Steroid tablets such as prednisolone. The NHS advises taking a daily dose with breakfast, partly to protect the stomach and partly because a morning dose is less likely to disturb sleep.
  • Stomach acid medicines such as omeprazole. Capsules are usually taken before a meal, preferably in the morning, because they work best when the stomach is getting ready to digest food.
  • Thyroid hormone (levothyroxine). This is usually taken in the morning on an empty stomach, because food can reduce how much is absorbed. In a Dutch trial of 105 patients, taking it at bedtime instead produced slightly better thyroid hormone levels, with no difference in how people felt. The lesson is less “morning versus night” than “keep it away from food, and be consistent”.

Vaccines: a morning advantage, with a caveat

The immune system also has a daily rhythm. In a UK trial, 24 general practices were assigned to give flu vaccines to 276 adults over 65 either in the morning (9–11 a.m.) or the afternoon (3–5 p.m.). A month later, the morning group had produced more antibodies against two of the three flu strains in the vaccine. That is an interesting result, but antibody levels are a stand-in for what really matters. The trial did not show that morning vaccination prevents more illness, and any vaccine is far better than a missed one. Measures that stand in for real outcomes can be misleading (see When a Measure Becomes a Target).

Cancer treatment: the most hopeful, and the most cautionary

Cancer is where chronotherapy has its longest history. Since the 1990s, researchers in Europe have tested chronomodulated chemotherapy, in which drug infusions are timed to the hours when healthy cells are thought to be least vulnerable. A 2012 analysis pooled data on 842 people with advanced bowel cancer from three large trials. Men lived longer on the timed schedule than on standard treatment, with a median survival of about 21 months versus 17.5 months. Women showed the opposite pattern and did slightly worse on the timed schedule. Body clocks, it seems, are not identical in everyone, and a “better time” for one group may not suit another.

More recently, attention has turned to immunotherapy, drugs that help the immune system attack cancer. In 2021, a study of 291 people with advanced melanoma at one US cancer centre found that those who received a larger share of their infusions after 4:30 p.m. had shorter survival. Many similar studies followed. In May 2026, a review combining 29 studies and more than 6,000 patients reported that earlier infusions were linked to a substantially lower risk of death.

But 27 of those 29 studies looked back at past records. Studies like this cannot easily rule out other explanations. For example, patients treated late in the day might differ in ways that also affect survival, such as having more complicated appointments or more delays in their care. Only one randomised trial was included. Published in Nature Medicine in February 2026, it reported that patients with lung cancer who received treatment before 3 p.m. lived far longer than those treated later. It attracted wide attention. Then other researchers pointed out inconsistencies in how the trial’s design, eligibility criteria, sample size and outcomes had been reported. In June 2026, the journal retracted the paper, saying the editors no longer had confidence in the results.

The idea itself is not disproven. Well-designed trials are under way. But for now, the claim that the time of an immunotherapy infusion makes a large difference to survival rests mainly on observational data. A finding that seems too dramatic to be true deserves to be weighed against how plausible it was beforehand (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

What this means in practice

  • Follow the instructions you were given. Most timing advice exists for good reasons, often about food, sleep or side effects.
  • Consistency usually matters more than the exact hour. For many once-daily medicines, taking them at the same time every day is what counts.
  • Don’t change the timing on your own. For some medicines, such as insulin, blood thinners or seizure medicines, changing the schedule can matter a great deal. Ask your doctor or pharmacist first.
  • Be wary of dramatic timing headlines. Ask whether the finding came from a randomised trial, and whether anyone else has repeated it.

The body clock is real and fascinating, and for a few treatments it may one day guide care. But the story so far is a reminder of how medicine makes progress: an exciting result, a period of enthusiasm, and then the slower, larger studies that tell us what actually holds up.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Many body processes, including blood pressure, hormone levels and immune activity, follow a daily rhythm.
  • For most people with high blood pressure, morning or bedtime dosing makes no measurable difference to heart attacks, strokes or deaths. Large randomised trials agree on this.
  • Many timing instructions matter mainly because of food, sleep or side effects, as with levothyroxine, omeprazole and prednisolone.
  • Short-acting statins such as simvastatin are reasonably taken in the evening; longer-acting statins work similarly at any time.

Still uncertain

  • Whether giving immunotherapy earlier in the day improves survival. Most evidence is observational, and the only randomised trial has been retracted.
  • Whether timed chemotherapy helps, and for whom; men and women appear to respond differently.
  • Whether the higher antibody levels after morning vaccination translate into better protection against illness.
  • Whether treatment timing should be tailored to individual body clocks, for example for night-shift workers.
