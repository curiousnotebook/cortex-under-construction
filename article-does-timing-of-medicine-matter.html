Does the Time You Take a Medicine Matter?

For some drugs, the hour of the day changes how well they work and how much harm they cause. For others, the question turns out to be much less important than the headlines suggest.

7 min read·1 November 2026

Most prescriptions say “once daily” or “twice daily” and stop there. Pharmacists sometimes add “take with food,” and occasionally “take at bedtime.” It is reasonable to wonder whether the hour matters. Our bodies follow a roughly 24-hour cycle, and that cycle affects how we absorb, process and respond to drugs. Chronopharmacology is the field that studies this. Its findings are real, but they are uneven, and the most interesting questions are the ones still being argued over.

Why the clock can matter

The body does not behave the same way at 7 in the morning and at 11 at night. Blood pressure typically dips during sleep and rises around waking. Liver enzymes that break down drugs vary across the day. Cells divide at different rates at different times, which is why some cancer treatments are timed. Reviews of the field describe the effectiveness and toxicity of many drugs as varying with dosing time, through changes in how a drug is absorbed and cleared, and through changes in the body’s response to it.

There is also a historical quirk. A review in a clinical pharmacology journal observed that most pharmaceutical studies have been carried out with morning dosing, without strong evidence that morning is the best time. In other words, we may have simply never tested many drugs at other times of day.

The blood pressure question

The most debated example is blood pressure medication. Several Spanish studies led by Ramón Hermida compared taking the daily dose on waking with taking it at bedtime. The largest, the Hygia Chronotherapy Trial, enrolled 19,084 adults with hypertension and reported that bedtime dosing was associated with substantially fewer cardiovascular events over about six years. The headline figure was a reduction of around 45 per cent in the composite of cardiovascular events, and the reported benefit was seen across several drug classes.

The trial has not gone unchallenged. Critics have questioned whether the randomisation was truly unrestricted, whether the size of the effect is plausible given a small difference in blood pressure during sleep, and whether the groups differed in the drugs they received. The authors have defended their methods. The disagreement has been unusually public, and it shows how a single large trial can be read very differently depending on the questions it was designed to answer.

Other trials have tested the same idea more cleanly. The UK TIME study, a randomised trial of morning versus evening dosing in people with hypertension, found no advantage for evening dosing in major cardiovascular outcomes or mortality. Smaller Canadian trials in nursing-home residents and in a larger general population also found no clear difference. Read together, the evidence does not settle the question: one large trial reports a big benefit, several others do not.

For blood pressure medicines, the timing question is open. The results are disputed, and no one should change when they take a prescribed dose on the strength of a headline alone.

Statins: a smaller effect than the internet suggests

Statins are a different story. Cholesterol is made most actively overnight, which led to a popular belief that statins should always be taken in the evening. The truth depends on the statin. Some, such as simvastatin, have a short half-life in the body, and in those cases evening dosing tends to lower LDL cholesterol a little more. Others, such as atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, stay active for much longer, and the timing makes little difference.

A Cochrane review of trials comparing morning and evening statin dosing found only a small number of eligible randomised trials, with few participants, and concluded that the evidence on lipid levels did not show a clear difference overall. A meta-analysis of morning versus evening dosing reported that short-acting statins lowered LDL cholesterol more in the evening, while long-acting statins were nearly equivalent at either time. Neither review tells us whether timing changes heart attacks or strokes, which is the outcome that matters most. A large Danish randomised trial comparing bedtime and morning statin dosing for cardiovascular events is registered but has not yet reported.

Chemotherapy: timing that helps some and not others

Cancer treatment offers the clearest examples of timing being deliberately used, and also the clearest warnings. The French team led by Francis Lévi has tested “chronomodulated” chemotherapy, in which drugs are given at particular hours to match the body’s rhythms. In the EORTC 05011 trial, which randomised patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, the timing of the irinotecan infusion affected side effects, and the pattern differed between men and women. For men, a morning infusion was associated with fewer serious side effects; for women, an afternoon infusion was. The authors recommended timing according to sex, but the study was designed to look at toxicity, and such subgroup findings need confirmation before they guide routine practice.

A larger trial, EORTC 05963, compared a chronomodulated schedule with a standard non-timed schedule of similar drugs. The chronomodulated version did not improve survival. The authors' interpretation was that delivering the standard schedule close to the maximum tolerated dose was more useful than the timed alternative. The result is a reminder that a plausible biological idea can fail when tested directly on outcomes that matter to patients.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Many drugs are absorbed, metabolised and act differently depending on the time of day, through biological rhythms.
  • For short-acting statins, evening dosing tends to lower LDL cholesterol a little more; for long-acting statins, the difference is small.
  • Some chemotherapy toxicities vary with the time of infusion, and this has been shown in randomised trials.

Still uncertain

  • Whether bedtime dosing of blood pressure medicines reduces heart attacks and strokes. The largest trial is disputed, and other trials found no clear benefit.
  • Whether statin timing changes cardiovascular events, as opposed to cholesterol levels.
  • Whether the sex-based timing findings in cancer treatment hold up in other studies.

What this means for you

If you take a prescribed medicine, the most important rule is simple: do not change the timing without talking to your doctor or pharmacist. Some medicines have specific instructions, such as taking them on an empty stomach or away from certain foods, and changing the time can make them less effective or more likely to cause side effects. Missed doses are a common problem, and a consistent routine often matters more than the exact hour.

If you are curious, ask your clinician whether the timing of a particular medicine has been studied, and what the evidence says. That question tends to lead somewhere useful. It also helps to remember that a single study showing a large benefit should prompt a second look, not an immediate change. This is the same logic that appears in The Placebo Puzzle, where early results can look more convincing than they are, and in Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine, where a striking result has to be weighed against what was likely beforehand.

The broader lesson is about how medicine is studied. The time of day has been treated as an afterthought in most trials, which is a gap rather than a conclusion. Some of the answers may be useful. Most of the important ones are still being tested.

This essay is for general education only and is not medical advice. Never start, stop or change the timing of a prescribed medicine without talking to your doctor or pharmacist.
