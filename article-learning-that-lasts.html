Forgetting Is Not the Enemy: The Science of Learning That Lasts

Rereading feels productive. Testing yourself feels frustrating. The research suggests those feelings may have things backwards.

5 min read·3 October 2026

You read a chapter carefully. You highlight the important parts. You read it again the night before the exam, and it all looks familiar. Then, in the exam hall, the details simply aren’t there. If this has happened to you, you’ve met one of the most counter-intuitive findings in psychology: the study methods that feel most effective are often not the ones that work best.

A scientist who memorised nonsense

In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran an unusual set of experiments on himself. To study memory without the help of meaning, he memorised lists of nonsense syllables, combinations like “DAX” or “BOK,” and measured how much he retained over time.

He found that forgetting happens fast at first and then slows down: much is lost within the first day, and what survives fades more gradually. This pattern became known as the forgetting curve. More than a century later, a careful replication published in 2015 found a very similar curve.

Forgetting is not a flaw to be ashamed of. It’s the normal background against which all learning happens. The question is what we can do about it.

Testing yourself is a way of learning, not just measuring

In a well-known 2006 experiment, students read short prose passages. Some then reread the passage; others took a test in which they wrote down everything they could recall, without looking. When memory was checked five minutes later, rereading had a slight edge. But after two days, and again after a week, the students who had tested themselves remembered substantially more.

This is the testing effect, also called retrieval practice. Pulling information out of memory appears to strengthen it far more than pushing it in again.

Space it out

The second big idea is the spacing effect. Studying the same material in several shorter sessions spread over time generally produces better long-term memory than the same amount of study crammed into one session. A large 2006 review that combined results from hundreds of experiments confirmed the benefit of spacing, and found that the best gap between sessions depends on how long you want to remember: the longer you need the knowledge, the longer the useful gaps tend to be.

Effort is not a sign that learning is failing. Recalling something you’ve half-forgotten is hard precisely because that struggle is doing useful work.

Desirable difficulties

The psychologist Robert Bjork coined a helpful phrase: desirable difficulties. These are conditions that make learning feel slower or harder in the moment, such as testing yourself, spacing sessions or mixing different types of problems, but which improve long-term retention.

The flip side is the illusion of fluency. When you reread a page, it feels easy and familiar, and familiarity is easily mistaken for mastery. Recognising something when it is in front of you is very different from recalling it when it isn’t.

What a major review concluded

In 2013, a team of researchers evaluated ten popular study techniques against the available evidence. Two stood out as having high utility: practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice. Several popular habits, including highlighting, rereading and summarising, were rated as having low utility, not because they are useless, but because the evidence for their benefit was weaker or more limited. Techniques such as explaining to yourself why something is true, and interleaving different kinds of problems, landed in between.

A simple routine to try

  1. Read, then close the book. Write or say everything you remember. Only then look back and check what you missed.
  2. Turn notes into questions. Instead of “Penicillin was discovered in 1928,” write “When was penicillin discovered, and how?”
  3. Come back later. Revisit the material after a day, then after a few days, then after a week or more. There is no single perfect schedule; what matters is returning after some forgetting has happened.
  4. Mix it up. Practise different types of questions together rather than one type at a time.
  5. Explain it simply. Teach the idea to an imaginary student. Gaps in your explanation reveal gaps in your understanding.

One caution connects this to how memory works. Each time you recall something, you rebuild it, and errors can creep in (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller). That’s why the checking step matters: retrieval practice works best when you get feedback.

And a final thought, borrowed from economics: if your only goal is a high mark on the next test, cramming can sometimes get you there. But marks are a measure of learning, not learning itself (see When a Measure Becomes a Target). If you want knowledge that is still with you next year, aim for the methods that feel a little harder now.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Forgetting is rapid at first and slows over time.
  • Retrieval practice improves long-term retention compared with rereading.
  • Spaced practice generally beats massed practice (cramming) for long-term memory.

Still uncertain

  • The ideal spacing schedules for complex, real-world skills rather than word lists and short texts.
  • How much these techniques improve deep understanding and problem-solving, not just recall.
  • How results vary between individuals, ages and subjects; much research involves students learning verbal material.
