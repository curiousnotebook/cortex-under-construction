Why Intelligent People Believe Things That Aren’t True

We tend to assume that brains are a shield against bad ideas. The research suggests intelligence is more like a powerful engine: very useful, as long as someone is steering towards the truth.

9 min read·23 October 2026

Linus Pauling was one of the most brilliant scientists of the twentieth century. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954 and the Nobel Peace Prize in 1962, and his work on chemical bonds helped shape modern chemistry. In the 1970s he became convinced that very large doses of vitamin C could help people with advanced cancer. When doctors at the Mayo Clinic tested the idea in randomised, double-blind trials, patients given 10 grams of vitamin C a day did no better than patients given a placebo. Pauling did not accept the result. He argued that the trials had not properly repeated his own work, and he kept defending the idea.

Pauling is not interesting because he was wrong. Everyone is wrong sometimes. He is interesting because a formidable mind did not seem to help him notice. That raises an uncomfortable question for anyone who values being clever: does intelligence protect us from false beliefs at all?

Being clever and thinking well are not the same thing

Psychologists who study reasoning draw a line between intelligence and rationality. Intelligence, as IQ tests measure it, is something like raw processing power: how quickly you learn, how much you can hold in mind, how well you solve a puzzle when someone hands it to you. Rationality is about how you use that power. Do you look for evidence that might prove you wrong? Do you notice when a question deserves more thought? Do you change your mind when you should?

For many classic thinking errors, the two travel together. People who score higher on cognitive tests tend to make somewhat fewer mistakes on probability puzzles and logic problems. But the psychologist Keith Stanovich and his colleagues found an important exception. Across many studies of myside bias, the habit of judging evidence and arguments in favour of what we already believe, the size of the bias showed very little relationship to intelligence. Clever people lean towards their own side about as much as everyone else.

The blind spot that brains don’t fix

In 2002, Emily Pronin, Daniel Lin and Lee Ross asked people how much various biases, such as taking credit for success while blaming failure on bad luck, affected themselves and how much they affected the average person. Most rated themselves as less biased than others. Even after reading a description of how a bias might have shaped their answers, many still insisted that their own judgements were accurate and objective. The researchers called this the bias blind spot. A set of preregistered replications published in 2021 found the same pattern.

A decade after the original study, Richard West, Russell Meserve and Keith Stanovich asked whether intelligence shrinks the blind spot. It did not. Students with higher cognitive ability were just as likely to see bias in others but not in themselves, and if anything their blind spot was slightly larger. The finding was later popularised as proof that smart people are more biased, which overstates it. What the study showed is narrower, but still striking: being clever did not help people see their own biases.

A better lawyer, not a better judge

Why would intelligence fail here? One influential answer comes from the psychologist Ziva Kunda. In 1990 she argued that people are more likely to arrive at conclusions they want, but only as far as they can build a reasonable-looking case for them. We don’t simply believe what we like. We believe what we like when we can justify it.

That small condition has a large consequence. If belief depends on finding justifications, then a person who is better at finding justifications has more room to believe what they want. Intelligence can make you a better lawyer for your own side. It does not automatically make you a fairer judge.

The cognitive scientists Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber take the idea further. They argue that human reasoning may have evolved less for discovering truth on our own and more for building arguments that persuade others, and for checking the arguments others make. On this view, our habit of searching for support for what we already think is not reasoning breaking down. It is reasoning doing what it does well, in a setting where nobody is arguing back.

The skin cream and the gun law

The best-known test of this idea came from Dan Kahan and his colleagues. They showed more than a thousand American adults a small table of made-up results and asked what it showed. In one version, the table described whether a new skin cream made a rash better or worse. Getting the right answer meant comparing proportions rather than raw numbers, which is harder than it looks. As expected, people with stronger numeracy, the ability to work with numbers, were much more likely to get it right.

In the other version, the very same numbers described whether cities that banned carrying concealed handguns saw crime rise or fall. Now the answers split along political lines: people were more likely to get the right answer when it suited their politics. In the original study, this split was largest among the most numerate participants. The researchers called it motivated numeracy: skill with numbers, used selectively.

The twist: the famous finding hasn’t held up well

The skin-cream study became a favourite of books and talks about why clever people disagree. Then other researchers tried to repeat it.

A large preregistered replication published in 2021 found that people were still pulled towards answers that fitted their politics, but it did not find that this pull reliably grew with numeracy. In 2023, a team using a nationally representative sample of Americans, twice the size of the original, reached a similar conclusion. Everyone showed some bias towards politically congenial answers, but people with higher numeracy were more accurate whichever way the right answer pointed. Other analyses suggest that part of what looks like political bias may come from people’s prior beliefs about the specific facts, rather than from their political identity as such.

Research on fake news points the same way. In 2019, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand asked more than 3,000 people to judge real and fake political headlines. People who scored higher on a test of reflective thinking were better at telling true from false, even when a fake headline flattered their own side. In their reading, people who fall for fake news are usually not reasoning too cleverly in the wrong direction. They are mostly not stopping to reason at all.

This is a useful correction. The broad claim, that people of every ability level reason in a slanted way about emotionally charged questions, has held up well. The more dramatic claim, that intelligence makes the slant worse, looks much weaker than it once did. On average, more capable and more reflective thinkers are better at sorting truth from falsehood. The interesting question is why that advantage so often switches off.

Intelligence is horsepower, not steering. It helps most when we are genuinely trying to find out what is true, and least when we already know which answer we want.

Ability needs a reason to switch on

Try this puzzle, from the economist Shane Frederick’s Cognitive Reflection Test. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

The answer that leaps to mind is 10 cents. It is wrong: if the ball cost 10 cents, the bat would cost $1.10 and the total would be $1.20. The right answer is 5 cents. The puzzle is not hard. It catches people because the first answer feels so obviously right that they don’t stop to check it, and plenty of students at highly selective universities get it wrong. Intelligence is no help if it is never called on.

Motivation matters in a deeper way too. In two online surveys of a few hundred people each, Tomas Ståhl and Jan-Willem van Prooijen found that stronger analytic thinking went with more scepticism about paranormal claims, and in the first survey about conspiracy theories too, but mainly among people who said it mattered to them that their beliefs rested on logic and evidence. Among people who cared less about that, analytic ability made little difference. Intelligence seems to work like a tool that only gets picked up when someone actually wants the job done.

None of this lets us diagnose Pauling from a distance. But his story fits the pattern: brilliance in one field, deep personal investment in an idea, and more than enough skill to keep building a case for it.

What actually helps

If intelligence is not a shield, what is? The research points to habits rather than talents.

  • Consider the opposite. In a classic 1984 experiment, Charles Lord and his colleagues asked people to imagine how they would have judged a study if exactly the same methods had produced the opposite result. This reduced bias more than simply telling people to be fair and unbiased.
  • Pause to ask whether it is true. In experiments by Pennycook and colleagues, including one run on Twitter, a simple nudge to think about the accuracy of a headline improved the quality of the news people went on to share.
  • Notice when you want an answer. Motivated reasoning pulls hardest when a conclusion protects something you care about: your identity, your reputation, a decision you have already made. That feeling of wanting is a cue to slow down.
  • Ask what would change your mind. If you can’t name any evidence that would, the belief is probably resting on something other than evidence.
  • Distrust how good your own argument feels. For a clever person, a persuasive case is cheap to build. The fact that you can argue well for something is weak evidence that it is true.

There is something freeing in all this. Being intelligent is not the same as being right, and nobody is exempt from the pull of what they would like to be true. The people who reason best are not the ones who never feel that pull. They are the ones who have learned to notice it.

Our memories, too, quietly rebuild the past to fit the story we prefer (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller). And thinking well about any new piece of evidence means weighing it against what was likely beforehand (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • People see bias more readily in others than in themselves, and higher cognitive ability does not shrink this blind spot.
  • Myside bias, favouring evidence that supports what we already believe, is only weakly related to intelligence.
  • People at every ability level judge evidence more favourably when its conclusion suits their political views.
  • On average, more reflective thinkers are better at telling true headlines from false ones.
  • Deliberately considering the opposite reduces biased judgement more than a general instruction to be fair.

Still uncertain

  • Whether higher intelligence or numeracy actually increases politically biased reasoning. The original finding has not replicated well.
  • How much apparent political bias comes from identity, and how much from people’s existing beliefs about the specific facts.
  • Whether open-mindedness questionnaires capture the real habit. Their scores predict many reasoning skills but, puzzlingly, not the avoidance of myside thinking.
  • How long the benefits of debiasing techniques last outside the lab.
