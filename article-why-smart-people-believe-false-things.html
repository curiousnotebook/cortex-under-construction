Why Intelligent People Believe Things That Aren’t True

Being clever does not make us immune to error. It can make our mistakes more persuasive, to ourselves most of all.

6 min read·23 October 2026

It is a comfortable assumption that a sharp mind is a well-defended mind. If you can follow an argument, spot a weak statistic and remember the details of a study, surely you will not be fooled by nonsense. Psychologists have tested this assumption for decades, and the results are uncomfortable. Intelligence helps in many ways, but it does not reliably protect us from believing false things. In some cases it seems to make the problem harder to see.

The bias you cannot see in yourself

In 2002, the psychologist Emily Pronin and her colleagues described what they called the bias blind spot. People readily accept that others are influenced by biases, such as favouring information that flatters what they already think. But when asked whether they themselves are similarly biased, most judge themselves to be less so than the average person. The error is not that we are biased; it is that we are biased and rate ourselves as the exception.

The natural question is whether clever people are better at spotting their own blind spots. Richard West, Russell Meserve and Keith Stanovich tested this directly in 2012. They measured the blind spot alongside cognitive ability and found that it was not reduced by cognitive sophistication. In several measures, people with higher cognitive ability showed a larger blind spot, not a smaller one. Being free of the blind spot also did not mean being free of the biases themselves: the people who were most sure they were unbiased were not measurably less prone to the classic errors.

Intelligence does not reliably tell you when you are wrong. It can tell you that you are right more convincingly.

Myside thinking

Keith Stanovich has studied a closely related pattern he calls myside bias: the tendency to generate arguments for our own position more easily than for the other side, to scrutinise opposing evidence more harshly, and to design weaker tests for ideas we already favour. In a 2013 review with West and Maggie Toplak, Stanovich argued that myside bias is almost unrelated to intelligence. Tests of cognitive ability do not measure the skill of avoiding it, which means a person can score highly on a test of reasoning and still show a strong myside tilt.

Stanovich later developed this into a book, The Bias That Divides Us (MIT Press, 2021). His central claim is that myside bias creates a real blind spot among people who are proud of their intellect. They assume their reasoning is a neutral instrument, when it may be serving a conclusion they reached for other reasons.

Reasoning as a lawyer, not a judge

Why would more reasoning ability make things worse? One explanation is that reasoning is not always used to find the truth. It can also be used to defend a position. A person who is good at building arguments can construct a more elaborate case for a conclusion they already favour, and find more reasons to dismiss evidence that threatens it. Their skill is real; it is simply pointed in a direction chosen in advance.

The clearest demonstration of this comes from Dan Kahan and his colleagues at Yale. In a study of 1,111 American adults, participants first completed a test of numeracy, the ability to work with quantitative information. They then solved a statistics problem about a skin cream. When the data showed the cream worked or failed in a neutral way, more numerate people did better, as expected. But when the same numbers were presented as the results of a city’s gun-control ban, the pattern shifted. Participants’ political views strongly predicted their answers, and the most numerate people were the most polarised. Those who were best at the maths tended to reach the conclusion that matched their politics, even when it was not supported by the data.

Kahan called this motivated numeracy. The finding is widely cited, but it is worth being careful about what it shows. Some researchers argue that the mechanism is less simple than “smart people reason their way to their politics,” and the effect has been debated in the years since. What seems robust is the broader point: higher skill did not bring people closer to agreement on contested facts.

Lazy, not biased?

Not every study points the same way, and this is where the story becomes more hopeful. Gordon Pennycook and David Rand asked a different question about misinformation. Rather than assuming that people believe fake news because they want to, they asked whether reasoning helps people tell true headlines from false ones, even when the false ones suit their politics.

In three studies totalling 3,446 American online participants, they found that people who scored higher on the Cognitive Reflection Test, a short set of puzzles that tempt you toward a quick but wrong answer, rated fake headlines as less accurate. Crucially, this held for headlines that matched the participant’s own political ideology. Analytic people were not simply better at spotting the other side’s lies. They were better at telling real from fake overall. The authors concluded that susceptibility to fake news was better explained by a lack of careful thought than by partisan motivation.

A related 2019 study by Michael Bronstein, Pennycook, Adam Bear, Rand and Tyrone Cannon, based on more than 900 participants, found that the tendency to believe implausible fake headlines was linked to less analytic and less open-minded thinking, and that this link could partly account for why dogmatic people were more likely to accept them.

Pennycook and Rand’s work suggests that the difference is not the amount of intelligence someone has, but how willingly they stop to check. That is a disposition, and dispositions can be cultivated.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • People rate themselves as less biased than others, and this bias blind spot is not reduced by higher cognitive ability.
  • Myside bias shows little or no relationship to measured intelligence.
  • Analytic, reflective thinking is linked to better discrimination between true and false news, including news that flatters one’s own side.

Still uncertain

  • Much of the evidence comes from online samples and laboratory tasks. How well it transfers to everyday arguments and to high-stakes decisions is still being tested.
  • Whether the mechanism behind motivated numeracy is motivated reasoning in the strict sense, or something more subtle, remains debated.
  • Whether training people to think more analytically reduces their everyday errors for lasting periods is not yet clear.

What this means for the rest of us

The lesson is not that intelligent people are foolish, or that intelligence is worthless. Reasoning ability is valuable, and the same skills that build a persuasive case can also expose a weak one. The problem is that they are not automatically aimed at the truth. Kahan’s later work offers a hopeful twist: in a 2017 study, the trait that most reliably weakened politically driven reading of scientific evidence was not knowledge or numeracy but curiosity about science, a genuine interest in information for its own sake.

Some practical habits follow from the research, though none of them is a guaranteed cure. One is to ask what evidence would change your mind before you examine the evidence. Another is to notice when you find the other side’s arguments unusually easy to dismiss. A third is to remember that the feeling of having thought carefully is not the same as having thought carefully. Clever people are not less likely to be wrong. They are simply better equipped to defend being wrong, and the first step is to notice when you are doing it.
