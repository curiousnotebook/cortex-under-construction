More Than the Molecule: What a Pill’s Colour, Price and Score Line Really Do

A tablet is not just its active ingredient. Its colour and price may change how well people say it works, a change in its look can make people stop taking it, and its coating and the line across its middle decide whether it can safely be split.

7 min read·3 December 2026

Pick up a strip of tablets and look closely. There is a colour, a shape, perhaps a letter or number stamped on it, a shiny or chalky surface, and sometimes a thin line pressed across the middle. It is easy to think of all this as packaging. The real medicine, surely, is the chemical inside.

But the outside of a pill does real work. Some of it is chemistry: coatings that protect the drug, or protect you from it. Some of it is psychology: how a pill looks and what it costs can change how people feel after taking it, and whether they keep taking it at all.

Does colour change how a pill works?

The idea is popular. In 1996, a team led by Anton de Craen reviewed the studies on drug colour in the BMJ. Across the studies they found, people tended to see red, yellow and orange pills as stimulating, and blue and green pills as calming. When they looked at real medicines, sleeping pills and anti-anxiety drugs were more often blue, green or purple than antidepressants were. Their conclusion was that colour affects how people expect a drug to act, and “seems to influence” how well it works.

That second part is much weaker than it sounds. The studies behind it were few and small. One of the best known, from 1972, gave blue or pink sugar pills to 56 medical students, who reported feeling drowsier after the blue ones. Others involved a few dozen patients and many comparisons, so some “significant” results may have appeared by chance. A 2024 critique by the writer Mike Hall pointed out that the most striking results came from the weakest studies. The review itself described the effects on effectiveness as inconsistent.

The fair summary is that colour clearly shapes what people expect from a pill. Whether it changes how well a drug actually works is not well established.

Brand, price and number

Similar experiments have tested other features. In a 1981 study in the BMJ, the psychologists Branthwaite and Cooper gave 835 women with regular headaches either aspirin or a dummy pill, in either a well-known brand’s packaging or a plain one. Branded pills were rated as giving more relief than unbranded ones, whether they contained aspirin or not. But the pain relief was self-reported, and the dummy pills may not have tasted like aspirin.

In 2008, a team including the behavioural economist Dan Ariely gave 82 volunteers mild electric shocks before and after taking a dummy “painkiller”. Half were told it cost $2.50 a dose; half were told it cost 10 cents. Of those told it was full price, 85% reported less pain afterwards, compared with 61% of those told it was cheap. It was a small, short laboratory study, but it is widely cited.

Even the number of pills may matter. A review of placebo groups in ulcer trials found that 44% of patients taking dummy pills four times a day had healed ulcers after four weeks, compared with 36% of those taking them twice a day. The authors called their explanation speculative: the trials being compared were not randomised against each other, and the four-a-day trials were mostly older.

All these findings point the same way: expectations colour experience, especially for symptoms people rate themselves, like pain. But most are old, small or hard to interpret, and they are better treated as interesting clues than as firm facts (for more on how expectation shapes symptoms, see The Placebo Puzzle).

A pill’s appearance clearly changes what people expect from it. The strongest evidence is not about how it works, but about whether people keep taking it.

When the pill changes colour

Here the evidence is more solid. Generic medicines contain the same active ingredient as the original brand and must deliver it in the same way, but they often look different. A pharmacy may switch between manufacturers, so the same prescription can arrive in a different colour or shape from one month to the next.

Aaron Kesselheim and colleagues at Harvard studied what happens next. Among people taking medicines for epilepsy, those whose pills changed colour between refills were more than 50% more likely to stop taking them. In a larger 2014 study of more than 11,000 people taking heart medicines after a heart attack, a change in pill colour raised the odds of stopping by about 34%, and a change in shape by about 66%. Nearly three in ten patients had a change in pill appearance in the first year.

The likely reason is simple: people may think they have been given the wrong medicine, or worry that a new-looking pill will not work as well. This matters in India, where the same medicine is often sold under many brand names, each with its own look. If a familiar tablet suddenly looks different, it is worth asking the pharmacist rather than quietly stopping.

The line down the middle

Many tablets have a groove pressed across them, called a score line. It is easy to assume this means the tablet can be split into two equal doses. Often it does. Sometimes it does not.

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration’s 2013 guidance on scoring says a tablet should only be described as having “functional scoring” if tests show the halves contain similar amounts of drug, lose very little material when broken, dissolve properly and remain stable. In Europe, a medicine’s official product information is supposed to say which kind of score it has: either “The tablet can be divided into equal halves”, or “The scoreline is only to facilitate breaking for ease of swallowing and not to divide into equal doses.” In other words, some lines are there only to make a large tablet easier to swallow.

Splitting a tablet without a proper score is less accurate than people imagine. In a 2011 Belgian study led by Charlotte Verrue, volunteers split eight kinds of tablets into 3,600 halves and quarters using methods common in nursing homes. Almost a third of the pieces were more than 15% off the intended weight, and about one in seven were more than 25% off. For most medicines that may not matter much. For drugs where the difference between an effective dose and a harmful one is small, it can.

What the coating is doing

The surface of a tablet is often a carefully designed layer, and breaking it can change how the medicine behaves.

  • Enteric coating. Some tablets are coated so they survive stomach acid and dissolve only further along in the intestine. This either protects the drug from acid, as with some acid-reducing medicines such as omeprazole, or protects the stomach from the drug, as with some forms of aspirin and diclofenac. Breaking or crushing the coating can stop the drug working as intended or cause stomach irritation.
  • Modified release. Tablets labelled SR, CR, ER, XR, XL, MR or LA are designed to release their drug slowly over many hours. Crushing them can release the whole dose at once. Britain’s NHS pharmacy guidance gives modified-release morphine as an example where this could be dangerous. Some modified-release tablets can be halved but never crushed, so it depends on the specific product.
  • Film and sugar coatings. These usually mask a bitter taste, make the tablet easier to swallow, protect it from light and moisture and give it a colour. According to NHS guidance, they do not usually change how the drug is absorbed, although a broken tablet may taste unpleasant or keep less well.
  • Hazardous medicines. Some cancer and hormone medicines should not be split or crushed at home, because the dust can expose the person handling them.

A practical checklist

  • Split a tablet only if your doctor or pharmacist says that product can be split, not just because it has a line.
  • Never crush or chew tablets marked as slow-release or enteric-coated unless you have been told it is safe.
  • If you need to split tablets, a proper pill cutter is more accurate than a knife or your fingers.
  • If your medicine suddenly looks different, ask the pharmacist before you stop taking it.
  • Remember that a cheaper or plainer-looking medicine with the same ingredient is not weaker.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • People associate pill colours with particular effects, such as blue with calming.
  • Changes in the colour or shape of generic pills are linked to people stopping their medicines.
  • Enteric-coated and modified-release tablets can behave differently, sometimes dangerously, if broken or crushed.
  • Splitting tablets by hand, especially unscored ones, often produces unequal doses.

Still uncertain

  • Whether colour changes how well a drug actually works, rather than how people rate it.
  • How large and lasting the effects of price and branding are outside small experiments.
  • Whether standardising pill appearance would improve how consistently people take their medicines.
  • How often unsafe splitting and crushing happen at home, especially in India.
