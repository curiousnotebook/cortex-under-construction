Where Gods Live: How Geography Shaped Religion

Religions did not appear at random. The land, rivers, climate and neighbours around a people helped decide what they believed, and the pattern of belief still follows the map.

5 min read·28 October 2026

Ask why a particular faith is common in one part of the world and rare in another, and you will usually get an answer about history: conquest, missionaries, trade routes. Those explanations matter, but they push the question back a step. Why did the first religions take the shapes they did, in the places they did? Part of the answer lies in the ground beneath people’s feet.

Settling down made religion bigger

For most of human history, people lived in small bands that moved with the seasons. Religion in such groups was real but local, tied to particular places, animals and ancestors. The shift to farming changed the stakes. Where crops could be grown reliably, people stayed put, stored surplus food, and built larger communities. Larger communities contain more strangers, and strangers are harder to trust.

One influential idea, developed by researchers including Ara Norenzayan, is that moralising gods, believed to watch over behaviour and punish cheats, helped hold large groups together. Cooperation among people who will never meet is fragile, and a god who sees everything offers a cheap kind of policing. This is the “Big Gods” hypothesis. It is debated, but it fits a striking pattern: moralising gods tend to appear in societies that are already large and complex, not only in small ones.

Rivers, plains and the first temples

Geography decided where surplus farming could take off. The river valleys of the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus and the Yellow River offered fertile soil and water, and in each of them early states grew up around temples, priests and written records. Religion here was tied to kingship, harvests and the calendar. Priests kept the calendar, and the calendar told farmers when to plant.

The surprise is how early religious building began. At Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey, large carved stone enclosures were erected around 9500 BCE, before farming was widespread in the area. This suggests that ritual and gathering may have helped drive settlement, not only follow it. The chicken-and-egg question is still open, and the site is a reminder that the relationship between geography and belief runs in both directions.

Geography rarely dictates a religion. It shapes which ways of living, and therefore which kinds of gods, are likely to take hold and spread.

Isolation, crossroads and the shape of today’s map

Once religions existed, geography kept them apart or pushed them together. Mountain ranges, deserts and oceans slowed contact, so local traditions could evolve on their own. Crossroads did the opposite. The trade routes of the Silk Road and the Indian Ocean carried ideas along with goods, which helped Buddhism spread from India across Central and East Asia and Islam spread along the coasts and caravan paths of the Indian Ocean world.

Jared Diamond’s book Guns, Germs, and Steel argued that the east–west orientation of Eurasia let crops, animals and ideas travel along similar latitudes, speeding up the spread of farming and everything that came with it. The argument is broad and has been criticised for leaning too heavily on environment, but it captures a real point: the direction a landmass runs can affect how quickly its cultures meet.

Today’s pattern is largely the result of these layers. Christianity is concentrated in the Americas and Europe, a legacy of European expansion. Islam dominates the Middle East and North Africa, and large parts of Southeast Asia and South Asia. Hinduism is concentrated in South Asia, and Buddhism across much of East and Southeast Asia. Sub-Saharan Africa is strongly Christian and Muslim in different areas. Much of this map was drawn by migration, empire and missionary work, not by climate alone.

A warning about tidy stories

It is tempting to read these patterns as destiny: the desert produced monotheism, the river produced temples. Such stories are seductive and often wrong. Many groups living in similar environments developed very different beliefs, and religions have repeatedly changed their shape when they moved into new surroundings. Environment sets limits and nudges, but people, leaders and history make the choices within them.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Settled farming led to larger, more stratified societies, and religion became closely tied to political authority.
  • Today’s distribution of major religions reflects migration, empire and trade as much as local environment.

Still uncertain

  • Whether moralising gods caused larger societies to cooperate, or simply followed their growth.
  • Whether ritual sites such as Göbekli Tepe drove the switch to farming or came after it.
  • How far environmental explanations, such as Diamond’s, hold up across the whole world.
