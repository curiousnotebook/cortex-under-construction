Who Said So?

Do We Really Need to Bathe Every Day?

Our elders insisted on a bath every single day. The science says they were right, but mostly about certain parts of the body, and certain kinds of weather.

5 min read·21 October 2026

In many homes, the rule is simple: no bath, no breakfast. Skip it on a cold winter morning and someone will notice. Ask why it has to be every day, and the answer is usually some version of “because that’s how it’s done.” Meanwhile, the internet is full of skin experts warning that daily showers strip your skin, and of people proudly bathing twice a week. So who is right?

As with many inherited rules, the honest answer is: partly both. But the reasons are more interesting than either side usually admits.

Where the rule comes from

It isn’t hard to see why a daily bath became non-negotiable in much of India. The climate is hot and humid for much of the year. For generations, most people worked outdoors, in fields, on roads, in markets, and came home covered in sweat and dust. A daily wash was the obvious answer.

For many families, the morning bath is also part of prayer or ritual, a way of starting the day clean in more than one sense. That meaning is real, and it isn’t something science can or should weigh in on. This article is only about what the bath does for the skin.

What your skin is actually doing

Your skin is not a surface that simply collects dirt until it is scrubbed. Its outermost layer, the stratum corneum, is a thin wall of flattened dead cells held together by fats (lipids), rather like bricks and mortar. This wall keeps water in and germs out, and it is constantly renewing itself as old cells flake away.

The surface of healthy skin is also slightly acidic. A widely cited analysis of the research concluded that natural skin pH is, on average, a little below 5, and that this acidity helps the friendly bacteria that normally live on our skin. Scientists call this acidic film the acid mantle.

Here is the catch. Soaps and other cleansers work by dissolving fats, and they cannot always tell the difference between grime and the skin’s own lipids. Traditional alkaline soaps can also push the skin’s pH upwards for a while after washing. Research on cleansers has linked harsh washing to that familiar tight, dry, sometimes itchy feeling, which is a sign that the barrier has been disturbed. Very hot water makes this worse.

For most people with healthy skin, this is a small effect that recovers quickly. For people with dry skin, eczema or older skin, it can matter a great deal.

Why we smell, and where

Fresh sweat is almost odourless. The smell comes later, when bacteria living on the skin, especially types called Corynebacterium and Staphylococcus, break down substances in sweat into strong-smelling chemicals. This happens mainly in the armpits and groin, where special sweat glands (apocrine glands) release the raw material the bacteria feed on. Feet are another hotspot, because shoes keep them warm and damp.

Your genes play a part too. A single variant in a gene called ABCC11 decides whether the apocrine glands release these odour “ingredients” at all. People with two copies of the variant produce very little underarm odour, and, as a bonus fact, they have dry, flaky earwax rather than the sticky kind. This variant is very common in East Asia and rare in Europe.

A large British study found something revealing. Among people whose genes meant they produced almost no underarm odour, roughly three in four still used deodorant regularly. In other words, much of our hygiene routine is driven by habit and social expectation, not by what our bodies actually need. That is a very “Who said so?” finding.

Body odour comes from a few specific places: armpits, groin, feet and skin folds. Your shins and forearms are rarely the problem.

The best test so far

Surprisingly few studies have tested how often people should bathe. One of the most useful was published in 2025. Researchers in the UK randomly assigned 438 people with eczema, children and adults, either to bathe or shower once or twice a week, or six or more times a week, for four weeks.

The result: there was no meaningful difference in eczema symptoms between the two groups. No serious harms were reported in either. The researchers concluded that people with eczema can choose whichever routine suits them.

This is useful, but it has limits. The study lasted only four weeks. Participants knew which group they were in, and they rated their own symptoms. It was done in Britain, a cool country, in people with eczema. It tells us that bathing less often is not harmful for that group, at least in the short term. It doesn’t tell us what is best for a healthy person sweating through an Indian summer.

Where the elders have a point

Heat and sweat change the picture. Fungal skin infections such as ringworm (tinea) thrive on warm, damp skin. A recent expert review lists living in a hot, humid environment, tight or non-breathable clothing, and poor hygiene among the important risk factors, and notes that stubborn, hard-to-treat ringworm has become especially common in the Indian subcontinent. Washing away sweat, and then drying skin folds properly, makes it harder for these fungi to settle in.

And one kind of washing is supported by strong evidence: handwashing. A large analysis of 30 studies found that better hand hygiene reduced stomach and bowel infections by about a third and respiratory infections by about a fifth. Plain soap worked as well as antibacterial soap. If you only remember one hygiene habit, make it this one.

A sensible middle path

Putting it together, the evidence suggests that how you wash matters at least as much as how often:

  • A daily rinse with water is fine, and in hot weather or after sweating, sensible.
  • Use soap where odour and infection start: armpits, groin, feet and skin folds. The rest of the body usually needs less.
  • Choose lukewarm water, keep baths or showers short, and prefer mild cleansers.
  • Dry skin folds and the spaces between your toes thoroughly.
  • If your skin feels tight or dry, use less soap, not necessarily fewer baths, and moisturise afterwards.
  • Wash your hands often, whatever you decide about the rest.

Verdict: partly true. You don’t need to scrub your whole body with soap every day. But in a hot, sweaty climate, a daily wash of the right places makes good sense. The elders got the habit right, even if “because we always have” was never the real reason.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Body odour is produced by skin bacteria acting on sweat from specific glands, mainly in the armpits and groin.
  • A single gene variant (ABCC11) strongly reduces underarm odour in people who carry two copies.
  • Harsh soaps and hot water can disturb the skin’s protective barrier.
  • Handwashing with ordinary soap reduces stomach and respiratory infections.
  • Warm, damp, covered skin favours fungal infections such as ringworm.

Still uncertain

  • The ideal bathing frequency for healthy people; it has hardly been tested in trials.
  • Whether results from people with eczema in cool countries apply to hot, humid climates.
  • How washing habits affect the skin’s community of bacteria over years.
