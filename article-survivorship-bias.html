Survivorship Bias: The Success Stories You Never Hear About

We learn from the winners we can see. The people who tried the same thing and quietly disappeared are missing from the lesson.

6 min read·25 October 2026

Walk into any bookshop and you will find shelves of books about people who dropped out, took a risk, or ignored the experts and made a fortune. Scroll through social media and the pattern repeats. These stories feel instructive, and they are vivid and easy to remember. But they share a hidden flaw: they are told only about the people who made it. The rest are not on the shelves, not in the feed, and not in the room when the lessons are drawn. This is survivorship bias, the habit of drawing conclusions from the cases that survived a process while overlooking those that did not.

The bombers that came back

The most famous example comes from the Second World War. Allied bombers were being shot down at a damaging rate, and the military wanted to add armour to protect them. Armour is heavy, so the question was where it would do the most good. Commanders examined the aircraft that returned from missions and mapped the bullet holes. The damage clustered on the wings and around the fuselage, and the obvious response was to reinforce those areas.

The statistician Abraham Wald, working with the Statistical Research Group in the United States, saw the problem differently. The planes he was looking at had all survived. Holes in the wings were not a reason to armour the wings, because a plane hit there could evidently make it home. The aircraft that did not return were the missing data, and they had presumably been hit in other places. Wald argued that armour belonged where the returning planes had few holes, such as the engines, since planes hit there were the ones that failed to come back.

Popular retellings differ on details, and some versions oversimplify what the military actually did. But the core logic is sound, and the lesson has been repeated in aircraft design ever since. The data were real. The error would have been to treat the surviving sample as a picture of all the planes that flew.

Before learning from a success, ask where the failures went, and whether they would look the same if we could see them.

Funds that vanish

The same logic appears in finance, where it has been studied more precisely. Mutual funds that perform badly are often closed or merged into other funds, and they then disappear from the databases researchers use. A study that only looks at the funds still operating at the end of a period is looking at a group that was, by construction, more successful.

In 1992, Stephen Brown, William Goetzmann, Roger Ibbotson and Stephen Ross showed how serious this can be. They demonstrated that if funds disappear according to whether they fall into the poorer part of the distribution, a survivor-only sample can create the appearance of performance persistence: funds that did well last year seem to keep doing well. The effect could be strong enough to account for some of the evidence that past results predict future ones. A later study by the same group, Survival (1995), found that poor performance raised the probability of a fund disappearing, and that the apparent persistence was mostly among funds that lagged the market. The lesson for investors is less comfortable than it sounds. The table of "top performing funds" is a table of survivors, and it is incomplete by design.

The dropout billionaires

Founders make a more intuitive case. Lists of billionaires who left university to build companies are common, and they suggest a route to success. Sendhil Mullainathan, an economist who has written about this, makes the statistical point plainly: there are far more people who dropped out to start companies and failed than people who succeeded, and the failures rarely make the lists. Looking only at the billionaires leaves out the much larger group that did the same thing without the outcome.

Mullainathan also points to a subtler issue. Survivorship bias is not only about ignoring visible failures. It is about forgetting the selection process that produced the data we have. Those who succeeded are not a random sample of dropouts. They are the ones who passed through a filter of luck, timing, resources and connections, and that filter is invisible in the story.

Failure is also harder to study because it is harder to find. A failed company rarely publishes a case study, and its founder rarely gives a talk. That is part of why the bias persists. The visible sample is not just small; it is shaped by what survives to be seen.

A quieter version: the famous who lived

Survivorship effects can also show up in how we interpret who lived longest. A widely discussed 2001 study by Donald Redelmeier and Sanjay Singh compared Academy Award-winning actors and actresses with other actors and reported that the winners lived longer. The finding was striking, but later commentary has pointed out that it is easy to misread. Winning an award requires surviving long enough to have a career, and it is not clear which factors in the comparison were causing what. The method has been discussed as a teaching example of how selection can shape an apparently surprising result.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Analyses that include only surviving units, such as funds or companies, can overstate average performance and create false patterns of persistence.
  • Failures are harder to observe than successes, because they are less often documented, celebrated or studied.
  • The reasoning that protected WWII bombers is a clear case of looking for the data that is missing from a sample.

Still uncertain

  • How much survivorship bias distorts everyday advice, such as the habits of successful founders or entrepreneurs, is hard to measure, because the missing failures are missing.
  • How much of the Oscar-winners finding survives closer scrutiny is still discussed; the study is best read as a warning about selection, not as a settled result.
  • Which practical habits best reduce the bias in everyday decisions has less direct evidence than the bias itself.

Seeing the missing data

The remedy is simple to state and hard to practise. Before you draw a lesson from a success, try to reconstruct the group it came from. How many people tried the same thing? How many of them failed, and how would you find out? Which parts of the story were chosen because they were memorable? Wald’s question was in effect a question about the denominator: what fraction of all the planes had these holes, and what happened to the ones without them?

This is an application of the same discipline that appears in Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine. In both cases, the vivid evidence in front of you has to be weighed against the much larger population it came from. It is also related to When a Measure Becomes a Target, where a number that looks like success can be produced by the way it is chosen and reported.

Survivorship bias will not go away, because most of the world we see has already been filtered. The point is not to stop listening to winners. It is to remember that they are clues rather than the whole picture, and to ask what the sample would look like if the people who vanished were included too.
