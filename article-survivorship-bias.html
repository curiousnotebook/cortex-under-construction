Survivorship Bias: The Success Stories You Never Hear About

We learn from the winners we can see. The people who tried the same thing and quietly disappeared are missing from the lesson, and that gap can turn good reasoning into confident nonsense.

8 min read·25 October 2026

There is a story that turns up in almost every lesson on clear thinking. During the Second World War, military analysts studied bombers returning from missions over Europe. The bullet holes clustered on the wings, the body and the tail. The engines had surprisingly few. The obvious plan was to add armour where the holes were. Then a mathematician called Abraham Wald pointed out the flaw. These were the planes that came back. Planes hit in the engines were missing from the sample because they had not survived. The armour, he said, should go where the holes weren’t.

It is a wonderful story, and its central idea is right. But like many stories about good reasoning, it has itself been polished by retelling. Looking at what actually happened is a small lesson in the same bias it describes.

What Wald actually did

Wald was a member of the Statistical Research Group, a team of mathematicians at Columbia University working on wartime problems. Around 1943 he wrote a series of technical memoranda on a hard question: how can you estimate how vulnerable each part of an aircraft is, when the only planes you can examine are the ones that survived? His key assumption was that hits during combat were spread fairly evenly over a plane. If so, the parts that showed surprisingly little damage on returning planes were the parts where a hit was most likely to bring a plane down.

So the core insight is genuine, and it is clever. The details of the popular version are another matter. When the statisticians Marc Mangel and Francisco Samaniego reviewed Wald’s work in 1984, they found mathematics, not a dramatic meeting. The memoranda estimate probabilities. They do not tell the military what to do. W. Allen Wallis, who ran the group, confirmed that Wald assumed hits were spread evenly, but his own history of the group says little about Wald and aircraft. The famous drawing of a plane covered in red dots, shared millions of times online, is a modern illustration rather than a wartime document. How much Wald’s analysis changed real armour decisions is not well documented.

The story we keep retelling is the version that survived because it is memorable. The drier, more complicated version faded from view.

The idea in one sentence

Survivorship bias is the mistake of drawing conclusions from the cases that made it through some filter, while forgetting the cases the filter removed. It becomes dangerous when the filter is linked to the very thing you want to learn about. If you study successful businesses to learn what makes businesses succeed, the filter (success) is exactly what you are trying to explain.

The cats who fell further

In 1987, two vets in New York, Wayne Whitney and Cheryl Mehlhaff, described 132 cats brought to a city animal hospital after falling from high-rise buildings. They reported something odd: cats that fell from above about seven storeys tended to have less severe injuries than cats that fell from lower floors. One explanation is physical. A cat that falls far enough reaches a steady top speed, relaxes and spreads out like a parachute.

There is another possible explanation. A cat that dies on the pavement is unlikely to be taken to a vet, so it never enters the study. If more of the long falls were fatal, the hospital would see only the lucky survivors of those falls. The data cannot easily tell these two explanations apart, because the cases that would settle the question are exactly the ones that are missing.

The funds you can still see

Investment funds are one of the best-studied examples. A fund that performs badly does not usually announce its failure. It is quietly closed or merged into a more successful fund, and its history drops out of many databases. Look only at the funds that exist today and their average past performance looks better than the experience of the investors who were actually in the market.

In 1996, Edwin Elton, Martin Gruber and Christopher Blake tracked every US mutual fund that existed at the end of 1976, including those that later disappeared, so they could measure how much this filtering flattered the numbers. An earlier paper by Stephen Brown and colleagues showed something subtler: when weaker funds drop out of the data, it can look as if past performance predicts future performance even when it does not.

The pattern is not just a historical curiosity. S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes regular reports that deliberately count the funds that vanished. Its year-end 2025 report on India found that across fund categories, about 27% of funds that existed ten years earlier had been merged or liquidated. Over the same ten years, around 76% of actively managed Indian large-cap equity funds did worse than their benchmark index. The glossy list of “top funds” in a newspaper shows the survivors. The full count tells a very different story.

The success stories in business

The same filter shapes what we believe about building a business. According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, of private-sector business establishments that opened in March 2013, about 80% were still operating a year later, about 51% after five years and only about 35% after ten. The founders we read about come from the small fraction that not only survived but flourished.

This is why stories about famous university dropouts teach so little. We hear about the dropouts who built giant companies, not about the far larger number who left university and had ordinary or difficult lives. When economists Pierre Azoulay, Benjamin Jones and colleagues used US Census data to study founders directly, they found that the average age of founders of the fastest-growing new firms was about 45, not the twenty-something of popular myth.

The organisational researcher Jerker Denrell has argued that learning from survivors does more than exaggerate success. It can reverse the lesson. Risky strategies produce both spectacular winners and spectacular failures. If the failures disappear from view, risk-taking can look like a recipe for success even when, across everyone who tried it, it did no good at all.

Before learning from a winner, ask who else tried the same thing, and where they are now.

Science has survivors too

In 2001, a study in the Annals of Internal Medicine reported that actors and actresses who won an Oscar lived about four years longer than other performers. The idea that success adds years to life made headlines. In 2006, a team at McGill University reanalysed the data and found a hidden survivorship problem. The original analysis treated winners as winners from birth. But to win an Oscar, you first have to live long enough to win one. Those years of guaranteed survival had been credited to winning itself. Epidemiologists call this immortal time bias. Once each actor was counted as a winner only from the day they won, the advantage shrank to around a year and was no longer statistically significant.

Scientific literature as a whole has a similar filter. In 1979, the psychologist Robert Rosenthal described the “file drawer problem”: studies that find nothing are less likely to be published, so they sit in researchers’ drawers. The published record is made of studies that survived the journey to print, and that can make effects look larger and more reliable than they are. Weighing any single finding against everything that was likely beforehand helps here (see Why a Positive Test Might Still Mean You’re Probably Fine).

The everyday versions

Once you know the shape of this mistake, you see it everywhere.

  • “They don’t build them like they used to.” The old buildings, furniture and machines still around today are often the sturdiest of their time. The flimsy ones were thrown away long ago.
  • “I did it and I turned out fine.” Advice from people who took a risk and survived is advice from survivors. Those it harmed are rarely there to give the counter-argument.
  • Testimonials. A wall of happy reviews tells you about the customers who chose to write one, not about the ones who quietly gave up.
  • Our own memories. We remember the hunches that came true and forget the ones that didn’t, so our gut can seem more reliable than it is (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller).

How to spot it

Survivorship bias is hard to see because, by definition, the missing cases leave no trace. A few questions help.

  • What filter did these examples pass through? Being published, being famous, still being in business, being alive to tell the story.
  • Is the filter connected to what I want to know? If the filter is success, the examples cannot tell you what causes success.
  • Who is missing, and can I count them? The most reliable studies start from everyone who began, not from those who finished, as the fund and Oscar reanalyses did.
  • Would I have heard about it if it had failed? If not, silence is not evidence of success.

None of this means survivors have nothing to teach. Wald’s whole method relied on studying the planes that came back. The point is to reason about the missing cases, not to pretend they were never there.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Funds that perform poorly are more likely to close or merge, so data that include only surviving funds overstate typical performance.
  • Most new businesses close within a decade, so well-known success stories are a small and unrepresentative slice.
  • Classifying people by an outcome that happens later in life, without accounting for the time before it, can create survival advantages that are not real.
  • Studies with null results are less likely to be published, which can inflate the apparent strength of published evidence.
  • Wald developed methods for estimating aircraft vulnerability from damage on planes that returned.

Still uncertain

  • How much of the “falling cats” pattern reflects real physics and how much reflects fatal falls that never reached a vet.
  • How far Wald’s analysis actually changed wartime armour decisions; the popular account goes well beyond the documents.
  • How large survivorship effects are in fields where failures are rarely recorded at all, such as careers, the arts or ancient history.
