How We Know What’s Inside the Earth Without Ever Going There

No one has ever seen the Earth’s core, and no drill will ever reach it. Yet we know its size, its layers and roughly how hot it is. The secret is listening to earthquakes.

7 min read·19 November 2026

The deepest hole humans have ever drilled is in the far north of Russia. The Kola Superdeep Borehole reached 12,262 metres in 1989, more than twelve kilometres straight down. Drilling eventually stopped, partly because the rock at the bottom was far hotter than expected. It sounds enormously deep. But the centre of the Earth is about 6,371 kilometres away. Kola got roughly a five-hundredth of the way there, and only about a third of the way through the continental crust in that region.

Every diagram of the Earth’s interior you have seen in a textbook, with its crust, mantle, liquid outer core and solid inner core, describes places no person, machine or camera has ever been. So how do we know any of it?

The first clue: the Earth is too heavy

In 1798, the English scientist Henry Cavendish used a delicate experiment with lead balls hanging on a wire to measure the pull of gravity between objects. From that, he worked out the density of the whole Earth: about 5.45 times that of water. The modern value is about 5.5.

That was a puzzle. Ordinary surface rocks are only about half as dense. If the outside of the Earth is relatively light, the inside must be much heavier, probably packed with dense metal such as iron. It was a powerful clue, but it couldn’t say where that heavy material was, or what shape it took.

Listening instead of looking

The breakthrough came from earthquakes. When an earthquake happens, it sends waves of energy through the whole planet, and sensitive instruments called seismometers can record them on the other side of the world. These waves change speed and direction depending on what they pass through, just as light bends when it passes from air into water. By timing when and where waves arrive, scientists can reconstruct what they must have passed through. It is a little like tapping a watermelon to judge what is inside, or like a medical scan, but using the planet’s own tremors.

Two kinds of wave matter most. P waves (primary waves) push and pull the ground in the direction they travel, like sound. They can pass through solids and liquids. S waves (secondary waves) shake the ground from side to side. They can only travel through solids, because a liquid can’t spring back from being sheared.

Building the map, one boundary at a time

In 1906, the British geologist Richard Dixon Oldham noticed that some earthquake waves arrived late when they had to travel a long way through the Earth, as if they had passed through a central region where they slowed down. It was evidence of a core.

In 1909, the Croatian scientist Andrija Mohorovičić studied an earthquake near Zagreb and found two sets of waves arriving at different times. One had travelled through the upper rocks; the other had dipped down and sped up in a denser layer below. He had found the boundary between the crust and the mantle, now called the Moho. It lies about 5 to 10 kilometres below the ocean floor, and around 35 kilometres below most continents.

In 1913, Beno Gutenberg worked out how deep the core begins: nearly 2,900 kilometres down. Then came a striking observation. On the side of the Earth far from an earthquake, S waves never arrive at all. Since S waves cannot pass through liquid, the simplest explanation was that the core is molten. In 1926, Harold Jeffreys used this and other evidence to argue that the core is liquid. P waves revealed the same thing in a different way: bent sharply as they entered the liquid core, they left a shadow zone, a band roughly 104 to 140 degrees around the globe from the earthquake, where almost no direct P waves were expected.

Much of what we know about the deep Earth comes not from what we can see, but from waves that arrive late, arrive bent, or don’t arrive at all.

Inge Lehmann and the waves that shouldn’t have been there

By the 1930s, the picture seemed settled: a rocky mantle surrounding a liquid iron core. Inge Lehmann, head of the seismology department at Denmark’s Geodetic Institute, was not so sure. She had spent years poring over earthquake records from around the world.

One earthquake caught her attention. In June 1929, a large earthquake struck the Buller region of New Zealand’s South Island. Its waves were recorded at stations as far away as Siberia. Some of those stations sat in the shadow zone, where direct P waves were not expected. Yet faint P waves had arrived there, and they had arrived with noticeable strength.

Lehmann worked out what could explain them. If, deep inside the liquid core, there was a second boundary, a smaller and denser inner region, some P waves would bounce off or bend through it and be redirected into the shadow zone. In 1936 she published her idea in a paper with one of the shortest titles in the history of science: “P′”, pronounced “P prime”. She proposed that the Earth has a solid inner core inside the liquid outer one. She estimated its radius at about 1,400 kilometres. Today’s best figure is about 1,220 kilometres, which makes the inner core roughly the size of Pluto.

Leading seismologists, including Gutenberg, Charles Richter and Jeffreys, accepted her interpretation within a few years. Even so, the inner core’s solidity wasn’t firmly confirmed until 1971, when Adam Dziewoński and Freeman Gilbert analysed the way the whole Earth rings like a bell after very large earthquakes.

Lehmann did all this in a field with very few women. She once wrote to her nephew: “You should know how many incompetent men I had to compete with, in vain.” She kept working long after retiring in 1953, discovered another boundary in the upper mantle that now bears her name, and became the first woman to receive the American Geophysical Union’s William Bowie Medal. She died in 1993, aged 104.

An unexpected push from the Cold War

Seismology took a huge leap forward for a reason that had nothing to do with the Earth’s core. In the late 1950s, the United States wanted a way to detect secret underground nuclear tests before agreeing to a test ban. Earthquakes and nuclear explosions both send waves through the ground, so the US funded a global network of standardised seismometers. Built between 1961 and 1967, the World-Wide Standardized Seismograph Network eventually had about 120 stations. The high-quality data it produced transformed seismology and helped provide evidence for plate tectonics.

What we know now, and what still surprises us

Today, thousands of seismometers record earthquakes around the clock. Together with laboratory experiments that squeeze iron to enormous pressures, they paint a detailed picture. The inner core is thought to be mostly iron and nickel, with some lighter elements. Its temperature is estimated at around 5,700 kelvin (about 5,400 °C), similar to the surface of the Sun. It stays solid despite that heat because the pressure is so immense. The liquid outer core, churning around it, generates the Earth’s magnetic field, which helps protect life from harmful radiation from space.

The inner core still has secrets. Some studies suggest it may have its own “innermost” inner core with different properties. Others suggest it rotates at a slightly different speed from the rest of the planet. A 2023 study proposed that it stopped spinning faster than the surface around 2009 and may be swinging back, in a cycle lasting decades. These are active, debated questions, worked out from the same kind of evidence Lehmann used: tiny differences in when waves arrive.

There is a pleasing symmetry in this journal. Clair Patterson worked out the age of the Earth by studying lead in a meteorite (see The Man Who Dated the Earth). Lehmann worked out its innermost structure by studying faint wiggles on paper records. Neither ever touched what they discovered. Sometimes the best explanation is the one that requires one new hidden thing, as with an unseen planet or an inner core, provided the evidence demands it (see Occam’s Razor).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The Earth has a crust, a rocky mantle, a liquid outer core beginning nearly 2,900 km down, and a solid inner core.
  • The inner core has a radius of about 1,220 km and is made mostly of iron and nickel.
  • S waves do not pass through the liquid outer core, which creates a shadow zone on the far side of the planet.
  • The liquid outer core generates the Earth’s magnetic field.

Still uncertain

  • Exactly how hot the inner core is; estimates differ by several hundred degrees.
  • How old the inner core is; estimates range from about half a billion to more than a billion years.
  • Whether, and how, the inner core rotates relative to the rest of the planet.
  • Whether there is a distinct “innermost” inner core, and what it would tell us about Earth’s history.
