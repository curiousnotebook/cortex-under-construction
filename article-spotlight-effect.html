The Spotlight Effect: Everyone Is Not Watching You

The stain on your shirt, the word you stumbled over, the bad haircut: it feels as if the whole room noticed. Research suggests most people barely did, and that they think about you more kindly than you expect.

7 min read·9 November 2026

In the late 1990s, psychologists at Cornell University asked students to do something mildly humiliating. Each student put on a T-shirt with a large picture of the singer Barry Manilow, who was then considered deeply uncool among American undergraduates. They walked into a room where a small group of other students were already sitting, stayed briefly, and were then led out. Afterwards, they were asked to guess how many people in the room had noticed whose face was on their shirt.

On average, the shirt-wearers guessed that about 46% of the room had noticed. In fact, only about 23% could say who was on the shirt. The wearers had overestimated by a factor of two. Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky called this the spotlight effect: the feeling that a social spotlight follows us around, when it is mostly shining on someone else, or on nothing at all.

Not just embarrassing shirts

The researchers ran the same experiment with shirts people were proud of, showing figures such as Bob Marley or Martin Luther King Jr. Wearers guessed that 48% of observers would notice. Only 8% did. We overestimate attention to our good points as well as our bad ones.

In another study, 193 students held group discussions and then predicted how fellow group members would rank them on things like speech errors, offensive comments and remarkable contributions. They expected their slip-ups and their best moments to stand out more than they actually did. Their peers, it turned out, had been noticing everyone a little, and no one in particular very much.

Why we feel watched

The explanation is not vanity. It is position. You are the one person you can never step away from. You feel the heat in your face, you heard the crack in your voice, you know exactly how long you spent choosing that shirt. Everyone else is busy with their own version of the same experience.

Gilovich and his colleagues suggested that we anchor on our own vivid experience and then fail to adjust enough for how little of it others share. One clue supports this. In a later version of the T-shirt study, students who wore the shirt for 15 minutes before going into the room guessed that 37% of people would notice, compared with 51% for those who walked straight in. As their own awareness of the shirt faded, so did their sense that everyone was looking at it.

Many cultures have a phrase for the fear this produces. In much of India it is “log kya kahenge?”, “what will people say?”. The research suggests a gentle answer: probably less than you think, and they will say it less often.

You are the main character in your own story. In everyone else’s, you are a passer-by.

Others can’t read you as well as you think

A close cousin of the spotlight effect is the illusion of transparency: the belief that our inner feelings show on the outside. In 1998, the same research group found that people telling lies overestimated how easily observers could spot their lies, and that people tasting an unpleasant drink while trying to keep a straight face overestimated how obvious their disgust was.

This illusion matters most when we are nervous. In a 2003 study, Savitsky and Gilovich asked students to give a short speech. Some were simply reassured that nerves are normal. Others were told about the illusion of transparency: that audiences usually cannot see a speaker’s anxiety as clearly as the speaker feels it. The students who learned this felt less anxious, and observers rated their speeches as better.

Others judge us more gently than we expect

Even when people do notice our mistakes, they tend to judge them less harshly than we fear. In 2001, Savitsky, Nicholas Epley and Gilovich asked students to imagine social blunders, such as setting off a library alarm or turning up to a party without a gift. Those imagining themselves as the person who made the blunder expected to be judged far more harshly than those imagining watching it actually judged them. When students struggled with a set of word puzzles in front of others, they expected observers to think much less of their intelligence. Observers’ opinions barely changed.

The same pattern turns up in friendships. In 2018, Erica Boothby and colleagues paired strangers for short conversations and then asked each person how much they liked their partner, and how much they thought their partner liked them. Most people underestimated how much they were liked. The researchers called this the liking gap. It appeared in longer conversations, in real-world settings, among new university roommates over several months, and later in small groups and work teams. When the researchers looked for the cause, people seemed to dwell on their own awkward moments and overlook their partner’s signs of interest.

Teenagers and the imaginary audience

In 1967, the psychologist David Elkind described what he called the imaginary audience: the adolescent sense that everyone is watching and judging, as closely as the teenager is watching themselves. It helps explain why a pimple can feel like a catastrophe at fourteen. Elkind expected this feeling to fade with age. Later studies have been less tidy, with some finding that young adults score much like teenagers on measures of it. The imaginary audience, it seems, is not something we simply grow out of.

But we are watched a little

It would be wrong to conclude that nobody notices anything. In 2016, Boothby and colleagues described a mirror-image bias they called the invisibility cloak illusion. In university dining halls, students believed they were watching strangers far more than strangers were watching them. When pairs of students sat in a waiting room together, the person secretly asked to observe noticed much more about their partner than the partner expected. Even so, the spotlight effect appeared in the same studies: people still overestimated how noticeable a striking T-shirt was.

Put together, the picture is subtle. Others may quietly notice small, ordinary things about us. But the specific things we fixate on, the stain, the stumble, the awkward pause, are much less prominent in their minds than in ours, and they are rarely held against us.

The spotlight is also not equally bright for everyone. Someone who is the only person of their gender, background or appearance in a room, or who is in a public role, may genuinely draw more attention. The research describes typical experiences, not every situation.

How good is the evidence?

The original T-shirt studies were small, with just 15 shirt-wearers in each of the two main experiments. As far as published research shows, no one has yet carried out a large, direct replication of the exact design. That matters, because many famous psychology findings have shrunk or disappeared when repeated (see The Replication Crisis). The broader pattern is more reassuring. Related findings, including the illusion of transparency, the harsh-judgement studies and the liking gap, all point in the same direction, and the liking gap has been found repeatedly in different settings.

Turning the spotlight down

  • Halve the audience in your head. When you feel everyone noticed, remind yourself that people in these studies typically guessed about twice as many observers as actually noticed, or more.
  • Wait it out. The feeling of being watched is strongest right after the moment. It fades as your own attention moves on, and others’ attention has usually moved on already.
  • Remember what you noticed about others. Think of the last time a friend tripped or misspoke. You probably barely remember it, and didn’t think less of them.
  • Know that nerves are less visible than they feel. Simply knowing about the illusion of transparency helped speakers perform better.
  • Assume you were liked a little more than you think. On average, that is what the evidence shows.

For most people, the spotlight effect is an ordinary quirk of perspective. For some, the fear of being watched and judged becomes intense enough to restrict everyday life. That can be a sign of social anxiety, which is common and treatable, and worth discussing with a doctor or counsellor.

Our minds also replay our awkward moments long after everyone else has forgotten them, and memory can make them seem bigger each time (see Your Memory Is a Storyteller). The kindest correction is a simple one: most people are far too busy worrying about their own spotlight to keep track of yours.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • People tend to overestimate how much others notice their appearance and actions, both good and bad.
  • People overestimate how clearly others can read their emotions, such as nervousness.
  • People usually expect harsher judgements of their mistakes than observers actually make.
  • After conversations, people tend to underestimate how much others like them.

Still uncertain

  • How large the spotlight effect really is; the original studies were small and have not been directly replicated at scale.
  • How the effect differs across cultures, ages and online settings.
  • How the spotlight effect and the invisibility cloak illusion fit together in everyday life.
  • Whether simply learning about these biases has lasting benefits beyond a single task.
