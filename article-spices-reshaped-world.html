How Spices Helped Reshape the World

The pepper and chilli in your kitchen once launched fleets, funded the first tradable company shares, provoked a massacre and pulled India into an empire. A short history of how small, fragrant things changed the map.

9 min read·23 December 2026

Open almost any kitchen cupboard in India and you will find a small treasure chest: black pepper, cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, turmeric, cumin, red chilli. They are so ordinary that we buy them in plastic packets and rarely think about them. For much of history, though, some of these spices were among the most valuable goods on Earth. People crossed oceans for them, built companies to trade them, fought wars over them, and in one case wiped out almost an entire island population to control them.

The story of spices is a story of how trade, scarcity and desire can reshape the world. It also happens to run straight through India.

Pepper in a pharaoh’s nose

Black pepper comes from a vine native to south-western India, along the Malabar coast of what is now Kerala. It travelled astonishingly far, astonishingly early. When the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II died in 1213 BCE, the embalmers who prepared his mummy placed black peppercorns in his nostrils. Nobody knows exactly why, but the peppercorns had made a journey of thousands of kilometres, more than three thousand years ago.

Rome’s pepper bill

By the time of the Roman Empire, the pepper trade had become an industry. Greek and Roman sailors learned to use the monsoon winds, which blow towards India for part of the year and back towards Africa for the rest, to cross the Arabian Sea directly. The geographer Strabo reported that as many as 120 ships a year sailed from Egypt to India. Much of the pepper was loaded at Muziris, a port on the Kerala coast. In Rome itself, pepper was stored in specialised warehouses known as the horrea piperataria.

The trade worried some Romans. In the first century CE, the writer Pliny the Elder complained that India drained the empire of fifty million sesterces every year, a sign of how much Roman wealth flowed east in exchange for pepper and other luxuries. A remarkable document survives from this trade: the so-called Muziris papyrus, from the mid-second century CE. One side records a loan to finance a trading voyage to South India; the other records the official valuation of a cargo brought back on a ship called the Hermapollon, used to calculate the customs duty owed to Rome.

Pepper had become so valued that when the Gothic leader Alaric besieged Rome in the early fifth century, his demands for lifting the siege reportedly included thousands of pounds of pepper alongside gold and silver.

Why were spices so precious?

A common explanation says that people in medieval Europe used spices to hide the taste of rotten meat. Historians largely reject this. In his book Out of the East, the Yale historian Paul Freedman points out that spices cost far more than meat, and that those wealthy enough to afford spices could also afford fresh meat. Nobody would pour gold onto a rotting dinner.

Spices were valued for other reasons. They were symbols of wealth and status, the way to show guests that your household could afford the exotic. They were used as medicines, because medieval medicine taught that foods affected the balance of bodily “humours”. And they carried an aura of mystery: Europeans believed they came from somewhere near paradise, since few knew where they actually grew.

Their price was also a product of distance. A sack of pepper might pass through the hands of Indian growers, Arab and other merchants, traders in Egyptian ports such as Alexandria, and Venetian shippers before it reached a kitchen in London or Paris. Each step added a mark-up. Venice grew rich as one of Europe’s main spice importers, sending armed convoys of galleys to bring spices back. According to the historian Jack Turner, a pound of pepper sold by Venetian traders in London could cost roughly a week’s wages for an unskilled labourer.

Spices were not valuable because they hid bad food. They were valuable because they were rare, came from far away, passed through many hands, and signalled wealth, health and a touch of paradise.

Sailing for spices

If you could reach the places where spices grew without paying every middleman along the way, the profits would be enormous. That calculation helped launch the age of European ocean exploration.

In 1492, Christopher Columbus sailed west from Spain hoping to reach the riches of Asia, spices among them. He landed instead in the Caribbean. There he encountered a fiery fruit that he named pimiento, the Spanish word for pepper, even though it was unrelated to black pepper. It was the chilli.

Six years later, the Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama sailed around the southern tip of Africa and reached Calicut, on India’s Malabar coast, in May 1498. According to an account by a member of his crew, when local traders asked the Portuguese what had brought them so far, they answered that they had come in search of Christians and spices. When news of the sea route reached Venice, its merchants feared, correctly, that pepper would now bypass the Mediterranean altogether. Over the following century and more, the centre of Europe’s spice trade moved away from Venice, first to Lisbon and later to Amsterdam and London.

Shares, monopolies and a massacre

In 1600, a group of English merchants founded the East India Company. Two years later, in 1602, the Dutch founded the United East India Company, known by its Dutch initials VOC. The VOC did something new: it raised money by selling shares that ordinary investors could buy and later trade, and shareholders could lose only what they had invested. More than six million guilders were raised. The oldest known record of a VOC share, from 1606, shows that early dividends were sometimes paid not in cash but in spices.

The VOC’s methods could be brutal. Nutmeg and its sister spice mace grew at the time only in the tiny Banda Islands, in what is now eastern Indonesia. Determined to control the entire supply, the Dutch fought the islanders for years. In 1621, forces under the VOC governor-general Jan Pieterszoon Coen conquered the islands. Historians’ estimates vary, but of a population often put at around 15,000, only about a thousand may have remained on the islands afterwards: the rest were killed, died of starvation and disease, were enslaved or fled. The VOC then divided the land into nutmeg plantations worked by enslaved people brought from elsewhere.

One small nutmeg island, Run, had been held by the English. After a war between the two countries, the 1667 Treaty of Breda left Run with the Dutch, while the English kept a Dutch colony they had captured in North America: New Amsterdam, which they renamed New York. To the Dutch, at the time, a nutmeg island seemed the better bargain.

From spices to empire

Squeezed out of the richest spice islands by the Dutch, the English East India Company concentrated on India. Indian pepper was one of its first major earners, but the company soon discovered something even more profitable: Indian cotton cloth, such as calico and muslin. Textiles came to dominate its trade, followed later by Chinese tea, which the company paid for partly with Indian opium.

Over time, a trading company became a ruling power, with its own armies, taxes and territory across the subcontinent. In 1858, the British Crown took over the company’s Indian territories directly. The search for spices had not caused British rule in India on its own, but it was where the road began.

The spice trade helped create the modern joint-stock company and the global ocean economy. It also brought conquest, slavery and colonial rule, much of it carried out by trading companies in pursuit of profit.

The chilli: India’s adopted spice

One of the strangest twists of the story is that India’s most famous “hot” spice is an immigrant. Chillies are native to the Americas. After Columbus, Portuguese traders carried them around the world, including to their base at Goa, where a sixteenth-century botanist recorded chillies under the name “Pernambuco peppers”, after a region of Brazil. India, Africa and much of Asia adopted the newcomer with remarkable speed, while Europe was slower to take to it.

Today it is hard to imagine Indian food without chilli. And the immigrant has become India’s most valuable spice export: in 2023–24, according to the government, chilli exports were worth about 1.5 billion US dollars, more than any other spice.

Spices today

India is now described by the government as the world’s largest producer, consumer and exporter of spices, growing more than 60 of the 109 spices recognised by the International Organization for Standardization. In 2024–25, Indian spice exports were worth about 4.45 billion US dollars, sold to China, the United States, the Gulf and many other markets.

Yet spices are no longer luxuries. As production spread to new regions and trade became easier, prices fell and spices moved from the tables of kings to everyone’s kitchen. Even the VOC learned this lesson the hard way: by flooding the market with spices, it sometimes made its own dividends hard to sell. Scarcity had made spices precious. Abundance made them ordinary.

That may be the deepest lesson of the spice trade. Value is not built into an object. It comes from scarcity, distance, desire and control, and when those change, the world that was built around them can change too (see Where Gods Live for another story of how geography shaped history).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • Black pepper from India was traded over long distances by at least the late Bronze Age, and on a large scale with the Roman Empire.
  • The desire to reach Asian spices directly helped motivate the voyages of Columbus and da Gama.
  • The Dutch East India Company introduced publicly tradable shares, and its quest for a spice monopoly involved the violent conquest of the Banda Islands.
  • Chillies came to India from the Americas after 1498 and are now its leading spice export.

Still uncertain

  • The true scale of Roman trade with India; ancient figures such as Pliny’s may be exaggerated or rhetorical.
  • Exact numbers killed, enslaved or displaced in the Banda Islands; estimates vary widely.
  • Why the Egyptians placed pepper in Ramesses II’s mummy.
  • How much the spice trade, rather than other forces, drove later colonial expansion.
