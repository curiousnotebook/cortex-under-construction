India’s One Clock: Two Hours of Sunlight, One Time Zone

The sun rises over Arunachal Pradesh about two hours before it reaches Kutch, yet the whole country reads the same time. Why India chose one clock, what it costs, and why Assam’s tea gardens kept their own.

7 min read·27 November 2026

On a June morning in Dong, a village in Arunachal Pradesh near India’s eastern edge, the sun comes up at roughly four o’clock. In Ghuar Mota, a village at the far western edge of Gujarat, sunrise that same day comes a little after six. That is about two hours apart, yet both places read exactly the same time on their clocks.

India stretches across roughly 29 degrees of longitude, more than 2,900 kilometres from west to east. The sun takes about four minutes to cross each degree, so the country spans close to two hours of sunlight. But since the middle of the twentieth century, the whole country has run on a single clock: Indian Standard Time, or IST.

How India came to have one clock

Before standard time, every town kept its own time, set by the local sun. When the railways arrived in the 1850s, they needed a common clock to run timetables, and adopted “Madras Time”, based on the longitude of the Madras Observatory. By the 1880s, British India had two official times: Bombay Time and Calcutta Time, which were more than an hour apart.

In 1905, the colonial government chose a central meridian at 82.5 degrees east, a line that passes near Mirzapur in present-day Uttar Pradesh. Because the Earth turns 15 degrees every hour, 82.5 degrees east sits exactly five and a half hours ahead of Greenwich. That is where the unusual “half hour” in IST comes from. Indian Standard Time came into force on 1 January 1906.

Not everyone accepted it. Bombay’s local time was about 39 minutes behind the new standard, and many in the city saw the change as an imposition. The lawyer and civic leader Pherozeshah Mehta opposed it in the Bombay Municipal Corporation, arguing that people had not been consulted. Calcutta held on to its own time until 1948, and Bombay until 1955. Only then did one clock truly govern the country.

The clock has been changed briefly since. During the Second World War, India moved its clocks one hour forward as “War Time”, from 1942 to 1945. Daylight saving was also reportedly used for short periods during the wars of 1962, 1965 and 1971 to save energy.

Time zones are not a fact of nature. They are a human decision about whose sunrise everyone else should follow.

Garden time

In Assam, one group never fully went along. Tea gardens there have long kept their own “chaibagan time” or “bagan time”, about an hour ahead of IST. Accounts date the practice to the British planters of the nineteenth century, who wanted workers in the fields as soon as it was light. Reports often cite the Plantations Labour Act of 1951, which allows governments to set the local time for a particular industrial area. How strictly gardens follow bagan time today is less clear; some reports describe tea work as simply starting early by IST.

The garden clock makes sense when you look at the sky. By my calculation from standard astronomical formulas, Guwahati’s sun rises at around 4:30 am IST in late June, and sets at around 4:35 pm in late December. In Dong, December sunset comes at around 4:10 pm. Offices, schools and banks that open at 10 am lose several hours of morning light, and people need lights on for much of the evening.

The far west has the opposite experience. In December, the sun in Ghuar Mota does not rise until after 7:30 am IST, so children in western India often go to school in the dark.

What a single clock costs

The most striking research on this comes from the economist Maulik Jagnani. He noticed that, because all of India shares one clock, the sun sets later by the clock in the west than in the east. Using national survey data, he found that children in places with later sunsets slept less, by roughly 30 minutes for every hour of later sunset. They went to bed later but still had to wake up at the same time for school.

That lost sleep seemed to matter. In his published study, chronic sleep deficits were associated with fewer years of schooling and lower rates of finishing primary and middle school, with effects larger among poorer families. In a 2019 blog post, Jagnani gave a rough estimate that splitting India into two time zones could bring human capital gains of over US$4.2 billion a year, about 0.2% of GDP. He was careful to call this a “back-of-envelope” calculation, noting that a full cost-benefit analysis would be needed. It is a good reminder that clock time can quietly shape people’s lives (see The Myth of the Perfect Routine, on what happens when schedules clash with body clocks).

Energy is the other argument. The engineers Dilip Ahuja and D.P. SenGupta spent years modelling India’s electricity demand. In a 2012 paper, they concluded that moving the whole country’s clock forward by half an hour, to six hours ahead of Greenwich, would save more energy than either daylight saving or two time zones. They forecast savings of over 2 billion units of electricity a year, mostly by cutting evening peak demand.

Proposals, and refusals

The idea of changing India’s clock has come up again and again. In 2002, a high-level committee of the Department of Science and Technology concluded that separate time zones “may not provide any major advantage” and could complicate schedules for airlines, railways and communication services. Instead, it suggested that eastern states simply start their working day earlier, something states can do on their own.

In January 2014, Assam’s chief minister Tarun Gogoi announced that his state wanted to move to bagan time, an hour ahead of IST. A separate time zone needs the central government’s approval, and it was never granted. In 2017, the Gauhati High Court dismissed a public interest petition asking for a separate time zone for the Northeast, saying the decision belonged to the Government of India. That same year, Arunachal Pradesh’s chief minister Pema Khandu complained that people in his state rose “as early as 4 am”, while offices opened at 10.

In 2018, scientists at CSIR-National Physical Laboratory, the institution that keeps India’s official time, published a detailed proposal in Current Science. They suggested two zones: IST-I, the current time, for most of the country, and IST-II, one hour ahead, for the northeastern states and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. The boundary would run along the West Bengal–Assam border, where they said only two railway stations would need to adjust. The government’s answer, as reported at the end of 2019, was that a high-level committee had recommended against two time zones “for strategic reasons”, without saying publicly what those were.

The case for one clock

The single clock has serious defenders. Two time zones could cause confusion for trains, flights, broadcasting and banking, and, in the worst case, accidents. Some argue that a separate clock could make the Northeast feel more separate from the rest of the country, which is already joined to it only by the narrow Siliguri Corridor. Others argue the opposite: that a clock which ignores the region’s sunlight is itself a sign of being overlooked. The filmmaker Jahnu Barua, who campaigned for a separate time zone for years, has argued that the mismatch contributes to “social alienation”.

India is not alone. China spans five geographical time zones but has used a single official time, Beijing Time, for decades. In Xinjiang, in the far west, an unofficial local time two hours behind Beijing is used alongside it. Large countries like the United States and Russia take the opposite approach, using several zones.

Between “one clock” and “two clocks” sit simpler options that rarely make headlines: moving all of India forward by half an hour, or letting eastern states start offices, schools and courts earlier. These do not require a new time zone, but they also have not been widely adopted.

What it tells us

A clock seems like the most neutral thing in the world. But every time zone is a choice, and it decides whose mornings are bright, whose evenings are dark, and whose children go to bed later. India’s single clock was a decision made for railways, administration and unity. Whether it still fits a country of 1.4 billion people spread across two hours of sunlight is a question that has not gone away.

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • India spans close to two hours of solar time but uses one official time, UTC+5:30, based on the 82.5° E meridian.
  • IST came into force in 1906; Calcutta and Bombay kept their own times until 1948 and 1955.
  • Sunrise and sunset in the Northeast come far earlier by the clock than in western India.
  • Repeated proposals for a second time zone have been examined and not adopted.

Still uncertain

  • How large the real economic and educational costs of the single clock are; current figures are estimates.
  • How much energy a change would actually save in today’s economy.
  • What the “strategic reasons” against two time zones are, since they have not been publicly explained.
  • How widely tea gardens follow bagan time today, and exactly when and how the practice began.
