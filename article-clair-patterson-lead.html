The Man Who Dated the Earth and Found the Poison in Our Air

Clair Patterson set out to answer one of science’s oldest questions: how old is the Earth? The lead that kept ruining his measurements led him somewhere far more urgent.

7 min read·15 November 2026

In the late 1940s, a young American chemist named Clair Patterson had a problem he couldn’t get rid of. He was trying to measure tiny amounts of lead in rock samples, and every measurement came out too high. There was lead in his chemicals, lead on his equipment, lead in the dust of the laboratory and even lead in his own hair. Wherever he looked, there was far more lead than there should have been.

Most scientists would have treated this as a nuisance to be worked around. Patterson treated it as a question. Answering it took him the rest of his life, and it changed the air that billions of people breathe.

A clock made of lead

Patterson’s original goal was to find the age of the Earth. The idea relies on a natural clock. Uranium slowly decays into lead at a known rate, so the mix of different forms of lead in a rock records how much time has passed. The difficulty is that the Earth’s surface has been constantly melted, reshaped and recycled, so its oldest rocks are gone.

Patterson’s solution was to look to space. Meteorites formed at the same time as the rest of the solar system, and many have barely changed since. Using a fragment of the Canyon Diablo meteorite, which made the famous Meteor Crater in Arizona, he finished his analysis in 1953. His result, published in 1956, was that the Earth is about 4.55 billion years old. Until then, the most common estimate had been about 3.3 billion years. Patterson’s figure has stood, with only minor refinement, ever since.

But he could only get there by solving his contamination problem. When he moved to the California Institute of Technology, he built one of the world’s first ultra-clean laboratories: sealed against outside air, with filtered ventilation, acid-washed equipment and chemicals he purified himself. Researchers wore special clothing to avoid bringing dust in. It was there, in clean conditions, that the real size of the problem became clear. The lead wasn’t just in his lab. It was everywhere.

Where was all the lead coming from?

The likeliest answer was in every fuel tank. In 1921, Thomas Midgley, an engineer working for General Motors, had found that adding a compound called tetraethyl lead to petrol stopped engines from “knocking”, a damaging, inefficient form of combustion. It was cheap and effective. It was sold under the name “Ethyl”, which avoided the word “lead” altogether.

The danger was known from the start. In 1924, workers at refineries in New Jersey producing the additive fell seriously ill, with symptoms so alarming that newspapers called it “loony gas”, and five died. In total, 17 workers in the industry died from lead exposure in the 1920s. After a brief pause and a government review, sales resumed. For the next four decades, much of the research on whether leaded petrol was safe was led by Robert Kehoe, the industry’s chief medical adviser. His position was that lead in humans was natural and that there was no proof that ordinary exposure caused harm. The burden, in effect, was on critics to prove danger, not on the industry to prove safety.

For forty years, the question asked was “Can you prove leaded petrol is harmful?” rather than “Can you prove it is safe?”

Reading history in the ocean, the ice and our bones

Patterson set out to measure what “natural” lead levels actually were. He found that the surface of the ocean, which receives fallout from the air, contained much more lead than the deep ocean beneath it. In 1965 he published a paper, “Contaminated and Natural Lead Environments of Man”, arguing that people in industrial countries were carrying far more lead than nature ever intended.

Then he went to the ice. Snow in Greenland falls in yearly layers, preserving a record of the air like pages in a book. Patterson’s team found that lead in Greenland ice had risen at least 200-fold since about 800 BC. It climbed with ancient and industrial smelting, and then rose most sharply after 1940, as leaded petrol spread around the world.

Patterson’s team also compared modern human bones with skeletons from Peru, about 1,600 years old. Modern bones contained 700 to 1,200 times more lead. Even canned food carried a hidden dose: a study he co-authored found that canned tuna contained roughly 10,000 times more lead than fresh fish, much of it from the lead solder used to seal the cans.

The price of saying it

Patterson’s conclusions were deeply unwelcome. The lead and petrol industries were powerful, and he was challenging decades of reassurance. Several research bodies, including the US Public Health Service, refused him contracts. In 1971, he was left off a National Research Council panel on lead in the air, even though he was arguably the leading expert in the world on the subject.

He kept going. Other researchers added evidence of a different kind. In 1979, the paediatrician Herbert Needleman used children’s baby teeth, which store a record of lead exposure, to show that children with higher lead levels scored lower on intelligence tests and were rated by teachers as more distractible and less able to follow directions.

Taking the lead out

Change came slowly, and partly for an unexpected reason. From the 1975 model year, new American cars needed catalytic converters to cut other pollution, and lead ruins them. Unleaded petrol became necessary for engineering reasons as well as health. The US steadily reduced lead in petrol and banned it for road vehicles in 1996. Lead solder in food cans was phased out too.

The effect on people was dramatic. Between the late 1970s and the early 1990s, average blood lead levels in Americans fell by 78%. Among young children, the proportion with levels considered worrying at the time fell from most children to a small minority. The researchers who reported this attributed the fall mainly to removing lead from petrol and from food cans.

The rest of the world followed. India made unleaded petrol mandatory nationwide from 1 February 2000. In 2021, Algeria used up its last stocks, and the United Nations Environment Programme declared that leaded petrol for cars had been eliminated everywhere on Earth. Patterson did not live to see it. He died in 1995.

The poison isn’t gone

Removing lead from petrol was one of the great public health victories of the last century. But lead itself is still with us. A 2020 report by UNICEF and the organisation Pure Earth estimated that about 800 million children worldwide, roughly one in three, have blood lead levels of at least 5 micrograms per decilitre, a level at which harm to the developing brain is a concern. More than 275 million of them were estimated to live in India.

Today’s sources are less visible than a car exhaust. They include informal recycling of lead-acid batteries, lead dust carried home from workplaces, some paints, and certain cosmetics and traditional remedies. The report named sindoor and a traditional tonic powder, ghasard, among products that have been found to contain lead. A 2023 analysis in The Lancet Planetary Health estimated that lead exposure cost children under five around 765 million IQ points worldwide in 2019, and contributed to about 5.5 million adult deaths from heart disease. Like any large modelling study, these figures come with uncertainty, but they suggest the problem remains enormous.

Some researchers have also linked falling lead exposure to the drop in violent crime in many countries since the 1990s. That idea is intriguing but contested, because crime is influenced by many factors at once.

The lesson in plain sight

Patterson’s story is about more than lead. A scientist chasing a measurement problem in a lab noticed that the “background” was wrong, and that the normal state of things wasn’t normal at all. The contamination everyone else had learned to ignore was the discovery.

It is also a story about who has to prove what. For decades, a widely used product was presumed safe until proven harmful. Similar questions arise with new chemicals, medicines and technologies today (see Why the Dose on the Box Was Set for Men). And like the doctors who linked ulcers to a bacterium, Patterson shows how long a correct idea can take to win against comfortable assumptions (see The Bacterium in the Stomach).

What we know, and what we don’t

Well established

  • The Earth is about 4.55 billion years old, as Patterson calculated from meteorite lead in the 1950s.
  • Lead in the environment rose enormously with smelting and, after 1940, with leaded petrol.
  • Removing lead from petrol and food cans was followed by large falls in people’s blood lead levels.
  • Lead harms children’s developing brains, and no safe level of lead in the blood has been identified.
  • Leaded petrol for cars has now been phased out worldwide.

Still uncertain

  • The exact global toll of lead today; large estimates rely on modelling with limited blood-test data in many countries.
  • How much falling lead exposure contributed to declines in crime.
  • Which sources matter most for children in India today, and in which regions.
  • How quickly lead from old petrol, still stored in soils near busy roads, continues to affect people.
